# Frameworks — finding (F105–F114)

## F105 · World blueprint
**Also known as:** Nine-Element Setting Blueprint (Rubin); World Context and World Power System (Emunds)
**Use when:** Building a story world, contemporary or invented, so that place and rules create pressure on the characters and keep the plot from leaning on luck.
**Avoid when:** The world is a throwaway backdrop for a short anecdote; detail that does not press on a character or carry the theme only slows momentum (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Write one sharp line for each of nine categories: landscape or seascape, weather, architecture, economy, government, history (distant and recent), religion and ethnicity, military and police, culture (dress, food, customs) (Rubin).
2. Zoom into specific rooms, streets or gathering spots and run all five senses over each, so the reader feels what living there is like (Rubin).
3. Alternatively sort the rules into four layers: physics, biology, society and relationships, mentality. Note the adversity each layer produces (Emunds).
4. Build a power table: for each kind of power (physical, biological, social, mental, hidden or magical) list what the protagonist can reach and what the antagonist can reach. Add technology, since it multiplies power (Emunds).
5. Check that every law is logical and binds every character the same way (Emunds).
**Common mistakes:** Listing trivia that pressures no one; rules that bend for the hero; a world so generic the characters could be moved elsewhere unchanged.
**Example:** A writer sets a story in a mountain mining town. Thin air makes every task slow (physics), the company owns the only store (economy), and an old strike still divides families (history). The power table shows the foreman controls wages while the heroine controls the only radio. The cold, dim bunkhouse smells of wet wool, and that single room becomes the scene where the two powers collide.
**Sources:** Rubin; Emunds

## F106 · Developing a signature true story
**Also known as:** The Moth-Style Storytelling Process (Bowles); Signature Story Development Process (Forman)
**Use when:** You need one true first-person story about yourself for a stage slot, an interview, an investor pitch, a speech opening, or a way to introduce yourself at work.
**Avoid when:** The piece is a toast or tribute that needs no personal arc, or the tale is fiction or third-person (Bowles).
**Steps:**
1. Mine memory for turning points. Use open prompts such as the first, last, best or worst time of something, and notice where you pause, because pauses often hide emotion (Bowles). Forman adds three priming prompts: a mentor, relative or boss who shaped you; a major challenge with the choices and insights it brought; a book, film or tale that moved you.
2. Pick one episode and state its meaning, its stakes and the arc from who you were at the start to who you became (Bowles).
3. List every sensory detail and every movie-like scene, then keep only the sharpest (Bowles).
4. Choose a structure, or a small framing device, and write a bullet outline. Give it a grabbing opening, a causal chain of emotional pulse points, and an ending that ties the lesson to the listeners' situation (Bowles; Forman).
5. Tell it aloud to a trusted listener or small group. Mark slow spots, confusion, missing feeling and memory gaps; cut to the time limit (Bowles).
6. Prepare versions for different rooms by expanding, trimming or shifting which people and details lead, without inventing facts (Forman).
7. Audit before use: is it too intimate, does it brag, are the facts right? Forman supplies a twelve-question checklist. Public leaders must verify even minor details, because critics seize on small slips.
**Common mistakes:** Memorizing a script; keeping every detail; telling a story that is overly intimate or reads as bragging; skipping the fact check; posting a vulnerable story online where it fixes your public identity.
**Example:** A new school principal needs a story to introduce herself at her first staff meeting. The worst-day prompt brings back her first week as a substitute, when a silent boy finally read aloud. Her outline has four beats: the chaos, the boy at the back, the book he chose, her decision to stay in teaching. Two former colleagues hear it, ask for the smell of the old radiator and catch a wrong year, and she trims a shorter version for parents' night.
**Sources:** Bowles; Forman

## F107 · Insight-led creative brief
**Also known as:** The Creative Process, Creative Brief and James Webb Young's Idea Technique (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Starting a campaign, or auditing concepts that feel weak, so that ideas rest on a verified consumer tension.
**Avoid when:** Guesswork replaces observation of real consumers; teams then talk in internal jargon (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Research: state your questions, read secondary data, then gather primary material (qualitative, quantitative, observation, social listening) and interpret it.
2. Distill an actionable insight: a truth that is new, unexpected and relevant, often a cultural contradiction that unlocks motivation.
3. Write a one-to-two-page creative brief answering eight core questions.
4. Generate ideas with Young's five steps: gather specific and general raw material; combine it and ask why not; incubate by setting it aside; let the idea arrive; refine it against the brief.
5. Rate lasting impact on the effectiveness ladder, from an influential idea up through a behavior breakthrough, a sales spike, a brand builder and a commercial triumph to an enduring icon.
**Common mistakes:** Jumping to executions before the insight; guessing instead of observing consumers; talking in internal jargon.
**Example:** A bike-repair chain watches commuters at rush hour and hears the same complaint: a flat tire ruins the one hour of the day that is theirs. The insight is that riders treat the commute as private time, not transport. The brief targets that feeling, and the team gathers images of dawn rides, sets them aside overnight, and returns with a campaign about protecting your hour.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F108 · Cone of plausible futures
**Also known as:** Cone of Plausibility (Lupton)
**Use when:** Planning strategy, anticipating disruption, or testing the long-term consequences of a present decision.
**Avoid when:** You want a forecast; the cone is a thinking tool and cannot say what will happen (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. Trace the past developments that produced today's conditions.
2. Anchor the narrow tip of the cone in the present state.
3. Project four widening bands: probable (trends continue), plausible (logical extensions), possible (broader realistic range) and preferable (the outcome you want).
4. Throw in wild cards, rare shocks with large consequences, to pressure-test assumptions and spark ideas.
5. Use the futures to inform present decisions.
**Common mistakes:** Treating a scenario as a prediction of what will happen.
**Example:** A regional library board traces thirty years of shrinking book loans. The probable future is a quiet building with few visitors; plausible is a community hub; possible is a tool-lending center; preferable is a neighborhood workshop. A wild card, a free city-wide broadband launch, makes the board postpone a costly shelving purchase.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F109 · Two-uncertainty scenario matrix
**Also known as:** Scenario Matrix (Lupton)
**Use when:** Aligning a team around strategic trade-offs and naming directions that depart from the status quo.
**Avoid when:** More than two forces truly drive the outcome; the grid holds only two variables (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. List the outside and inside forces that could shape the project: demographics, habits, regulation, income, technology.
2. Pick the two most decisive and make them the vertical and horizontal axes, each with two poles.
3. Explore what happens if each quadrant becomes dominant; usually one quadrant is business as usual.
4. Give every quadrant a memorable name and a short story.
**Common mistakes:** Trying to capture more than two variables on one grid; leaving the quadrants without memorable names and stories.
**Example:** A hospital emergency department crosses staffing (stable or scarce) with patient volume (steady or surging). The four quadrants become Quiet Watch, Thin Ice, Full House and Overflow Season. The team writes Overflow Season as a short story of a nurse triaging patients in a hallway at 3 a.m. Hearing it, the board funds an on-call surge roster before winter.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F110 · Design fiction with forced connections
**Also known as:** Design Fiction and Forced Connections (Lupton)
**Use when:** Early ideation, escaping clichés, probing the social or ethical effects of technology, or breaking groupthink in a workshop.
**Avoid when:** You need a shippable, marketable product; this method produces ideas and critique, not production designs (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. Set a speculative frame: deal cards that combine an object, a terrain, a mood and an arc, or pair a familiar product with random attributes.
2. Attach jarring adjectives (disposable, religious, mechanical) or verbs (melt, mend, mock, magnify) to the subject.
3. Sketch or prototype the artifact that results, from utopian to dystopian, and read it backward like an archaeologist to expose the assumptions it reveals.
**Common mistakes:** Expecting a ready-to-ship product from the exercise; treating the sketch as a production design.
**Example:** A team designing a school lunchbox draws it with the verb mend and the adjective borrowed. The sketch is a box that patches its own dents and is lent between siblings. In discussion the group notices it had assumed every lunchbox belongs to one child, and starts exploring shared meal kits.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F111 · Empathic design immersion
**Also known as:** Deana McDonagh's Guide to Empathic Design (Lupton)
**Use when:** Designing for people whose age, ability, culture or work setting differs from yours.
**Avoid when:** Field contact is impossible and you would rely on assumptions; skipping observation keeps designers in an expert mindset (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. Gather background, then meet users in their own setting; observe and interview, noting what they say, do and feel.
2. Collect raw material: photos, surveys, video, transcripts, notes, sketches.
3. Turn the data into design-relevant information by synthesizing findings into insights.
4. Let empathy incubate, then convert inspiration into concrete solutions, treating users as peer experts who co-create rather than passive testers.
**Common mistakes:** Skipping field observation; testing users as passive subjects instead of partners.
**Example:** An architect redesigning a community kitchen for refugee families cooks alongside six families in their temporary flats, photographing how they squat to grind spices and noting who talks while cooking. Back at the studio she sorts the photos and notes into two insights: work surfaces must sit low, and cooking is social. After a week away from the material, she invites two grandmothers to rearrange cardboard mock counters, and their layout, a low island ringed by stools, goes into the plan.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F112 · Research-based audience persona
**Also known as:** Persona + Scenario (Lupton); Tribal Persona Profiling (Butler); Personas (Sandler); Tribal Exclusion Criteria (Butler); Sales-Team Mining (Butler)
**Use when:** Starting a content program, entering a segment, separating buying roles in a B2B deal, or testing design and messaging decisions against concrete users.
**Avoid when:** The persona would be invented from stereotypes; a vague profile with no real pain point fails, and one persona alone risks excluding real users (Lupton; Butler).
**Steps:**
1. Observe and interview, then cluster recurring needs, goals and obstacles, including extreme users (Lupton). Interview salespeople and shadow prospect calls to capture exact questions, objections, hopes and unspoken fears (Butler).
2. Record demographics (age, role, company size, industry, location) and state who the profile excludes (Butler).
3. Record psychographics: fears, pain points, goals, hobbies, tribal language (Butler). Lupton's template adds name, archetype label, backstory, resources, emotions and goal.
4. Document what problem the person is trying to solve and what they already know about your brand and solution (Butler).
5. Write a short scenario showing how the persona reaches the goal (Lupton). Make separate profiles for distinct buying roles without agonizing over exact percentages (Butler).
6. Rank primary, secondary and tertiary personas, with real photos, right after stating the problem (Sandler).
7. Test each piece or design decision against each persona.
**Common mistakes:** Over-analyzing noisy data into false patterns; gendering a universal product; a single persona.
**Example:** A cargo-bike seller profiles a school-run parent and a delivery-fleet manager separately. The parent fears hills with two kids; the manager fears downtime. The profile excludes casual hobby cyclists. A scenario for each shows the parent's tale is a rainy Monday, the manager's a missed delivery window, and the ad copy for each is tested against its own person.
**Sources:** Lupton; Butler; Sandler

## F113 · Audience Layer Profile
**Also known as:** Audience Layer Compositions (Paulsen)
**Use when:** Profiling an audience before drafting, or resolving a clash between the creator's culture and the audience's.
**Avoid when:** Demographics alone seem enough; they ignore friction in the moment of encounter (Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. Core persona: the innermost motivations, values and traits.
2. Audience culture: the norms they share, tacit rules, etiquette and common references.
3. Audience perception: the mental filters and biases that shape how a message is read.
4. Audience context: the spatial, temporal, social and medium constraints at the moment they meet the story.
5. For each layer list traits in two columns, works for you and works against you, and adjust tone, codes or medium where the second column is heavy.
**Common mistakes:** Treating persona demographics as enough; ignoring friction at the moment the audience meets the story.
**Example:** A Spanish payments startup drafts onboarding emails for small shop owners in Japan. The chart puts reliability in the works-for column at the core persona layer, but culture, perception and context weigh against it: owners expect formal address, distrust unknown foreign firms, and read email on a desktop after closing. The team switches to a formal tone, leads with its local bank partner, and sends at 8 p.m. local time.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## F114 · Consumer Narrative Research
**Also known as:** Logic Tree and Presence Studies (Vincent); Coaxing the Narrative (Vincent); Co-Tellership Observation (Vincent)
**Use when:** Scoping research on an existing brand to learn whether consumers already hold a brand narrative before you write or revise one.
**Avoid when:** You need statistically representative numbers; these methods reveal reactions and latent meaning, and focus groups cannot author a brand narrative (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Start the logic tree with the question of whether consumers perceive a brand narrative, and assume no until evidence says otherwise.
2. Run long interviews in McCracken's five stages: note isolated points, analyze them singly and against secondary data, sketch archetypal profiles, test rival hypotheses, extract patterns across interviews.
3. Run narrative inquiry with roughly 500-word consumer essays and paired interviews, observing who leads, corrects or harmonizes the shared story.
4. Coax the narrative: begin with broad questions about peak life memories and peel inward like an onion, mentioning the brand last, so answers are not led.
5. Use ZMET, an eight-step collage method with consumer-chosen photos, to expose unconscious metaphors.
6. Use relaxed 60 to 90 minute one-on-ones to find childhood-rooted cues across five motives: survival, orientation, expectation, adaptation, play.
**Common mistakes:** Asking consumers to write taglines; mentioning the brand in the first question.
**Example:** A regional coffee roaster assumes at first that customers hold no story about it. Researchers ask twelve customers to bring photos that capture how their mornings feel, and in the collage sessions three of them independently choose a lighthouse. Relaxed one-on-ones trace the image to the orientation motive: the café is where they get their bearings before work. That metaphor, not any tagline a customer wrote, becomes the starting point for the narrative.
**Sources:** Vincent
