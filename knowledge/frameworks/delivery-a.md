# Frameworks — delivery (F181–F191)

## F181 · Rehearse by telling, not memorizing
**Also known as:** Two-Half Story Learning Process (Haven); Familiarization Rehearsal Method (Bowles); Steps for Writing Your Story & The Beat-Sheet Method (Leitman); Write the Hidden Theme on Top (Leitman); Stressing Product Words (Leitman); The Dinner Test & Scene-Based Delivery (Dicks); Play the Small Venues (James); Learning a Story by Heart (Maguire)
**Use when:** You must tell a story aloud, for a stage, keynote, toast, meeting or conversation, and want it known by heart without sounding recited.
**Avoid when:** The text is an authored literary tale whose exact words matter (Haven keeps memorization there). Also skip the extended intake steps if the basic ones already let you recall the story.
**Steps:**
1. Reduce the story to scenes: a bullet sheet of about ten lines (Bowles), a sequence of at most seven physical scenes (Dicks), or a hand-drawn storyboard.
2. Memorize verbatim only the opening lines, the closing lines and key refrains or transition openers (Haven; Dicks; Maguire). Drop the written text early.
3. Write the universal theme at the top of your notes for your own eyes and never say it aloud (Leitman).
4. Underline words naming things you love or hate and shift your tone on them when you speak (Leitman).
5. Stand and run a rough draft aloud start to finish, peeking at notes only when stuck. Budget time per section with a timer and aim slightly under the slot (Bowles).
6. Rehearse the second half on its own so the ending is as polished as the opening (Bowles).
7. Tell it to real people, never a mirror: first casually at a meal, then from shrinking note cards to a less invested group, adjusting wherever listeners laugh, cringe or drift, until no notes remain (James; Dicks).
8. Run a fresh-ears listener test, then stop rehearsing hours before the event (Bowles). Maguire stretches this over two to three weeks: silent mental movie every three days, aloud runs in your real posture, and a fast-forward and slow-motion speed drill.
9. Check against the Dinner Test: the story should come across as a leaner, polished form of the way you would tell friends over dinner (Dicks).
**Common mistakes:** Cramming the beat sheet up to the last minute (Leitman); rehearsing in front of a mirror (Dicks); reading from notes as a shortcut, which keeps you reciting (Maguire).
**Example:** A chemistry teacher signs up for a five-minute story slam about the day her first class set off the fire alarm. She draws five quick sketches as a storyboard, learns only her opening and closing lines word for word, and writes fear makes us careful at the top of the page for herself alone. She runs it standing with a timer, then drills the second half separately. She tells it to her carpool on two mornings, cuts a joke that got silence, and stops rehearsing at lunch on the day of the slam.
**Sources:** Haven; Bowles; Leitman; Dicks; James; Maguire

## F182 · Recovering from a blank on stage
**Also known as:** The Great-Amazing-Never-Fail Safety Net (Haven)
**Use when:** You are telling a new or under-rehearsed story and may lose your place, or already have.
**Avoid when:** Asking listeners what happens next works only as an emergency move, unless the piece is planned as a participation story.
**Steps:**
1. Beforehand, frame a new story as a work in progress, telling confident scenes dramatically and summarizing fuzzy bridges conversationally.
2. When memory fails, stop, smile and nod as if savoring a favorite moment, and breathe slowly. This buys five to ten silent seconds.
3. If you skipped something, step back with a calm narrator aside noting what listeners need to know now, with no grimace or apology.
4. Still blank? Tread water: repeat your last line up to three times with rising emphasis, describe sensory details of the current scene, ask listeners what they expect next, or calmly admit you lost the ending.
**Common mistakes:** Apologizing or showing anger at yourself, which breaks the spell.
**Example:** A best man is halfway through a toast about a disastrous camping trip when the next part goes blank. He stops, smiles at the groom as if savoring the memory, and takes a slow breath. Realizing he skipped the leaking tent, he adds calmly that the guests need to know the tent had a hole in the roof. Still unsure, he repeats his last line with more weight and describes the rain hammering the canvas until the punchline returns.
**Sources:** Haven

## F183 · Stage and session preparation
**Also known as:** Stage and Event Preparation Sequence (Haven)
**Use when:** You run any planned storytelling session and want logistics not to sabotage it.
**Steps:**
1. Prepare the room: plain backdrop, seats in rows or a gentle arc with families together, staggered chairs. Sit in the audience seats yourself to check light, sightlines and whisper audibility.
2. Appoint another adult beforehand as house manager for latecomers, restroom questions and disruptions.
3. Use one consistent enchantment cue (a bell, drum, song, special chair or vest) and guide arrivals into your seating plan.
4. Before starting, review only first line, last line and refrains. Walk in purposefully, give a short introduction, and pause one beat.
5. After the last line, hold your spot without apologizing or explaining the moral. Afterwards log how well the audience fit on a planning sheet.
**Common mistakes:** Seating children apart on the floor in front of seated adults; reading toddler squirming as boredom.
**Example:** A hospice volunteer runs a monthly memory evening for residents and their families. She sets chairs in a gentle arc with each family seated together, then sits in the back row to check that a quiet voice carries to residents with hearing aids. A nurse agrees in advance to handle late arrivals and anyone who needs to leave. Switching on one table lamp signals the start; she reviews only her first and last lines, walks in, gives a short introduction and pauses. After the last line she stays still without explaining, and later notes on her sheet which stories held the room.
**Sources:** Haven

## F184 · Levels of audience participation
**Also known as:** Three Situations of Audience Participation (Haven)
**Use when:** You are deciding whether and how listeners should speak or move during a story.
**Avoid when:** The goal is for listeners to remember the story content. Haven's study found children assigned lines recalled saying their lines more than the tale.
**Steps:**
1. Situation 1, seemingly spontaneous: model repeated lines slowly with a nod and fixed gesture, stumble on a famous line so listeners correct the character, or pose rhetorical questions that keep the plot moving.
2. Situation 2, assigned and rehearsed: give audience sections short memorable lines or seated sounds, and rehearse your stop cue as carefully as the start cue. Best for preschool and primary ages.
3. Situation 3, full-body movement: invite young children out of their seats only with a clear crowd-control plan, and sparingly.
**Example:** A ferry captain tells passengers the legend of the harbor's lost lighthouse. Each time he reaches the refrain, and the fog rolled in, he says it slowly with the same sweep of his hand, and by the third time the passengers say it with him. Later he gives the wrong year for the wreck on purpose, and a regular commuter calls out the right one. He keeps the plot moving with questions such as who would sail on a night like that.
**Sources:** Haven

## F185 · Conversation-to-performance spectrum
**Also known as:** The Three Levels of Storytelling (Haven); Conversation–Performance Continuum (James); The Storytelling Spectrum and The Uncanny Valley of Business Storytelling (Callahan)
**Use when:** You are deciding how much planning, length and polish an occasion needs.
**Avoid when:** Do not hold everyday tellers to the stage standard (Haven). Do not import big-plot frameworks such as the hero's journey into oral workplace stories (Callahan).
**Steps:**
1. Place the setting on the line: conversation (spontaneous, short sentences, listeners may interrupt), middle ground (boardrooms and classrooms mixing structure and interaction), or performance (prepared, listeners stay silent) (James).
2. Haven's version: informal tellers, community tellers who retell familiar tales, and professional performers.
3. For workplace stories, stay at the small, conversational end. Heavy craft or a put-on storytelling voice makes listeners feel manipulated (Callahan).
4. Match length, rehearsal and polish to the level you chose.
**Common mistakes:** Delivering a long uninterrupted monologue in a setting that expects turn-taking (James); celebrating rare dramatic company stories instead of many small daily ones (Callahan).
**Example:** An engineering manager on a video standup tells a ninety-second story about the typo that took down the test server last week. She speaks plainly and lets a teammate cut in with how he found it. For the company's annual conference she turns the same incident into a scripted, rehearsed ten-minute talk where the audience stays silent.
**Sources:** Haven; James; Callahan

## F186 · Four types of metaphor
**Also known as:** Four Types of Metaphor (Aristotle)
**Use when:** You want vivid prose or verse through apt comparison without losing clarity.
**Avoid when:** The passage is already dense with figures; too many turn it into a riddle.
**Steps:**
1. Genus to species: use a general word for a specific one (my ship stands here, meaning anchored).
2. Species to genus: use a specific word for a general one (ten thousand deeds for many).
3. Species to species: swap two specific words from the same category (drawing off life for cutting).
4. Analogy: where A is to B as C is to D, put D in place of B or B in place of D, or negate a transferred attribute (evening of life for old age; a wineless cup for a shield).
**Example:** A trail blogger writes that her group drank the view from the ridge, a species-to-species swap of one specific act, drinking, for another, taking in. She then calls the empty lot at the trailhead the stage after the last curtain, an analogy: a lot is to a hike's end as a stage is to a show's end.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## F187 · Guiding the gaze
**Also known as:** The Gaze (Lupton)
**Use when:** Attention order matters in a poster, layout, editorial spread, sign or illustration.
**Avoid when:** Conventions of gaze can exclude viewers unless you deliberately subvert them (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. Plan around reading order (Latin script runs left to right, then downward) and whether viewers are hunting for something or just scanning.
2. Anchor attention with faces, eyes, pointers, contrast and anomalies.
3. Remove low-contrast clutter and generic filler that make viewers look away.
4. Check that the eye travels a beginning-middle-end path.
**Example:** A hospital corridor is stripped of loose notices. A bold orange floor stripe starts at the entrance desk, passes a framed photo of a smiling porter, and ends at the lift doors, where the only sign hangs. Visitors read start, route, destination without stopping.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F188 · Gestalt grouping and pop-out
**Also known as:** Gestalt Principles (Lupton)
**Use when:** You organize maps, diagrams, type, icons or interfaces and need clear wholes and one standout item.
**Avoid when:** Ambiguous figure and ground, or weak orientation contrast, slows recognition when speed matters.
**Steps:**
1. Group with proximity, similarity, common fate, closure, symmetry, continuation, figure and ground, and simplicity.
2. Make one item pop with a change in color, orientation, scale or motion. Color and bold scale register faster than subtle rotation.
**Example:** A pharmacy groups cold remedies on one shelf block with matching blue labels (proximity, similarity). Every shelf tag is plain black on white except one recall notice printed larger in red, which customers spot before they read anything else.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F189 · Affordance
**Also known as:** Affordance (Lupton)
**Use when:** You design controls, navigation, packaging, doors or icons where the possible action must be obvious.
**Avoid when:** Learned habits persist even when inefficient, so do not expect users to drop them.
**Steps:**
1. Match form (buttons, sliders, handles, shadows, highlights) to the intended action.
2. Use familiar controls and clear separation so users can find targets quickly.
3. Avoid conflicting signals, such as unlabeled endless scrolling or deceptive controls, that overload working memory.
**Example:** A booking app styles the confirm button as a raised, high-contrast block and greys out the unavailable dates, so a first-time user taps the right one.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F190 · Memory Encoding Model
**Also known as:** Simplified Memory Type Model (Paulsen)
**Use when:** You design a pivotal aha moment, an installation, or a high-retention educational or brand story.
**Avoid when:** Intense beats are crowded together; without breathing room the audience tires instead of encoding.
**Steps:**
1. Name the memory you target: short-term, long-term, cognitive (the aha), or physical and sensory.
2. Code cognitively in five steps: awareness, knowledge, understanding, application, perspectivation.
3. Code sensorially across sight, sound, smell, taste and touch.
4. Code emotionally by pairing contrasting basic emotions in a balanced rhythm, laugh then tear.
**Example:** A museum room on water scarcity lets visitors carry a full bucket (touch), hear a dry-well echo, learn the daily ration, then see a child's laugh at a new tap, and ends on a quiet bench.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## F191 · Native Social Content Rules
**Also known as:** Six Rules of Outstanding Content (Vaynerchuk); Write longer, substantive insights for B2B feeds (Vaynerchuk)
**Use when:** You create or audit a social post, visual or native ad.
**Avoid when:** Platform features and algorithms shift, so keep testing instead of treating the rules as fixed.
**Steps:**
1. Native: match each platform's look, tone and value expectations rather than recycling assets.
2. Do not interrupt: fit into the entertainment, news or social stream people came for.
3. Rarely demand: give connection, escape or utility before any pitch.
4. Tie into pop culture: current shows, music, holidays and moments your audience cares about.
5. Go micro: publish small pieces often, reacting to real-time events, with active community management.
6. Stay consistent and self-aware: adapt tone and format per network while the core brand identity stays constant.
7. On professional feeds such as LinkedIn, native means longer, substantive analysis, not slang.
**Common mistakes:** Reposting the same asset everywhere; opening with a sales ask.
**Example:** A tire shop writes a 300-word LinkedIn breakdown of why winter tires wear unevenly, in plain professional language rather than a meme. On its short-video channel it runs a weekly series where viewers guess a tire's age from its tread, and a mechanic replies to every comment.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk
