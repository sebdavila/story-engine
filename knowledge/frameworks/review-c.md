# Frameworks — review (F361–F371)

## F361 · Story impact ladder
**Also known as:** Hierarchy of Story Impact (Callahan)
**Use when:** Choosing which stories to retell, or polishing a thin fragment that has potential.
**Steps:**
1. Grade the story on three levels. Basic: it describes what happened. Good: it lets the listener see what happened through visual moments. Great: it lets the listener feel what happened by evoking emotion.
2. For a basic fragment, ask follow-up questions of the person who lived it: what did you see, what did they do, what was said.
3. Re-enact the key moment with the concrete detail and words the teller gave, until the listener sees and then feels it.
**Common mistakes:** Retelling a flat report of results instead of the moment.
**Example:** A nurse reports that a patient who had been on the ward for months finally walked out. Asked what happened, she describes him stopping at the door, putting on his own jacket and saying to the night staff, "Keep my bed warm for the next one." The retelling moves from reporting to feeling.
**Sources:** Callahan

## F362 · Top-down multi-pass revision
**Also known as:** First-Draft-to-Polish Writing Process (Hauge); Three-Stage Editing Process (Butler); Consistent Tense Lock (Hauge); Homophone Search & Irrelevance Cut (Hauge); Revising and Rewriting Process (Apps); Three-Level Systematic Editing Progression (Haven)
**Use when:** You have a rough draft of any written or spoken story and need to turn it into clean, focused prose.
**Avoid when:** Revising before the draft is complete. Young writers skip peer debriefs and use age-appropriate checks (Haven).
**Steps:**
1. Write the whole draft straight through, fast, without fixing mechanics (Apps; Hauge). Hauge starts from a chronological list of everything learned, sorted into his steps.
2. Let it rest: two weeks to a month (Apps), about a week at minimum (Haven), so your mental movie fades.
3. Read it through, marking problems without stopping to edit. Read aloud; Haven also quizzes listeners.
4. Pass one, structure and theme. State the theme in one or two sentences and cut what does not serve it (Apps). Check the order of scenes and map scene intensity, for example scoring action, tension, humor and emotion from one to ten (Haven). Butler calls this developmental editing: arc, clarity, single-idea focus, gaps, tangents cut.
5. Pass two, scenes and opening. Replace summary with scenes and dialogue (Hauge), tune openings, climax and closings (Haven), and rewrite the opening again and again until it hooks (Apps).
6. Pass three, words. Tighten sentences, use active voice and strong verbs, remove deadwood, cut about fifteen percent of words (Haven). Lock one tense, past or present, for the whole story (Hauge). Cut any scene that fails to tie back to the hero's visible outer goal, no matter how moving it is (Hauge).
7. Pass four, proof. Run spelling and grammar and search for confused words such as their, there and they're, its and it's, lose and loose (Hauge). Butler's copyedit and proofread separate line-level from final fresh-eyes checks.
8. Bring in fresh readers near the end: friends, mentors, a trusted writers' group or an email buddy (Apps; Hauge). Butler insists writers never edit their own work, since the brain fills in what it expects. Avoid groups dominated by competing egos, and filter contradictory suggestions (Apps).
**Common mistakes:** Polishing sentences in scenes that will later be cut (Haven). Letting the author be the only editor (Butler).
**Example:** A consultant, Marcus, writes a client success story in one sitting, then waits a week. First pass: he states the theme as "Small changes first beat big rollouts" and deletes two tangents on his own career. Second: he turns "the team worked through the migration" into a night-shift scene with dialogue. Third: he trims adverbs, locks past tense and cuts 15%. A colleague then proofs it.
**Sources:** Hauge; Butler; Apps; Haven

## F363 · Ethics check before telling
**Also known as:** Ethics, Ten Considerations (Sandler); The Manipulation Test and Audience Test (Callahan); Multi-Gender Sensitivity Review (Butler)
**Use when:** Before release of a product narrative, campaign or story, to test whether it respects the audience.
**Steps:**
1. Manipulation test (Callahan): would the listener feel betrayed on learning how the story was used to sway them?
2. Audience test (Callahan): if a real person from the story sat in the audience, would they feel offended or embarrassed?
3. Run Sandler's ten considerations: usability, efficiency, safety, accessibility, transparency, avoiding excessive persuasion, beware of bad actors who could misuse it, understanding the impact on adopters' daily lives, environmental impact, and captivating hearts and minds.
4. If the story uses sexual attraction or innuendo, have both men and women review it before launch (Butler).
5. Rework or drop anything that fails.
**Common mistakes:** Treating urgency cues in a checkout flow as harmless when they amount to coercion.
**Example:** A fitness brand drafts a story about a customer, Joan, whose before-and-after photo is used without her sign-off. The audience test fails: Joan would feel exposed. The team asks her permission and lets her choose the photo.
**Sources:** Sandler; Callahan; Butler

## F364 · Story compass audit
**Also known as:** The Story Compass, Five Compass Points (D'Costa); Iterative Outlining Stage 2 and the Three Tracks (D'Costa)
**Use when:** Auditing a starter outline, outline or rough draft so every plot point, character and scene serves one story, before polishing prose.
**Avoid when:** Looking for fixes to sentence grammar, small continuity slips, plot holes or pacing; those belong to a later stage (D'Costa).
**Steps:**
1. Set Compass Point 1, plot: the one overall drive of the protagonist's goal.
2. Fix Point 2, protagonist: one, or a balanced pair.
3. Fix Point 3, genre: one primary genre, or a proven two-genre hybrid.
4. Fix Point 4, tone: boundaries across five tonal attributes (see F368).
5. Fix Point 5, theme: one viewpoint on life, if the story needs one (see F369).
6. Pick your track: outline track (completed outline), rough-draft track (summarize each scene in one or two sentences to build the starter outline), or mid-draft track (when the urge to overhaul hits halfway; see F365).
7. Test every element of the starter outline against the five points over successive interim outlines until you have one unified intentional outline. Keep what fits; cut or rework what does not.
8. Optionally fix plot holes, structure and pacing, then draft or edit from the outline. Run a second round of the compass on the finished draft to catch scene, diction and dialogue deviations.
**Common mistakes:** Polishing scenes that later get cut for breaking genre or tone. Once fixed, the compass closes off other directions; deviations inside one story harm unity (D'Costa).
**Example:** A short-story writer fixes: protagonist Brother A, genre heist action, theme the emptiness of revenge. A scene where Brother B takes over and one where a ghost turns slapstick both fail the compass and are cut before any line editing.
**Sources:** D'Costa

## F365 · Outline the rival option and compare
**Also known as:** Forced Elaboration (D'Costa); Mid-Draft Track Decision Process, Story Y vs. Story Z (D'Costa); Handwritten Decision Rationale (D'Costa)
**Use when:** You are torn between two plots or two protagonists, or an urge to overhaul a half-written draft arrives.
**Avoid when:** The choice is about genre, tone or theme, or about combining both options; the process assumes an all-or-nothing choice of plot or protagonist (D'Costa).
**Steps:**
1. Forced elaboration (two plots in one outline): separate plot 1 and plot 2 from starter outline A. Build outline B (all plot 1) and C (all plot 2) by brainstorming within each: add an antagonist, expand mini-goals, add a stakes subplot, or swap a midpoint.
2. Stack A, B and C side by side. Outcomes: B or C clearly wins; modified A wins with deliberate handoffs between acts; neither works (revamp or shorten); or both are strong, so pick one, or publish both for independent novels.
3. Mid-draft version: pause drafting and outline only the unwritten remainder with the new plot or character (Story Z), keeping hero indicators aligned (F366).
4. Compare Story Z with the original (Story Y): does the enthusiasm hold up on paper and solve nagging problems, or collapse?
5. For plots decide: reject inner-critic sabotage and finish Y; adopt Z (draft forward, then rewrite the first half); or shelve one gem. For protagonists, ask whether it is sabotage, muse protection (an easier character) or muse growth (a harder one, marked by relief and synchronicity).
6. Handwrite the reasons for your choice and keep the note nearby to re-read in rough patches.
**Common mistakes:** Recycling the same flawed protagonist and premise for two books without changing the flaw and theme (D'Costa).
**Example:** Forty-five percent into a novel, a writer wants the hero's best friend to lead. She outlines the back 55% with the friend in front. It resolves three stuck subplots and creates no holes, so she adopts the switch and writes her reasons on an index card.
**Sources:** D'Costa

## F366 · Protagonist signals
**Also known as:** Hero Indicators, Priming and Follow-up (D'Costa); Single-protagonist vs. Dual-protagonist vs. Ensemble story (D'Costa); Delayed Stakes Placement (D'Costa); Phenotype Swap Test (D'Costa)
**Use when:** Readers latch onto a secondary character, or you are choosing between protagonist candidates.
**Avoid when:** Exceptions exist: opening on the villain in action, mystery or thriller, or on stakes characters for momentum; epics allow more secondary arcs if protagonists share scenes (D'Costa).
**Steps:**
1. Audit six indicators in Act 1 (priming) and Acts 2 and 3 (follow-up): story launch (first scene or point of view); bonding cues (likeable, sympathetic, fascinating); ancillary networks (friends, family, colleagues); scene distribution (count, point of view, dialogue, interior monologue, voiceover, flashbacks); personal agency (getting in and out of trouble); and character arcs.
2. Have priming indicators, follow-up indicators and marketing cues (title, query, cover) all aim at one protagonist, or share equally between two; two leads is the most D'Costa recommends.
3. If misaligned, outline three versions: A alone, B alone, A and B as a pair. Choose on skill, deadline and potential.
4. Delay stakes: first set up the protagonist's launch, bonding cues and opening scenes, and only then linger on the tragic past of the supporting cast.
5. Phenotype swap: try making the protagonist much older or the other gender, and optionally flip the antagonist's gender, to find richer stakes or remove creepiness.
**Common mistakes:** Opening on a sympathetic side character's childhood so the audience adopts them as the hero.
**Example:** A pilot script titled for Captain Wells opens with a deckhand's family drama. Moving the opening to Wells hauling a net in a storm aligns launch, bonding cues and title.
**Sources:** D'Costa

## F367 · Genre promise and delivery
**Also known as:** Genre Fulfillment and Depth-vs.-Breadth Balancing (D'Costa); Single-genre, Two-genre hybrid and Kitchen-sink story (D'Costa); Standalone work vs. Series (D'Costa); Adjective-Stuffed Logline Test (D'Costa); Non-Romantic Subplot Substitution (D'Costa)
**Use when:** You are pitching a story to buyers, checking what Act 1 promises, or choosing which scenes to dramatize or drop.
**Avoid when:** In a series, an installment has to fit the series template, or else be spun off with a new cast (D'Costa).
**Steps:**
1. Audit expectations across the logline, poster or cover, trailer or cover copy, pitch, website, Act 1 and any series template.
2. Commit to one genre or a proven two-genre hybrid; drop kitchen-sink blends of three or more.
3. Stuff explicit genre adjectives and adverbs into your one-sentence logline, disregarding flow, and see which genre it signals.
4. Study a stack of commercial titles in the genre and list five or six conventions: shared elements, signature scenes, and scenes fans love or loathe.
5. Make the climax fulfill genre conventions in an original way.
6. Dramatize unexplored pockets to deepen the genre experience at escalating intervals; cut off-genre filler that adds breadth at the expense of depth.
7. Check each romance subplot, alone and together, for relevance (effect on plot, stakes, climax) and weight (page share). Cut extras or swap in friend, sibling, colleague, parent-child, mentor or boss conflicts.
**Common mistakes:** An extended slapstick opening in a science-fiction action film sets false comedy expectations (D'Costa).
**Example:** A cozy-mystery writer finds her logline reads "a gritty chase thriller" once adjectives are inserted. She cuts a car-chase prologue, adds a tea-shop clue scene early, and rewrites the climax as a quiet reveal over a ledger.
**Sources:** D'Costa

## F368 · Tonal boundaries
**Also known as:** Five Tonal Attributes and Strategic Tonal Management (D'Costa); Mild, Slapstick, and Mobster violence stories (D'Costa); Hyperrealistic vs. Hyporealistic stories (D'Costa); Early Uncharacteristic-Trait Signaling (D'Costa); Hyperrealistic Grounding Respite (D'Costa); Tone-Lightening Substitution (D'Costa); Non-Appreciator Tonal Disclaimer (D'Costa); Entertainment Journal & Genre Fast (D'Costa); Verisimilitude (Sandler)
**Use when:** Setting tonal boundaries in an outline, auditing diction and dialogue in a draft, or packaging a book for its readers.
**Avoid when:** Relying on a disclaimer where covers, settings and hooks signal another tone; it is not fail-safe (D'Costa).
**Steps:**
1. Define five attributes, marking unused ones not applicable: graphicness (onscreen vs. offscreen), humor (farcical, slapstick, lighthearted, sophisticated, juvenile, black, raunchy), violence (mild, slapstick or mobster tiers), verisimilitude (hyperrealistic, baseline 1 to 3, vs. hyporealistic, baseline 4 to 6) and darkness (evil's reach over innocents, protagonist and impunity).
2. Re-evaluate against preconceptions (series template, body of work, subgenre) and audience-pool effects such as PG-13 vs. R.
3. Scrub beats and later dialogue that cross the line. Guard against outside tamperers: your to-be-read pile, Hollywood chefs and the passage of time.
4. Tone-lightening substitution: swap lethal weapons for everyday objects, shorten bloody aftermaths, or warm family banter, keeping structure.
5. In absurd stories add a grounded respite, a brief true-to-life skeptical reaction right after an absurd beat.
6. Signal early: show a mild-mannered character dropping manners soon after introduction so later shock jokes feel earned. Put tone in Act 1 priming (first ten script pages or first one to three chapters).
7. Write a voice-filled book-description line about sex, gore, violence and profanity to deter mismatched buyers.
8. Keep an entertainment journal of what you consume with its tonal attributes, or abstain from same-genre media while outlining.
Sandler's verisimilitude adds internal plausibility and honest answers to dissent.
**Common mistakes:** A knife slashing in a romantic comedy with mild violence; screenwriters cannot control Hollywood chefs in production.
**Example:** A cozy podcast drama has a villain smashing a window with a crowbar. The writer substitutes a garden trowel and cuts the aftermath, and the book description promises "no gore, plenty of tea."
**Sources:** D'Costa; Sandler

## F369 · Theme extraction
**Also known as:** Six-Tool Theme Extraction and Winnowing (D'Costa); Theme-required vs. Theme-optional stories (D'Costa); Theme-Tools Reverse Engineering (D'Costa)
**Use when:** Once plot, protagonist, genre and tone are settled, including when you already have a theme or believe the story needs none.
**Avoid when:** Forcing a theme onto plot and characters before outlining, which risks preachiness (D'Costa).
**Steps:**
1. Decide if the genre needs a theme or arc: usually yes for drama and comedy, optional for high-stakes action or thriller.
2. Extract candidate themes from six areas: the nature of the protagonist's problem; weighty choices at forks and the climax; the key trait separating protagonist from antagonist; sources of conflict with the ancillary network; plot-credibility drivers such as stakes and backstory; and the resolution (gains, losses, unresolved subplots).
3. Include any gems this reveals, such as a sharper antagonist; select one prevailing theme; cut or recast scenes expressing rival themes.
4. Reinforce it by reverse-engineering the six tools: script a midpoint or climax dilemma between two ideals, or mirror the hero's flaw in the antagonist's strategy. Then check draft dialogue in a second round.
**Common mistakes:** Letting several themes compete in the same story.
**Example:** In a comedy about a mother planning a birthday party, arguments with her husband about delegating imply "balance," while a rival parent implies "let go of grudges." She picks time over money, cuts the delegation scenes and makes the rival a stepmom whose own party wins little.
**Sources:** D'Costa

## F370 · Relatability, novelty, tension, ease
**Also known as:** The Four Elements of Great Stories (Snow & Lazauskas); RENT (Lazer)
**Use when:** Structuring or auditing any brand story, video, article, keynote or pitch.
**Avoid when:** Relying on only one element; it captures fleeting attention, while lasting persuasion needs all four (Lazer).
**Steps:**
1. Relatability: anchor the opening in a character, cultural touchstone or struggle the audience recognizes as its own.
2. Novelty: once comfort is established, add an unexpected twist, fresh context or counterintuitive idea. Snow and Lazauskas warn that novelty before relatability confuses.
3. Tension: open a gap between what is and what could be, and keep closing and reopening it until the resolution.
4. Ease (fluency): simple words, low reading level, brisk pace. Lazer's test: a fast, jargon-free hook in the first seconds, often a curiosity gap.
5. Mark which element is missing and fix that one; one missing weakens the rest.
**Common mistakes:** Fast pacing without clarity breaks fluency (Snow & Lazauskas).
**Example:** A cybersecurity video opens with an IT clerk, Noor, awkwardly explaining her job to her aunt at dinner (relatable, easy). The aunt then reveals her own email was hacked last week (novelty), and the question hangs: could Noor fix it before Sunday's family wedding photos upload (tension)?
**Sources:** Snow & Lazauskas; Lazer

## F371 · Cultivating taste
**Also known as:** TASTE (Lazer)
**Use when:** You feel stuck in derivative work, want to train creative judgment, or need discernment to evaluate AI outputs.
**Avoid when:** Studying without producing; taste with no volume of output leads to paralysis and frustration (Lazer).
**Steps:**
1. Take back the wheel: break automatic feed scrolling by choosing non-algorithmic works deliberately each morning and evening.
2. Attune your taste: log your physical and emotional responses to stories and what makes particular lines or scenes excite or repel you, ideally with peers.
3. Study the masters: take apart writers you admire and practice their sentence rhythm, structure and influences.
4. Try and fail: ship many finished stories on fixed deadlines.
5. Embrace the gap: use the persistent distance between what you admire and what you produce as a compass rather than a cause to give up.
**Common mistakes:** Consuming only what the feed serves, so your taste is its taste.
**Example:** A junior copywriter, Ana, picks one radio essay each morning, notes the sentence that made her tense and why, rewrites it in her own subject, and publishes one 300-word piece every Friday. After three months her notes show she favors short sentences before a reveal, and she uses that.
**Sources:** Lazer
