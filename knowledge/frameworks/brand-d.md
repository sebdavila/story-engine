# Frameworks — brand (F321–F331)

## F321 · Value content program
**Also known as:** Three Tiers of Content Relevancy (Butler); Brand Value Story (Butler); Long-Form Value Depth (Butler); Anchor Content Gating (Butler); Micro-Ad Boosting (Butler)
**Use when:** You run blogs, guides, podcasts or lead magnets for a narrow, commodity or "boring" product and need a steady supply of topics that earn attention before any sale.
**Avoid when:** You need a single short promotional piece; this is an ongoing program, not one asset.
**Steps:**
1. Treat the whole program as one serialized storyline, like a TV season, with the customer as the main character; each blog, video or guide is an episode that helps them grow or solve a problem.
2. Brainstorm topics across three tiers: relevant (your product, features, how you work, client wins), semi-relevant (industry trends and adjacent subjects), and nonrelevant (personal or professional interests of your audience unrelated to your industry). The nonrelevant tier must still match a real interest of a defined persona.
3. Write flagship pieces as long as the beginning, middle, end and usefulness require (2,000 to 10,000+ words, or an hour or more of audio or video) instead of obeying an arbitrary word cap.
4. Give most content away ungated. Gate only the highest-utility anchor pieces (how-to guides, templates, inspirational pieces) behind an opt-in form to grow a permission-based list.
5. Put $5 to $100 behind your best long-form pieces on social platforms to pull targeted readers back to your site and warm cold audiences.
**Common mistakes:** Staying purely in the relevant tier, which runs out of audience interest within minutes for most narrow businesses; picking nonrelevant topics that match no persona interest.
**Example:** A small commercial-kitchen equipment dealer sells to first-time restaurant owners. Instead of posting spec sheets, it publishes a 5,000-word guide to surviving the first ninety days of a restaurant (semi-relevant) and a free playbook on managing a young kitchen crew (nonrelevant). Only the staffing-schedule templates sit behind an email form. A $40 boost on the guide brings owners from a local business group to the site.
**Sources:** Butler

## F322 · Essence of a subject
**Also known as:** Essence Model (Sandler)
**Use when:** You must establish the identity of an object, person or brand in exposition, or show how it changes across a story.
**Avoid when:** The description would require stripping so many defining attributes that the subject is no longer recognizable.
**Steps:**
1. Write the observable description: physical traits nobody could dispute.
2. State the intent: what it does or why it is used.
3. State the external appearance: how it makes its user look to other people.
4. State the internal feeling: how it makes its user feel.
**Common mistakes:** Removing too many defining attributes, so the essence loses recognizability.
**Example:** A weathered canvas tool roll: stitched brown canvas with twelve leather pockets (1); it keeps a carpenter's chisels in order on the job site (2); it marks the owner as someone who maintains old tools rather than buying new (3); it gives the owner a quiet sense of being prepared and of carrying his late uncle's trade forward (4). Changing the fourth line shows the roll's meaning shifting when the owner passes it on.
**Sources:** Sandler

## F323 · Mission, vision, value statements
**Also known as:** Mission | Vision | Value Statements (Sandler); Short Organizational Statements (Sandler)
**Use when:** You need to align a team or open a high-level pitch with very little time.
**Avoid when:** The statements would be long or generic; they must stay short and specific.
**Steps:**
1. Pick 3 to 5 prioritized action words that capture what the work is.
2. Write the mission: one or two direct sentences on the feasible, present purpose.
3. Write the vision: an aspirational picture of the future impact.
4. Write the value statement: the quantitative and qualitative benefits plus the core beliefs behind the promise.
5. Validate the three statements with internal and external audiences and cut any verbosity.
**Common mistakes:** Wordy statements; skipping validation with people outside the team.
**Example:** A community bike-repair cooperative writes: mission, we fix commuter bikes within a day at cost for riders who cannot afford a car; vision, a neighbourhood where nobody misses work because of a broken bike; value, repairs priced at parts plus ten minutes of labour, and every customer leaves knowing how to fix one thing themselves. The three lines open their funding pitch to the city.
**Sources:** Sandler

## F324 · Organizational storytelling capability model
**Also known as:** Framework for Organizational Storytelling (Forman)
**Use when:** You design an organization-wide storytelling strategy or diagnose why corporate stories fail to persuade stakeholders.
**Avoid when:** You want a rigid formula; the model guides and audits, and the facts you have limit which stories can be told.
**Steps:**
1. Foundation, authenticity: make stories credible, realistic and tangible; fact-check so words match deeds; start with active listening and open the story to the voices of significant others (employees, customers, communities, unofficial spokespeople).
2. Capabilities, fluency: build three interlocking skills: engaging both emotion and intellect; crafting the story (purpose, audience, timing or kairos, storyteller voice, characters, novelty and the unexpected, five-sense detail and visuals, narrative logic with pulse points, adapted versions); and working with technology (photo, video, blogs, social networks).
3. General objectives: earn or strengthen stakeholder trust first, then use it to inform, persuade and inspire.
4. Specific objectives: aim the trusted stories at concrete goals, mainly articulating and executing strategy, building or changing culture, and strengthening the brand.
**Common mistakes:** Jumping to brand or strategy goals without the authenticity layer; leaving media skills static while digital channels change.
**Example:** A regional hospital group wants to explain a merger. It first interviews nurses, patients and local clinics and verifies every claim against records (layer 1). It trains managers to tell short, specific stories on video and in staff chats (layer 2). The stories rebuild trust in the community (layer 3) and then support the culture shift the merger needs (layer 4).
**Sources:** Forman

## F325 · Brand tagline as a story filter
**Also known as:** Corporate Brand Tagline as Story Frame and Filter, "Human Energy" model (Forman)
**Use when:** You build or refresh a corporate brand campaign, especially in technical, commodity or low-trust sectors, and want internal culture and outside perception aligned.
**Avoid when:** You would use the tagline as a strict test that blocks backward-looking historical or regional stories; follow an 80/20 rule instead.
**Steps:**
1. State the industry's macro problem in lay language.
2. Poll stakeholder perceptions: benchmark competitors and interview internal leaders, industry influencers and the public to find genuine strengths.
3. Distill the company's solution and values into a short tagline that gives diverse stories one unifying moral.
4. Choose story themes where the company is truly strong and has human protagonists; vet every claim with subject experts, strategy, government affairs and legal.
5. Give employee and partner narrators a voice brief that strips jargon and balances a friendly, direct corporate tone with personal voice.
**Common mistakes:** Making the tagline a 100 percent filter that censors legacy stories; using claims nobody verified.
**Example:** A municipal water utility faces public worry about rising bills. Interviews show residents trust its field crews most, so the tagline becomes "Clean water, kept by neighbours." The team films a night-shift pipe repairer and a school-lab technician, each checked by engineers and counsel, and publishes them next to plain fact pages about rates.
**Sources:** Forman

## F326 · Co-created future brand storyline
**Also known as:** North Star Scenario Process for Brand Story Creation (Forman)
**Use when:** A division or company is leaving behind an old commodity identity and needs people to buy into one coherent story of the value ahead.
**Steps:**
1. Gather senior executives (the case had roughly 25) and have each tell personal stories and recall feelings tied to the core domain.
2. Draft a North Star scenario and test it with a wider leadership group (the case used about 150) against strategic questions: target customers, capabilities, risks, timeline. Vote on the candidate storylines.
3. Test the candidates externally through web surveys and in-person interviews with opinion leaders, regulators and buyers across several countries.
4. Build the winning storyline into a few strategic pillars and give each segment toolkits of market trends and proof points.
**Common mistakes:** Choosing the storyline inside headquarters only; skipping the toolkits so segments cannot apply it.
**Example:** A family-owned steel-fastener maker wants to be seen as a building-safety partner, not a bolt supplier. Its twelve managers trade stories about a failed joint they remember. A draft scenario goes to sixty sales and plant leaders for a vote, then to architects and inspectors in three cities. The winning line becomes three pillars, each with a one-page proof kit for sales teams.
**Sources:** Forman

## F327 · Contrarian big idea platform
**Also known as:** Big Idea Five-Step Process (Lazer)
**Use when:** You define a personal platform, book, keynote or company positioning and want a point of view built from the inside out rather than from trending topics.
**Avoid when:** You are unwilling to alienate conventional thinkers; copycat ideas fail even with a catchy tagline.
**Steps:**
1. Passion: name the intersection of industry, role, technology or skill you obsess over.
2. Lens: say what you see that the industry's accepted thinking misses, your contrarian truth.
3. Promise: specify the concrete transformation your audience gains by adopting your lens.
4. Story: craft a signature narrative that proves the lens and delivers the promise.
5. Tagline: once the story holds up, boil the idea down to a brief phrase people can repeat.
**Common mistakes:** Writing the tagline first; adopting a safe, commoditized idea.
**Example:** A veteran school-bus fleet manager obsesses over driver retention. Lens: drivers quit over disrespect, not pay. Promise: a fleet where drivers stay a decade. Story: the morning she let a driver redesign his own route and cut late arrivals in half. Only then does she settle on "Route it with the driver."
**Sources:** Lazer

## F328 · Social to subscribers to sales funnel
**Also known as:** The New Content Funnel (Lazer)
**Use when:** You build a creator platform, executive thought leadership or personal brand and want rented social reach turned into an owned audience.
**Avoid when:** You expect fast sales; the sequence needs 6 to 12 months of social experimentation, and selling before trust stalls conversion.
**Steps:**
1. Social: choose one or two platforms you truly like, study how content works natively there, and publish story-led hooks over 6 to 12 months.
2. Subscribers: tease deeper insight in posts and invite followers to an owned email list (Substack, beehiiv, Ghost).
3. Selling: build trust by delivering steady value, then say openly how the business earns and invite subscribers to purchase, hire you or upgrade.
**Common mistakes:** Relying on a platform audience you cannot export; going straight to selling.
**Example:** A freelance accessibility auditor posts short stories about one broken checkout flow a week on a single professional network. Each post ends with an invitation to the full teardown in her newsletter. After months of free teardowns she mentions her audit package and offers three paid slots to subscribers.
**Sources:** Lazer

## F329 · Owning past failures openly
**Also known as:** The Five Okays (James)
**Use when:** You speak about company heritage, flawed leaders or past corporate failures, where bad news can be found by anyone online.
**Avoid when:** The misconduct is ongoing, or you would falsely paint modern virtues onto the past, such as calling old penny-pinching an environmental choice.
**Steps:**
1. Take ownership of a shameful chapter of the past, provided the organization has admitted it, changed and rebuilt its reputation.
2. Own up to a founder's or leader's misconduct, provided the company has since worked to make amends.
3. Acknowledge bad past product or strategy decisions if the organization learned and recovered.
4. Admit foolish operational blunders, such as underpricing, if they were fixed.
5. Embrace a complete pivot away from the original business if it served customers better.
**Common mistakes:** Concealment; presenting a past flaw as a hidden virtue.
**Example:** A 90-year-old candy maker mentions on its heritage page that the founder once ran a factory with unsafe conditions and that regulators fined it. The page says what changed, names the safety policies and lists where researchers can read the records. Visitors read the flaw as a baseline for how far the company has come.
**Sources:** James

## F330 · Incomplete-pattern viral campaign
**Also known as:** Asymmetric / Viral Storytelling Model (Maxwell)
**Use when:** You market an idea or product with little time and budget and the offering has genuine quality.
**Avoid when:** The product lacks substance; disappointed customers share negative word of mouth 7 to 15 times versus 5 to 10 for positive experiences.
**Steps:**
1. Tie the story to a specific event with a fixed time and place and a deadline running, like a pop-up store or a contest drawing.
2. Confirm the audience already knows enough about your brand to fill the unstated gaps; if not, seed the market first with conventional complete stories.
3. Link the message to a live taboo, controversy or competition.
4. Isolate one trigger element (usually awareness) and repeat it in an incomplete pattern of 5 or 6 encounters so observers must solve the puzzle.
5. Turn the core sentence into a single visual icon and release it within a small community that sees itself as a group (a district, an office, an online forum), where people keep running into each other.
**Common mistakes:** Launching without a deadline; assuming the audience knows the brand when it does not.
**Example:** A ramen shop opening a two-week pop-up in a university district leaves paper noodle-shaped door hangers on dorm handles, each with one clue and a countdown, five days before opening. Students already know the shop from its late-night truck. They argue online about the riddle and queue on opening day.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F331 · Five pillars of a story-friendly culture (TOAST)
**Also known as:** TOAST, Five Characteristics of a Healthy Workplace Culture (Cody)
**Use when:** You diagnose a rigid workplace culture, bridge remote or hybrid silos, or prepare an organization for change.
**Avoid when:** Job insecurity is high; humour improves team performance mainly in low-insecurity conditions and must respect cultural limits.
**Steps:**
1. Trust: create psychological safety by supporting coworkers, allowing safe failure and lightening the mood ahead of tough feedback.
2. Openness: ask for ideas and criticism at every level and involve staff in change instead of planning in a black box.
3. Authenticity: stop bluffing until it works, admit mistakes in public and let people be honest as whole individuals.
4. Storytelling: replace cautious, sanitized copy with vivid, honest, humorous stories fitted to the audience.
5. Teamwork: foster camaraderie and recurring in-jokes so people feel bonded.
**Common mistakes:** Adding humour or storytelling before trust and openness exist.
**Example:** A distributed support team starts a ten-minute Thursday call: one round for updates, one for kudos and a silly poll. A manager opens by sharing her own botched refund email. Within weeks agents post their own mistakes in the channel, and cross-shift teasing replaces silence.
**Sources:** Cody
