# Frameworks — delivery (F192–F202)

## F192 · Narrative Cueing
**Also known as:** Narrative Cueing (Vincent)
**Use when:** An ad, package or visual must carry deep mythic weight in very little space or time.
**Avoid when:** The audience does not share the cultural reference or mythic pattern; the cue then reads as nothing.
**Steps:**
1. Identify an archetype or myth the audience already carries.
2. Choose one visual, verbal or situational clue instead of retelling the plot.
3. Let viewers connect the dots from the cue to the whole myth.
**Example:** An adoption agency's billboard shows a bathroom cup with a second, smaller toothbrush just added beside the first. No copy beyond the logo. Viewers supply the long wait, the paperwork and the first night home themselves.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F193 · Deep Close Narration
**Also known as:** Deep POV (Kamath); Swap pronouns to sharpen close third (Barker & Brown); Cut rampant looking (Barker & Brown); Strip sensory and cognitive filters (Barker & Brown)
**Use when:** You need the audience inside one person's head, for example to explain an unfamiliar user's problem where summaries fail to create empathy.
**Avoid when:** You have not researched the person; guessing a stranger's reality creates visible plot holes (Kamath).
**Steps:**
1. Research the character first: interviews and secondary sources covering relationships, moods and embarrassments (Kamath).
2. Narrate from that one mind only, with no narrator commentary and no hopping into others.
3. Delete filter verbs such as see, hear, feel, notice, think, realize, wonder and seem, and the looked, gazed, glanced lead-ins before actions (Barker & Brown).
4. Order sensory details as the person would notice them, smell before the floor pattern.
5. If third person feels flat, redraft the scene in first person, then convert back (Barker & Brown).
6. Stay in the character's day until the real emotional barrier shows, then bring in the solution (Kamath).
**Common mistakes:** Tagging thoughts with she wondered; jumping to the product before the barrier is felt.
**Example:** A transit planner asking a city council for a night-bus route first interviews six cleaners who finish at 1 a.m. Her scene stays inside Kwame, 24: bleach still on his hands, the station shutter already down, a taxi fare equal to two hours of pay. She cuts every he noticed and he felt. Only when he settles onto the staff-room chairs to sleep again does the 1:20 a.m. bus appear on her next slide.
**Sources:** Kamath; Barker & Brown

## F194 · Word Choice by FURY
**Also known as:** Principle of FURY (Cleland)
**Use when:** You draft or revise descriptions, traits and emotionally loaded scenes.
**Avoid when:** Applied out of order; rich or favorite words then degenerate into overuse or opaque prose.
**Steps:**
1. Familiar: default to everyday words, replacing vague generics like delicious with concrete ones like crunchy and nutty.
2. Unique: use a rarer exact term only when no common synonym carries the precise meaning.
3. Richness: add colorful, non-everyday words sparingly for style and voice.
4. Your favorite: allow a few signature words or pet qualifiers.
**Example:** A chef's memoir swaps a delicious stew for smoky, salty and thick enough to hold a spoon upright. Later, one rare word, unctuous, appears once, where nothing plainer fits.
**Sources:** Cleland

## F195 · What / So what / Now what meeting
**Also known as:** What? / So What? / Now What? Story Meeting Structure (Callahan)
**Use when:** You run an internal management or team meeting without slides and want it to end in decisions.
**Avoid when:** The Now What stage turns into extended storytelling; people need concrete actions then.
**Steps:**
1. What: open with a specific negative story showing the problem on the ground.
2. So what: share a contrasting positive story where a better approach already works, to spark discussion of implications.
3. Now what: move to concrete actions and who owns them; a brief story of the future is optional.
**Example:** A parks crew lead runs a Monday huddle about playground safety. What: on Saturday a mother found broken glass under a slide at 9 a.m., because the first sweep there happens at noon. So what: at the riverside park a retired neighbor walks the grounds at 7 and texts photos, and no one has been hurt there all summer. Now what: the crew sets a 7 a.m. sweep at three playgrounds, names who owns it, and opens a sign-up for neighbor walkers.
**Sources:** Callahan

## F196 · Storytelling habit loop
**Also known as:** The Business Storytelling Habit Loop (Callahan)
**Use when:** You want to build a daily habit of backing points with oral stories in meetings and conversations.
**Avoid when:** You have no repertoire yet; the habit needs stories already collected and up to a year of repetition.
**Steps:**
1. Cue: catch yourself about to state an opinion or abstract claim, such as starting with I think or there are three key points.
2. Routine: relay a specific story from your stock that makes the point real.
3. Reward: watch how the audience reacts emotionally while in the room, or how they retell your story days afterward.
4. Repeat until reaching for a story is automatic.
**Example:** A plant manager feels the urge to say safety matters, instead tells of the night a colleague spotted a loose guard rail, and sees two operators nod and later repeat it at lunch.
**Sources:** Callahan

## F197 · Universal storytelling principles
**Also known as:** The 10 Principles of Storytelling (Buster)
**Use when:** You draft, structure or rehearse any talk, pitch, brand or personal story and want a baseline check.
**Steps:**
1. Tell it as to a friend.
2. Orient listeners up front with place, time, setting and context.
3. Use selective active verbs; cut jargon and verbosity.
4. Collide two contrasting ideas or images to hook listeners and spark a third idea.
5. Find one gleaming detail, an ordinary object or moment that holds the story's essence.
6. Pass the flame: capture what first hooked you and hand it over.
7. Be vulnerable: share the feelings and doubts you really had.
8. Pick the dominant sense of the memory and foreground it.
9. Use yourself: your own experience is an ingredient.
10. Let go: reach the natural emotional peak, then close and leave fast.
**Common mistakes:** Opening with dry generalities instead of a personal moment (Buster's coaching of a talk on citizen diplomacy moved from generalities to a personal juxtaposition).
**Example:** A potter pitching a craft fair starts with the smell of wet clay in her grandmother's shed (place, sense), holds up one chipped cup (detail), admits she nearly quit twice, and ends on the first sale.
**Sources:** Buster

## F198 · Immersive training simulation
**Also known as:** Story Drive Engine / Virtual Immersion Framework (Salmon); Emotional Priming Before Shock (Salmon); Real-Time Clock Synchronization (Salmon)
**Use when:** You train crisis decisions, cultural empathy or trauma reconditioning where technical fidelity alone does not evoke real emotion.
**Avoid when:** Senior strategic training needing nuanced data, since emotional spasms displace it. Hyper-immersive combat stories can also dehumanize participants.
**Steps:**
1. Script a dramatic storyline with character profiles, multisensory cues and a central dilemma.
2. Prime participants emotionally with calibrated music, dialogue and framing before the shock so they suspend disbelief.
3. Let them decide in real time while a director feeds in data, maps and simulated media to steer toward the dilemma.
4. Optionally lock scene length to real elapsed time with a visible clock and parallel editing to sustain urgency.
5. Debrief choices, missed signals, errors and illusions.
**Example:** A hospital trains night nurses on a mass-casualty drill: a scripted family in the waiting room, sirens through speakers, a coordinator who adds a fake news alert at minute eight, and a debrief on who missed the second ambulance call.
**Sources:** Salmon

## F199 · Serialized cliffhanger sequence
**Also known as:** Soap Opera Sequence (Hauge)
**Use when:** You write a multi-day email campaign, or weave one story through a content-heavy talk, and want anticipation before an offer.
**Steps:**
1. Build on the six steps with an empathetic hero pursuing a clear goal against real conflict.
2. End each email, or pause the opening story of a speech, at a crisis, new obstacle or unanswered secret.
3. Open the next installment with a short recap of where the hero left off, then add new actions, obstacles and principles.
4. Introduce the offer gradually as episodes progress.
**Example:** A financial coach sends five emails. The first introduces Marta, a bakery owner who must decide by Friday whether to sign a lease, and ends as her landlord calls early. The second opens with a one-line recap, adds a rival bidder and one budgeting principle, and ends on an unopened letter. The course offer appears in the fourth email as the tool Marta used.
**Sources:** Hauge

## F200 · Staging and setting
**Also known as:** Dramaturgy & Goffman's Sign Vehicles (Sandler); Mise en Scène (Sandler); Checklist for Script Breakdown (Simson)
**Use when:** You plan a live pitch, demo, service journey or film scene where surroundings must communicate what words cannot.
**Avoid when:** Mismatched appearance or manners contradict your brand intent (Sandler). Budgets and clearances may force substitutions in production (Simson).
**Steps:**
1. Research the audience and test, so complexity becomes comprehension (Sandler).
2. Plan setting, appearance and manners: backdrop, attire and posture, gestures and expression (Sandler).
3. For a demo, also plan sets, props, lighting, costumes, who appears, and the composition of all of them (Sandler).
4. For a scene, log order, time, place, key observations, background climate, mood, color and texture, and first design ideas (Simson).
**Example:** A city archive plans an evening walk through its flood records for retirees. A test walk with five residents shows the maps confuse them, so each stop gets one enlarged photo instead. Guides wear plain grey coats, speak seated and keep gestures small; the reading room is lit by one lamp over the 1953 ledger, and the retired clerk who catalogued it sits in. The planner logs each stop's order, time, mood and paper texture before the first public night.
**Sources:** Sandler; Simson

## F201 · Visual framing for story
**Also known as:** Hitchcock's Rules of Visual Storytelling (Sandler); The Conceptual Tools of Cinematography (Simson); Eyeline Intensity Scaling (Simson)
**Use when:** You storyboard video, UI walkthroughs or slide sequences, or plan shots with a cinematographer.
**Avoid when:** Lighting setups so elaborate that they slow shooting and wear out actors (Simson).
**Steps:**
1. Open with an establishing view to orient before introducing characters (Sandler).
2. Treat the camera as the viewer's eyes and size things in the frame by importance, close-up for the crucial detail (Sandler).
3. Choose frame composition, light and color, lens, movement, texture and point of view to carry mood. Wide lenses exaggerate depth, long lenses compress space and isolate faces; move the camera only when story action motivates it (Simson).
4. Plan lenses across the film, as a lens plot that tightens with the story (Simson).
5. Bring an actor's eyeline closer to the lens as tension rises (Simson).
**Example:** A charity cuts a two-minute appeal about a flooded dairy farm. It opens on a drone shot of the drowned valley, then a close-up of the waterline on the barn door, and goes handheld only when the farmer runs to restart the pump. Long lenses isolate her alone in the mud in the middle section, then wide lenses return as neighbors arrive with shovels, so the frame opens as help comes. When she asks the viewer for support, her eyes move from off-frame to nearly into the lens.
**Sources:** Sandler; Simson

## F202 · Seven Secrets of spontaneous storytelling
**Also known as:** The Seven Secrets of Spontaneous Storytelling (Miller); Open with I wonder (Miller); Leave rough edges visible (Miller); Anchor stories in what you love (Miller); Collect daily wow moments (Miller); Breathe into your heart to drop ego (Miller); Gaze at your gestures, not the audience (Miller); Set a minimal, clutter-free story corner (Miller)
**Use when:** You must invent and tell a story on the spot, at home, in a classroom or with a small group.
**Avoid when:** The target is polished literary prose, or teens around 14 and up, who find fairy-tale framing babyish and respond to puzzles and quizzes.
**Steps:**
1. Imagination: treat it as a muscle; accept rough tales and answer unanswerable questions with a curious I wonder.
2. Observation: silently study your surroundings and weave in the five senses. Start from a subject you love if your mind goes blank.
3. Memory: draw on your biography and heritage, told straight or blended with fiction.
4. Props: use found objects or puppets as tactile prompts.
5. Wonder: collect small moments of delight daily, keep a clutter-free nook (a cloth, a candle lit at the start and blown out at the end), and use quiet stillness.
6. Play: remove excess toys and screens so unstructured play drives invention.
7. Confidence: breathe into your heart, treat the story as coming through you, clarify intention, repeat. While rhyming, keep eyes on simple hand gestures rather than the audience.
**Common mistakes:** Polishing before telling; freezing under an inner critic.
**Example:** At a rainy-day club, Ruth admits her tale is rough, picks a snail from the garden, holds it up, says I wonder where it is going, and lets the children decide.
**Sources:** Miller
