# Frameworks — finding (F135–F141)

## F135 · Family and witness interviews
**Also known as:** Three-Phase Interview Method (Apps); Roving Reporter Family Interviews (Maguire)
**Use when:** Writing about people, eras or family events where your memory has gaps, or mining elders for heritage stories.
**Avoid when:** Relatives are in a noisy group meal, or visible notebooks and recorders make them self-conscious. For drawing stories from non-family people, see F118.
**Steps:**
1. Before: Map the relatives (family tree, genogram, Maguire) and prepare clusters of open-ended questions, never yes/no. Check recorder batteries. State topic and time limit up front, about 45 minutes for elderly subjects (Apps) or about an hour (Maguire).
2. Ask permission to record, explaining you want to quote accurately. Warm up with small talk. State date, time and the spelled name on tape.
3. Meet each person alone in a quiet place, never while eating, playing cards or walking (Maguire).
4. Open with a photograph or a familiar family tale.
5. During: Listen through silences without filling them. Let tangents become stories. Probe using the journalist's questions (who, what, where, when, why, how) and request specific examples. Keep your question list out of sight (Maguire) and politely retake control if they start interviewing you.
6. After: Right away note the person's look and the setting. Transcribe promptly, removing filler words and following up on unclear points. Archive the audio to check quotes later.
**Common mistakes:** Taking notes mid-conversation (Maguire); rushing to fill pauses (Apps). If a subject refuses recording, switch to fast handwritten notes. If relatives disagree, use majority agreement for shared facts and respect each person's emotional truth (Apps).
**Example:** Marta wants her grandfather's story of leaving the village. She visits him alone on a porch, opens with a faded wedding photo, and asks what the morning of departure smelled like. She waits through a long silence, and he describes his mother's bread. Afterward she writes down his hands and the creaking chair before transcribing.
**Sources:** Apps; Maguire

## F136 · Numbered research inventory
**Also known as:** Research Inventory System (Apps)
**Use when:** Running any multi-source memoir, family history or book-length project.
**Steps:**
1. Print or create a paper copy of every item: interview transcripts, book notes, encyclopedia facts, vetted web articles.
2. Give each item a sequential number and file it in a three-ring binder or numbered folder.
3. Log number, title and a short description in a searchable computer index, so keywords lead straight to the physical folder.
**Example:** Ines is writing a history of her town's mill. She numbers a council minute 014, a retired foreman's transcript 015 and a flood photo 016, then logs them in a spreadsheet. Months later, searching flood, the index points her to binder two, item 016.
**Sources:** Apps

## F137 · Reading nonverbal cues while listening
**Also known as:** Five-Point Nonverbal Active Listening Checklist (Maxwell)
**Use when:** In a client meeting, negotiation or interview, especially while you are not speaking.
**Avoid when:** Moving straight into high-pressure negotiations untested; practice first in low-stakes meetings. Direct eye contact can read as rude or aggressive in some cultures unless you soften your own gaze first.
**Steps:**
1. Posture: note leaning in or back, slumped or tight shoulders, crossed legs or arms. Briefly mirror it internally to sense the emotion, not to manipulate.
2. Hands: watch how they emphasize points or mask hesitation, and notice when the person looks at their own hands, a sign of inner wrestling or imminent decision.
3. Vocal melody: listen past the words to tone, resonance, timbre and cadence, as to music.
4. Facial micro-tensions: quiet your inner monologue, stop rehearsing your reply, and watch for involuntary tension around eyes, nostrils, mouth and chin.
5. Gaze: soften your own eyes into an open, nonjudgmental look and meet the client's eyes to read excitement, boredom, amusement or receptivity.
**Common mistakes:** Rehearsing your next clever reply while the other person talks (Maxwell).
**Example:** In a budget review, Jonas listens to a department head approve a plan while her shoulders tighten and her voice drops. He softens his gaze, waits, and asks what part worries her. She tells him about the staff cut she expects.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F138 · Four modes of listening for personal stories
**Also known as:** Four Listening Processes (Maguire)
**Use when:** Collecting story material, giving an early draft shape or telling a story live.
**Avoid when:** Treating it as one pre-telling step; it is an ongoing discipline.
**Steps:**
1. Pay close attention to the ordinary stories other people tell.
2. Turn your attention inward to your memories, feelings and experiences you have never examined.
3. Imagine how your audience will hear the tale as you shape it.
4. While telling, listen in real time to how the room responds.
**Example:** Noor wants to tell a story about her late father's toolbox. She first listens to coworkers' small family stories, then sits quietly and asks why the toolbox matters. She imagines listeners wanting to know what was inside, and mid-telling hears a hush, so she slows down.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F139 · Unstructured incubation to surface memories
**Also known as:** Loafing and Inviting Your Soul (Maguire)
**Use when:** You face a blank memory, a block, or an over-insistence on factual accuracy.
**Avoid when:** You need a targeted outline against a deadline; this is open incubation.
**Steps:**
1. Set aside open-ended quiet time at least once a week, opened by a sensory ritual such as lighting a stick of incense that burns about 30 minutes, or a candle.
2. Sit with soft, unfocused eyes in a beginner's mind, holding no topic and chasing no result.
3. Allow images and childhood associations to float up with no censoring or editing.
4. Take brief notes only afterward.
**Example:** Every Sunday evening, Ravi lights a candle on the balcony and sits without a topic. In the third week, the smell of rain brings back his uncle's tin-roof shop. When the candle burns out he writes three lines about the sound of the drumming.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F140 · Memory legwork against outside records
**Also known as:** Memory Legwork and Horizontal Thinking (Maguire)
**Use when:** You recall an emotional flash but lack setting, order or physical detail.
**Avoid when:** Fact-checking turns into documentary perfectionism; the goal is evocative specificity. To prompt memories first, see F139.
**Steps:**
1. Lay your personal lifeline beside family lifelines and public historical timelines.
2. Inspect memory aids: photos, scrapbooks, old journals, year-in-review books.
3. Revisit childhood places and interview witnesses.
4. Go through photographs methodically, looking at composition, faces, body positions and objects in the background.
**Example:** Elena remembers only the fright of a school fire drill. She checks the 1989 timeline, finds the town's earthquake that autumn, revisits the old schoolyard and studies a class photo. She now recalls the cracked wall and the teacher's green coat.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F141 · Story thinking over critical thinking
**Also known as:** Story Thinking vs. Critical Thinking (Simmons)
**Use when:** Bullet points, analysis and demands for measurable outcomes have drained emotion from organizational communication.
**Avoid when:** The system is mechanical and its parts do not think for themselves; root-cause analysis and outcome focus still apply there.
**Steps:**
1. Suspend outcome focus and false clarity. Stop forcing stories to fit a preset bullet, and follow strong emotional currents while tolerating ambiguity.
2. Trust retrospective coherence: follow intuitions, anomalies and meaningful experiences now; the dots connect later.
3. Translate abstractions (acronyms, statistics, labels) into sensory reality from inside a character's body.
4. Just tell what happened: have practitioners recount specific, unvarnished six-minute episodes of how they handled a real dilemma. Switch to critical thinking only afterward, to polish.
**Common mistakes:** Forcing a story to match a predetermined point, which kills it (Simmons).
**Example:** A clinic director wants a lesson on patient respect. Instead of a slide listing values, a nurse tells what happened on Tuesday: an elderly man refused his pills, and she sat on his bed and learned he feared hospitals. The room discusses it before the director edits anything into a policy.
**Sources:** Simmons
