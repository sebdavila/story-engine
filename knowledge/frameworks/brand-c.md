# Frameworks — brand (F310–F320)

## F310 · Walk-through story spaces
**Also known as:** Nonlinear Branding Framework (Vincent); Ron Pompei's 3 Cs and Elemental Spatial Storytelling (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Designing hotels, stores, museums, theme parks, workspaces or online platforms where visitors move freely through the story.
**Avoid when:** The space is treated as a billboard; the effect collapses if staff or neglected rooms break character (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Mark the sacred space off from the profane: signal departure from everyday routine (Vincent).
2. Build a threshold and guardians (architecture, greeters, entry cues) that prepare visitors (Vincent).
3. Make the entry uplifting and expansive so people slow down and look up (Maxwell).
4. Add a warm focal point overhead near the entrance so people wander rather than head to the counter (Maxwell).
5. Use tactile, authentic textures underfoot to ground the visitor as explorer (Maxwell).
6. Layer sight, sound, scent, texture and taste clues (Vincent).
7. Lay out winding paths or self-contained three-act zones with surprises, flowing toward a social hub that orients everything else (Vincent; Maxwell).
8. Add interactive contrasts that spark personal discoveries visitors share, feeding community, culture and commerce (Maxwell).
9. Keep every room in character, including restrooms and back-of-house seams (Vincent).
**Common mistakes:** Cash register as the center; a broken-character washroom.
**Example:** A used-book café opens with a tall green door and a bell (threshold). A reading lamp glows above the entrance, floors are worn oak, and shelves wind toward a big shared table where strangers swap recommendations. Even the restroom wall carries a bookplate.
**Sources:** Vincent; Maxwell

## F311 · Dark Narrative Continuum
**Also known as:** Continuum of Dark Narratives (Vincent); Dark Narrative Categories (Vincent); Hitchcock Hint Rule (Vincent)
**Use when:** Marketing edgy challengers, indulgent luxuries or vice categories that tap shadow instincts.
**Avoid when:** Deception is involved or minors are targeted; it destroys trust and invites regulation (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Place the brand on one of three tiers: provocation (defying norms), indulgence (sanctioning appetite), or destruction (indulgence paired with real harm).
2. Offer a pressure valve: a safe alter ego or temporary escape instead of actual self-harm.
3. Keep motives understandable (McKee) without softening the villain-protagonist falsely.
4. Sanction without deception: honor consent and adult boundaries and state risks plainly.
5. For indulgence, apply the Hitchcock hint: suggest, then stop at the threshold so imagination amplifies desire.
**Common mistakes:** Cartoon-masking a harmful product to reach young people.
**Example:** A dark-chocolate liqueur brand runs an ad of a candlelit door closing just as a glass is lifted, with an age line and a clear strength label. It is indulgence, with the rest left to the imagination.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F312 · Brand Position and Promise
**Also known as:** Brand Story Clarity (Creek); Unique Selling Proposition (Maestas); Run a cross-tier discovery workshop (Maestas); Elastic-Band Overcorrection (Creek)
**Use when:** Setting a new brand narrative, briefing creators, competing in a crowded market, or rebuilding trust after inconsistent posts.
**Avoid when:** The team chases shiny new ideas or varies the promise between platforms (Maestas); one off-lane post before a big launch can sink trust (Creek).
**Steps:**
1. Gather staff from every level in a workshop to surface the traits that truly differ (Maestas).
2. Map the audience's challenges, fears, desires and blockers; fix your position and beliefs beyond money (Creek).
3. Confirm the benefit is real, wanted enough to move people, and one competitors cannot claim (Maestas).
4. Write a four-to-five line belief statement, a short tagline on your advantage, and a mantra stating what you do (Creek).
5. Check every signal (name, vocabulary, platforms, collaborations, props) for clarity, connection and conversion (Creek).
6. Repeat the same core promise on every touchpoint (Maestas).
7. After drift, publish a dense run of hyper-authoritative core-lane content to push off-lane posts down (Creek).
**Common mistakes:** Off-lane posts; changing the wording per channel.
**Example:** A family dental clinic gathers receptionists, hygienists and dentists. They find the shared trait "no lectures." Tagline: "Checkups without the scolding." After a week of off-topic tooth-whitening memes, the clinic runs ten hygienist-led explainers to restore the lane.
**Sources:** Creek; Maestas

## F313 · Rules of Engagement
**Also known as:** Rules of Engagement (Creek); Frontline Story Filtering (Creek)
**Use when:** Filtering ideas, scaling production across staff or agencies, or guiding user-generated content.
**Avoid when:** The list grows past five or turns into jargon; fast filtering stops (Creek).
**Steps:**
1. Gather input from stakeholders (or a mentor circle if solo) on ambition, risk, plan, difference and stand.
2. Cross-examine the list: remove duplicates, resolve contradictions.
3. Distill three to five non-negotiable rules simple enough for a child and short enough for a wall.
4. Repackage the rules per platform, the way one network packages the same news for breakfast and prime time.
5. Train customer-facing staff in the rules so real customer moments return as story leads.
**Common mistakes:** Too many rules; corporate language.
**Example:** A pet-adoption shelter agrees on four rules, including "real animals only" and "no guilt trips." A volunteer pitching a sad-eyes montage is told it breaks rule two. A kennel worker's tale of a dog learning to wag passes.
**Sources:** Creek

## F314 · Brand as a character
**Also known as:** Brand'Hero (Terenzzo); Brand Characters and Five Brand Archetypes (Butler); Twelve Jungian Archetypes (Sandler); Archetypes (Sandler); Anthropomorphism (Sandler)
**Use when:** Launching or repositioning a brand, aligning agencies, or giving daily content a consistent voice.
**Avoid when:** Switching archetypes mid-run (credibility dies), or casting the brand as hero when it should serve the customer; fake heroism or faked wealth draws backlash (Butler). Thin demographic personas sound fake (Terenzzo).
**Steps:**
1. Give positioning, values and customer desires a human shape by asking who the brand would be as a person (Terenzzo).
2. Choose the form: a personified character (mascot, AI voice) or a representative one (founder, spokesperson, long-term customer) (Butler).
3. Commit to one archetype. Butler's five: Mentor (coaches the customer), Ally (advocates through stress), Hero (courage), Herald (announces a new paradigm), Royal (exclusivity and status). Sandler works from Jung's twelve: Citizen, Sovereign, Rebel, Magician, Lover, Jester, Innocent, Explorer, Sage, Creator, Caregiver and Hero.
4. Write twenty traits plus flaws, habits, tastes, speech patterns and fears, then a full biography with home, family, career and origin (Terenzzo).
5. Add at least one short story of the character in action (Terenzzo).
6. Optionally give products human error states, such as a machine apologizing it is unwell (Sandler).
**Common mistakes:** A character with no flaws or backstory.
**Example:** A budget airline invents Gus, a gruff ex-baggage handler afraid of overpacked bags, as an Ally. His sheet lists his fear, his bad puns and his hometown. One short tale shows him quietly refunding a stranded grandmother.
**Sources:** Terenzzo; Butler; Sandler

## F315 · Backward campaign planning
**Also known as:** Backward Planning (Butler)
**Use when:** Designing a funnel or a multi-month content campaign before any writing begins.
**Avoid when:** Leadership, sales and marketing hold unstated conflicting goals, or teams over-plan instead of launching with just enough data and adjusting (Butler).
**Steps:**
1. Align executives, sales and marketing on the outcome and write it, with deadline, at the far right of a board.
2. Repeatedly ask what must happen just before it (webinar, email sequence, lead magnet) until you reach today.
3. Add time, budget and owner to each step.
**Common mistakes:** Delivering a goal (search ranking) a partner never wanted (sales leads).
**Example:** A cycling-tour company agrees with its sales lead: 40 booked tours by 1 June. Working backward gives a spring email series, a route-guide download, a March video and a blog launch next week, each with an owner and a cost.
**Sources:** Butler

## F316 · Organization-wide storytelling program
**Also known as:** The Magic Triangle of Business Storytelling (Callahan); Three Key Factors for Storytelling in Organisational Change (Dolan); The Grapevine Model for Values and Vision (Dolan)
**Use when:** Running a company-wide storytelling, culture-change or values program.
**Avoid when:** Staff are trained before their managers; the managers cannot coach. Leaders also cannot control the informal grapevine, only the stories fueling it, and advocacy drops when people see values ignored (Callahan; Dolan).
**Steps:**
1. Executives co-create and own a strategy story built on real trade-offs (Callahan).
2. Train managers first, then staff, over months with practice and coaching (Callahan).
3. Create routines that collect and broadcast frontline success stories, such as pre-shift huddles or an intranet feature (Callahan).
4. Check the culture tolerates leader vulnerability, train senior leaders and support staff in HR and communications, and have the CEO and executives tell and listen to stories (Dolan).
5. For values, have each leader write a personal story per value, including a failure to live it (Dolan).
6. Gather influencers from all levels in a safe workshop to develop and record value stories (Dolan).
7. Plan the questions colleagues will ask and the stories to share, and reinforce with peer recognition (Dolan).
**Common mistakes:** Posters and mugs instead of peer stories.
**Example:** A hospital laundry service trains its supervisors first. Each morning huddle, the supervisor asks two people for a story about a patient's clean linen mattering. The director shares her own tale of missing a deadline against the value of care.
**Sources:** Callahan; Dolan

## F317 · Personal brand through stories
**Also known as:** SEA Your Brand (Dolan); Personal Brand Story Process (Biesenbach); Personal Brand Story (Biesenbach); Accidental Hero and Third-Party Framing (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** Choosing stories for leadership talks, LinkedIn, networking, interviews, promotions or sales calls.
**Avoid when:** Labels contradict daily conduct (Biesenbach); any one of the three SEA parts is missing (Dolan).
**Steps:**
1. Strategy: decide what you stand for and how you want others to feel about you (Dolan).
2. Audit outside signals: why teams recruit you, review themes, awards; name what you are known for (Biesenbach).
3. Align bio, appearance, project choices (decline off-brand work) and daily speech (Biesenbach).
4. Authenticity: share stories that reflect who you really are (Dolan).
5. Capture one signature win in character, goal, challenge, resolution (Biesenbach).
6. Tell it modestly: stumble into the idea ("none of us knew what to do, then it occurred to me") or quote a colleague's praise (Biesenbach).
7. Evolution: update the story set as ambitions change (Dolan).
**Common mistakes:** Evolving and authentic but not strategic; strategic but not authentic.
**Example:** Priya, a hospital pharmacist, is known for catching dosing mix-ups. Her story: a night-shift nurse asked a casual question about a label, which led her to spot a double entry. She tells it as luck and ends with the nurse's quote.
**Sources:** Dolan; Biesenbach

## F318 · Brand as narrative universe
**Also known as:** Brand Story Shift, Product to Logo to Story (Salmon)
**Use when:** Consumers are cynical about advertising, loyalty is eroding, or the brand faces reputational backlash.
**Avoid when:** It would mask unchanged practices; this risks backlash, and overuse risks consumer burnout (Salmon).
**Steps:**
1. Rename the pieces: brands as stories, campaigns as narrative sequences, consumers as audience, logos as characters.
2. Excavate or invent one narrative: a founding myth, heritage episodes, or a counter-narrative that answers criticism.
3. Spread it through experience touchpoints and human ambassadors who retell it.
**Common mistakes:** A story that outruns real behavior.
**Example:** A century-old umbrella maker had lost young buyers. A journalist weaves ten dated episodes, from a storm-bound ferry to a 1960s film shoot, into a booklet. Staff in boutiques tell one episode per customer, and the repair counter really does repair.
**Sources:** Salmon

## F319 · Brand maturation toward myth
**Also known as:** Three Times of a Brand, Georges Lewi (Salmon)
**Use when:** Mapping a brand's long-term maturation or positioning during social and economic insecurity.
**Avoid when:** The aim is intimate human aspiration; the model pushes it into standardized commercial templates (Salmon).
**Steps:**
1. Time of heroism: the brand wins by bold differentiation.
2. Time of wisdom: it consolidates credibility and earns lasting trust.
3. Time of myth: it acquires a conscience, proves cultural legitimacy and resonates like a classical myth that gives meaning and belonging.
**Common mistakes:** Claiming myth status before credibility is earned.
**Example:** A regional tool-rental cooperative first wins by renting pro-grade machines to hobbyists (heroism), then spends years with honest repair records (wisdom). It now aims to be known as the place neighbors build their first deck, a communal rite.
**Sources:** Salmon

## F320 · Primal brand story formula
**Also known as:** The Primal Storytelling Formula (Butler)
**Use when:** Designing a B2B or B2C content strategy or a 90-day campaign.
**Avoid when:** Bureaucracy blocks origin or vision stories; begin with transformation and value stories. Expect early campaigns to be experiments needing iteration (Butler).
**Steps:**
1. Define the tribe with demographic and psychographic data: interests, fears, pain points; build a sharp persona.
2. Pick two or three primal urges and target emotions by asking how buyers should feel at purchase.
3. Build the story: a central brand character (personified or representative), one of five archetypes (see F314), one of five nonfiction plots, and one or more of four brand story types.
**Common mistakes:** Treating the first campaign as final.
**Example:** A 60-year-old valve manufacturer wants young engineers. Urges: security and significance. Character: a representative veteran engineer as Mentor. Story type: a value story about how rookies' ideas reached a product. Downloads and inquiries are tracked and the next story adjusted.
**Sources:** Butler
