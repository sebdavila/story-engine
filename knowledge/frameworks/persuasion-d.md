# Frameworks — persuasion (F257–F267)

## F257 · Daily message control
**Also known as:** Line of the Day and Spinning the Story, White House Model (Salmon); Controlled-Access Refusal (Salmon); Story-First News Framing (Salmon)
**Use when:** You need to understand or audit a campaign that pushes one pre-tested storyline per day through every channel so reporters cannot set the agenda. Salmon presents it as a political technique, not a neutral craft.
**Avoid when:** Deliberation, honest feedback or trust matters. Constant narrative control cuts leaders off from facts and wears audiences out, so fresh stories must keep replacing old ones.
**Steps:**
1. Route all messaging through one central hub that disciplines every other voice.
2. Pre-test phrasing with polls and focus groups to find short, memorable lines.
3. Issue one line or story of the day and have spokespeople, friendly outlets and staged visuals repeat it identically.
4. Reframe any bad event right away through coordinated briefings until the preferred reading sticks.
5. Decline off-script questions by saying that answering would pull coverage off today's message.
6. Define the day's storyline around the target audience's worldview, then choose and fit facts to it.
**Common mistakes:** Letting each spokesperson improvise; answering the hostile question and losing the day's line.
**Example:** A city mayor's office picks one line each morning: "Every bus on time by June." The press aide sends it to three councillors and a friendly local blog, and a ribbon-cutting at a depot supplies the picture. When a bus breaks down on live TV, a briefing within the hour recasts it as proof the new repair crew works.
**Sources:** Salmon

## F258 · Moral melodrama campaign
**Also known as:** The Scheherazade Strategy, Karl Rove (Salmon)
**Use when:** You are analysing how a candidate with a weak record steers attention away from facts by playing on cultural myths and fears. Salmon documents it as manipulation.
**Avoid when:** Accountability is the goal. It replaces factual debate with a faith-based plot and collapses when a real crisis overwhelms the story.
**Steps:**
1. Anchor the leader's persona in a personal story of suffering and redemption that makes the person likable.
2. Drop policy lists and build a good-versus-evil plot of sympathetic everyday heroes against threatening villains.
3. Keep inventing or escalating moral storylines that mix personal trauma, national legend and fear, so each vote reads as a symbolic act.
**Common mistakes:** Leaning on the plot with no fallback when events contradict it.
**Example:** A county sheriff candidate with a poor clearance rate tells of his brother's overdose and recovery, then casts the race as neighbours versus a faceless drug ring. Each week brings a new chapter, a new victim's family, a new rally. Questions about response times never get a slot.
**Sources:** Salmon

## F259 · Success story without bragging
**Also known as:** Autobiographical Non-Boasting Framework (Hauge); Autobiographical Success Story (Hauge); The Two Ways of Telling a Hero Story (Dicks)
**Use when:** You are the hero of the story and need trust, for example selling a high-priced offer, a job interview or a stage talk.
**Avoid when:** The story is about helping a client succeed: the client is the hero there. Use your own story sparingly beside client stories, since overuse breeds sameness.
**Steps:**
1. Open with real pain, fears, shortcomings or failures so listeners see you as ordinary.
2. Admit a real flaw or embarrassing mistake early so people root for an underdog.
3. Include doubts, obstacles and moments you wanted to quit.
4. Credit mentors, loved ones, advice and the method you promote.
5. If you solved something alone, place the breakthrough at a moment of near-defeat rather than claiming brilliance.
6. Shrink the win: tell one small step, credit teammates or luck, or set the win beside the failures around it.
7. Reframe a big outward feat around the inner struggle anyone can relate to.
8. Boast only about effort, such as years of study or costly training.
**Common mistakes:** Telling only the triumph; skipping the flaw; casting yourself as the hero of a client's win.
**Example:** A bakery owner pitching a franchise investor starts with the week her first oven fire emptied the shop and she nearly quit. She credits her aunt's recipe and a night-shift baker who stayed on. The win is told as a single Saturday when forty loaves sold out.
**Sources:** Hauge; Dicks

## F260 · Deep motivational needs
**Also known as:** Nine Primal Urges (Butler); Maslow's Hierarchy of Need (Sandler)
**Use when:** Choosing the drive a campaign or design story should anchor in, so it reaches people below conscious evaluation.
**Avoid when:** The urge would be pushed crudely: overused fear, empty curiosity clickbait, tone-deaf sex appeal or sectarian dogma cause backlash and lose trust.
**Steps:**
1. List the nine urges: food, shelter, clothing, safety, protection of helpless others, sex, curiosity, significance, spirituality.
2. Pick two or three that fit your audience and weave them through the campaign.
3. Show the urge being met (appetizing food rather than deprivation, for example) so you do not trigger anxiety.
4. For product or design stories, map features up five levels: basic function, reliable safety, social belonging, esteem and empowerment, then personal growth.
5. Treat the lower four levels as deficiency needs that hurt when neglected; only the top level is a growth need.
**Common mistakes:** Using one urge, usually fear, for everything.
**Example:** A smart-lock startup anchors its video in protection: a father checks his phone from a train and sees his daughter arrive home. The lock's basic function, reliability and the family circle form the rungs behind the scene.
**Sources:** Butler; Sandler

## F261 · Emotion as decision input
**Also known as:** Cornerstone and Gradient Emotions (Butler); Affect as Information Theory (Sandler)
**Use when:** Buyers lack time or motivation to weigh facts and will decide on feeling; headlines and narratives would land flat as plain claims.
**Avoid when:** The plan rests on extreme fear: audiences tune out and grow skeptical when dire predictions fail.
**Steps:**
1. Remember buyers feel blends of emotion, not one pure state.
2. Choose one or more cornerstone emotions: curiosity, fear, envy, lust, love, excitement, nostalgia, surprise.
3. Replace dry claims with scenes and sensory cues that evoke the chosen blend.
4. Cut jargon that overloads thinking capacity.
5. Make it personal by showing authentic emotion.
6. Test on several audiences, since emotional response varies.
**Common mistakes:** Naming the feeling instead of staging it; testing on one group only.
**Example:** A moving company stops claiming "careful handling." Its ad follows a retired teacher watching movers wrap her late husband's desk in a quilt, nostalgia mixed with relief. Two panels of customers confirm the blend before launch.
**Sources:** Butler; Sandler

## F262 · Jobs to be done framing
**Also known as:** Jobs to Be Done, JTBD (Sandler)
**Use when:** Writing a short value proposition or humanizing an innovation story around the progress a person is making instead of specs.
**Avoid when:** You have no research. The job must be validated with users, not guessed.
**Steps:**
1. State the impulse: "I need to..."
2. Add the context: "When/where..."
3. State the outcome: "So that..."
4. Name the friction removed: "Without..."; add primary and secondary tasks and the expected emotional response.
5. Validate with user research.
**Common mistakes:** Describing the product rather than the progress.
**Example:** A meal-kit service writes: I need dinner on the table, when I get home at 7 with two hungry kids, so that we eat together, without shopping. Interviews with ten parents show the real job is a calm hour, not recipes.
**Sources:** Sandler

## F263 · Audience interpretation models
**Also known as:** Sensemaking, Seven Properties (Sandler); Stimulus, Organism, Response Theory (Sandler)
**Use when:** Designing complex information or choosing colors, props, metaphors that might trigger unintended reactions.
**Avoid when:** You expect to predict every individual trigger; you cannot. Add explicit descriptors to guide conscious judgment.
**Steps:**
1. Expect people to anchor on selected cues, read the present against the past, favor what feels plausible over what is accurate, and be shaped by social expectations and ongoing updates.
2. Give clear context cues early so new data attaches to existing mental maps.
3. Treat the story or visual as the stimulus, the audience's past, fears and biases as the organism, and their inner or outward reaction as the response.
4. Name the intended reading explicitly so a vague cue does not invite a flawed one.
**Common mistakes:** Leaving cues vague so audiences invent their own explanation.
**Example:** A clinic redesigns a waiting-room poster about flu shots. The staff notice that a needle image may stir childhood fear, so they add a caption saying the shot takes seconds and swap the needle for a smiling nurse.
**Sources:** Sandler

## F264 · Cultural adaptation
**Also known as:** Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions Theory (Sandler); Local-Narrative Code Adaptation (Salmon)
**Use when:** Localizing a story, product or pitch for another culture or hostile market.
**Steps:**
1. Check the audience on six dimensions: power distance, individualism versus collectivism, competitive versus cooperative values, uncertainty avoidance, long versus short-term orientation, indulgence versus restraint.
2. Rewrite emphasis to fit, for example group consensus and long-term partnership instead of individual disruption and quick wins.
3. Weave in the local cultural codes rather than exporting the home-country narrative unchanged.
**Common mistakes:** Shipping one version everywhere.
**Example:** A Canadian payroll app tells a founder-hero story at home. For a market that values hierarchy and consensus, the Lisbon version features an accounting firm's partners deciding together, with a three-year support plan.
**Sources:** Sandler; Salmon

## F265 · Narrative transportation
**Also known as:** The Three Elements of a Bridge (Hall); Narrative Transportation (Sandler); Narrative Transportation and Retrospective Reflection (Gottschall)
**Use when:** Direct facts trigger skepticism and you want beliefs or actions to shift, or sales and leadership messages are failing.
**Avoid when:** The craft is weak or the preaching is open; either breaks the effect.
**Steps:**
1. Captivate: pull listeners into the story's world with concrete sensory scenes and character struggle so their inner chatter quiets.
2. Make the stakes felt in the body, so the threat seems real.
3. Give just enough facts and relatable characters with material feelings to prevent counterarguing.
4. Influence: let resistance dissolve inside the story so they adopt its view willingly.
5. State the message implicitly, with slight ambiguity, so they build the takeaway after the story ends and feel it is theirs.
6. Transform: leave a lasting imprint that leads to action and to retelling.
**Common mistakes:** Using only a flash discount or cute photo, which hits one element and collapses; stating the moral outright.
**Example:** A water-filter company shows Ana, a nurse, filling a baby bottle at 3 a.m. while the camera lingers on the sound of the tap and her pause. No line says "unsafe water"; viewers reach that themselves and several retell the scene at work.
**Sources:** Hall; Sandler; Gottschall

## F266 · Strategy told as numbered chapters
**Also known as:** Story in Chapters / Action Agenda for Corporate Strategy (Forman)
**Use when:** Leading a turnaround, merger integration or long redirection and you need a shared map of where the company is in the plot.
**Avoid when:** There is no culture and reward system behind the story, or metrics do not yet justify a later chapter; do not claim one early.
**Steps:**
1. Stabilize: stop the decline, assess reality, set direction and name leader behaviors.
2. Repair: run a short action checklist for compliance, quality, legal and cost problems while rebuilding trust and morale.
3. Turn around: reach first sales and earnings growth, launch products, crystallize the new culture.
4. Build the base: generate cash flow, fill the pipeline, scale by acquisition with careful integration.
5. Break out: reach sustained top-tier performance, or adapt the last chapter if a merger follows.
6. Tie every letter, metric and major decision to its chapter, and link the behaviors to appraisals and pay.
**Common mistakes:** Getting ahead of the facts; telling the story without incentives.
**Example:** A regional hospital group in financial distress frames five chapters over six years. Each monthly note starts with "We are in Chapter 2" and each report maps to a chapter. Bonuses depend on the shared-staffing behavior named in the story.
**Sources:** Forman

## F267 · End-user journey solution story
**Also known as:** Care Cycle Model / Human-Centric Lighting Design Model (Forman)
**Use when:** Selling complex B2B technology or turnkey services where institutional buyers must see the impact on the end user.
**Avoid when:** The solutions strategy breaks from a product-centric past; old product archives cannot support it.
**Steps:**
1. Map the end user's full journey or setting. In healthcare: prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, managing consequences, surveillance. In lighting: needs first, space second, solutions third.
2. Ask the institutional buyer about their gaps across those stages instead of pushing one device.
3. Tell a concrete story of one person moving through the cycle with the integrated solution, backed by data and metrics.
**Common mistakes:** Reciting device specs.
**Example:** Selling a warehouse-robotics system to a retail chain, a rep skips payload tables. She follows picker Luis through one shift: scan, route, restock, handover, showing where each robot removes a walk, then gives injury and throughput figures from a pilot.
**Sources:** Forman
