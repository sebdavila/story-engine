# Frameworks — structure (F089–F099)

## F089 · Stepping-stone scenes instead of a memorized script
**Also known as:** Crossing the Stream ("Major Stones" vs. "Bridge") and Four-Step Dramatic Pattern (Maguire); Portrait Gallery vs. Plot-Driven (Maguire)
**Use when:** You turn a raw spoken draft into a tight oral performance of about ten to thirteen minutes.
**Avoid when:** You add more than three main scenes; pacing and focus suffer in a standard telling.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a portrait gallery (facets of a person or place) or the four-step plot: status quo, change or impending drama, turning point or crisis, aftermath.
2. Pick three major scenes (the stones), two to three minutes each.
3. Budget about one minute for the intro, one for all transitions and one for the ending.
4. Memorize the scenes, not the words, and improvise the language between stones.
**Common mistakes:** Word-for-word memorizing; a fourth and fifth scene.
**Example:** A retired ferry captain tells of his last crossing: dock at dawn with the old engine, the fog turn when the radar died, and the passengers singing at the pier. He plans the stones, not the sentences.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F090 · Character built from a sacred flaw
**Also known as:** The Sacred Flaw Approach (Storr)
**Use when:** You start a story from scratch, or rewind from a setting, what-if premise or argument, to avoid flat clichés.
**Avoid when:** You treat it as a rulebook; drop steps once the character takes off, and do not push the flaw into caricature.
**Steps:**
1. Find the ignition point: pair an unexpected change with a flawed character whose weakness it triggers.
2. Write a scene from the first two decades showing the incident that planted a false belief.
3. Set the five personality dials (see F093).
4. Write a youth scene where acting on the false belief seems to work.
5. Write a confrontation where an authority challenges a flawed choice and the character defends it as virtue.
6. Render an opening through the character's biased mind.
7. Map the job, home, status, relationships and hidden weak spots the flaw has built.
8. Define conscious want and subconscious need, with urgency and secrecy.
9. Plot four stages with one or two reversals toward healing or deeper ruin.
10. Answer the dramatic question at the end.
**Common mistakes:** Pushing the flaw until the character is a caricature; keeping every step once the character takes off.
**Example:** A pharmacist who believes that being needed means being safe. Her father was left alone when she refused to cover a shift. A new colleague who needs no one shakes her, and she keeps overworking until her mistake costs a patient.
**Sources:** Storr

## F091 · Plot as layered change
**Also known as:** Plot as Symphony of Change (Storr)
**Use when:** You build act two, or find a flat, predictable middle.
**Avoid when:** You expect every layer to move equally; forms weight them differently, as procedurals lean on information gaps and character studies on shifting views of the protagonist.
**Steps:**
1. Advance outer events in a tight cause-and-effect chain.
2. Change the protagonist beneath the surface.
3. Shift their understanding, plan or goal.
4. Alter relationships and the paths of secondary characters.
5. Open, tease and close what the reader knows and suspects.
**Common mistakes:** Leaning on a formulaic beat sheet that yields repetitive stories.
**Example:** The middle act of a hospital drama. The ward loses power (event), the night nurse realises she no longer trusts her own judgement (inner), drops her transfer request and asks to run the unit (goal), her mentor turns cold while a junior doctor starts covering for her (relationship), and a maintenance log shows the generator test was faked (reader knowledge). Each scene moves at least two layers.
**Sources:** Storr

## F092 · Four curiosity triggers
**Also known as:** Loewenstein's Four Curiosity Triggers (Storr)
**Use when:** You craft openings, plant mystery boxes or sustain tension across slow stretches.
**Avoid when:** The audience has no context, or believes it already knows the answer; curiosity is strongest with partial clues.
**Steps:**
1. Pose a puzzle or unexplained incident.
2. Run a sequence whose outcome is uncertain.
3. Break an expectation with a red herring or surprising behavior that demands an explanation.
4. Show that someone in the story knows the answer while withholding it from the audience.
**Example:** A CFO opens an all-hands talk: the best-selling product line was quietly pulled from the website last quarter (puzzle), yet sales rose (broken expectation). She says the head of support knows why and will explain at the end (withheld knowledge), then walks through a three-week pilot whose outcome nobody knows yet (uncertain sequence).
**Sources:** Storr

## F093 · Personality dials for characters
**Also known as:** The Big Five Personality Dials (Storr)
**Use when:** You decide how a protagonist and cast react to stress, conflict and unexpected change.
**Avoid when:** You treat traits as binary switches; culture, upbringing and situation bend behavior around the core.
**Steps:**
1. Extraversion: high seeks attention and sensation; low withdraws.
2. Neuroticism: a high score means anxiety, anger and self-consciousness; a low score means composure when threatened.
3. Openness: high is curious and novelty-seeking; low prefers convention.
4. Agreeableness: high is trusting and sympathetic; low is competitive and hard-bargaining.
5. Conscientiousness: high prizes order and duty; low is impulsive.
6. Read the combination as the character's default way to control stress.
**Example:** A wedding photographer scores high on agreeableness and neuroticism and low on conscientiousness. When the couple's schedule collapses, she apologises to everyone, says yes to every new shot request and forgets the family portrait. Pleasing people is her way to control stress.
**Sources:** Storr

## F094 · Fifty-word speed story
**Also known as:** 50 Mighty Words framework (Cowie); 50-word linking assembly (Cowie)
**Use when:** You introduce a business, offer or project in a meeting, email, pitch or site where attention is scarce.
**Avoid when:** You mean to close a sale; it opens a conversation, so asking for a purchase is premature.
**Steps:**
1. Who: name the thing you promote, not the founder.
2. What: its main action in plain words, no jargon.
3. Where: its setting, place, sector or recognizable problem context.
4. When: credibility from experience, history, data timeframe or reputable partners.
5. How: the process, only after the first four have built curiosity.
6. Why: the concrete problem solved and benefit.
7. Join the six answers into one paragraph with minimal connectors, ending with one low-risk way to learn more. Use it as an email opener in place of a deck.
**Common mistakes:** Skipping a block; adding detail until it is far over fifty words.
**Example:** Ledgerline / does the books for family restaurants / in Chicago's neighborhood dining scene / for eleven years and three hundred kitchens / through one weekly pickup of receipts / so owners spend Sunday with family, not spreadsheets. Join the blocks with a few connectors, then offer a free month-end review.
**Sources:** Cowie

## F095 · Method, process, flexibility breakdown
**Also known as:** Breaking down the how (3-part Process model) (Cowie)
**Use when:** You unpack how a product or initiative delivers, before choosing what goes into the speed story (F094).
**Avoid when:** You try to fit all three parts into fifty words; choose the single most essential piece.
**Steps:**
1. Method: the headline approach in one clear statement.
2. Process: the core steps that put the method into practice, without technical detail.
3. Flexibility: what can adapt to user or team feedback, signaling room to collaborate.
**Common mistakes:** Listing every step; leaving out what can bend.
**Example:** A tutoring app. Method: short daily quizzes. Process: diagnose gaps on Monday, serve three drills, send parents a Friday note. Flexibility: quiz length changes with teacher feedback.
**Sources:** Cowie

## F096 · Universal story grammar
**Also known as:** The Universal Grammar of Storytelling (Gottschall)
**Use when:** You structure fiction, journalism, history or persuasion, or diagnose why an experimental narrative loses readers.
**Avoid when:** You write deliberately avant-garde work; breaking the grammar trades broad appeal for novelty.
**Steps:**
1. Start with real trouble, a predicament, not smooth sailing.
2. Escalate conflict, preferably between human wills, until a late turn, then stop soon after.
3. Tie the stakes to morality and give fitting justice, rewarding cooperation and punishing wrongdoing.
4. Allow chance to start the story but never to solve it; earn the climax through values and choices.
**Common mistakes:** A lucky rescue; resolution dragged out for pages.
**Example:** A bakery loses its lease on the day of a big order. The landlord's nephew and the owner clash, each move worsens things, and only her decision to share the oven with a rival wins the day. The story stops at the first sold-out morning.
**Sources:** Gottschall

## F097 · Cooperators against bullies (agonistic structure)
**Also known as:** Agonistic Structure (Gottschall)
**Use when:** You design protagonists and antagonists in drama or campaigns, or audit a story for tribal caricature.
**Avoid when:** You apply it to real people, politics or history; it produces dehumanized monsters (see F098).
**Steps:**
1. Build a prosocial protagonist who balances self-interest with the group, cooperates and grows morally.
2. Build a selfish, exploitative antagonist who threatens the group and stays morally static.
3. Stage a contest in which the group bands together to defeat the bully.
**Common mistakes:** A protagonist who never changes; an antagonist with no real threat.
**Example:** In a village-library tale, a volunteer learns to share the keys, while a developer hoards the land. The neighbors pool signatures and the developer withdraws.
**Sources:** Gottschall

## F098 · Trouble without villains
**Also known as:** History Without Villains / The Moral-Luck Model (Gottschall); Villain-free tragedy (Gottschall)
**Use when:** You write history, journalism or drama about real harms where goody-and-baddy framing would deepen division.
**Avoid when:** The subject is an atrocity; name the wrongs and the duty of repair explicitly, or it reads as excusing.
**Steps:**
1. Keep the intense trouble and high stakes of F096.
2. Replace cartoon antagonists with rounded people acting from recognizable motives such as love, fear, carelessness and social pressure.
3. Trace the harm to circumstance, small errors and bad luck, not innate wickedness.
**Common mistakes:** Telling an inverted counter-myth in place of the one-sided myth; excusing perpetrators of an atrocity.
**Example:** A history of a mill town's closure: the owner trims wages out of fear of a bank loan, the union rep rejects a concession out of loyalty to older workers, and the inspector retires without a successor. Each acts from recognizable motives. Together their choices and some bad luck with a cold winter empty the mill.
**Sources:** Gottschall

## F099 · Desire, overreach, return (sitcom arc)
**Also known as:** Comedy Writing Template / Seinfeld Plot Structure (Snow; Lazauskas)
**Use when:** You craft humorous brand anecdotes, everyday stories or cautionary tales about hubris.
**Avoid when:** The story needs an earnest, heroic transformation.
**Steps:**
1. Place the character in a familiar zone of comfort.
2. Give a desire that pulls them into an unfamiliar situation.
3. Let us watch them adjust and end up with what they were after.
4. Make them pay a heavy price or suffer ironic consequences.
5. Return them to their starting state, with a lesson learned or a change.
**Common mistakes:** Skipping the heavy price or ironic consequences.
**Example:** A quiet library volunteer, content with his desk lunch, signs up for a chili cook-off to impress a new colleague. He wins the first round and brags online. The final round is a ghost-pepper dish, and he ends up weeping in the lobby. Next day he is back at his desk with a plain sandwich, now admitting he likes it.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas
