# Frameworks — data

## F23 · Find the one insight (SAFE)
**Also known as:** The SAFE Process (Edwards)
**Use when:** You hold more numbers than the audience can absorb and must turn an analysis into a decision for generalist or non-technical decision-makers: a board update, a quarterly review, a recommendation deck.
**Avoid when:** A stakeholder has explicitly asked for the complete, unfiltered dataset with no editorial selection (Edwards). Even then, offer a one-line summary on top.
**Steps:**
1. Source: run the numbers or gather the existing datasets that bear on the question.
2. Analyse for this audience: list what each decision-maker is measured on, what they have said in public or in meetings, and what pressure their role puts on them. Mark every data point that touches one of those items.
3. Find the one: pick the single finding that is worth most to this audience. Write it as one sentence with a number in it. That sentence becomes the headline and the destination of the story (see the destination beat in F12).
4. Eliminate: remove every chart and figure that does not support that sentence from the main deck. Move them to an appendix or leave-behind (T032). Ask a colleague who has not seen the analysis to point at what they would not miss; cut those first.
5. Shape the remaining material with a data plot (S05) and a purpose (S04), then title each slide with its takeaway (T029).
**Common mistakes:** Presenting the analysis in the order you did it. Keeping a chart because it took effort. Choosing the finding that is most interesting to the analyst instead of most valuable to the listener. Picking two or three headline findings, which dilutes all of them.
**Example:** A retail analyst has forty charts on store traffic for a regional director whose bonus depends on margin. She notices that weekday-evening visits now produce a third of margin with a quarter of the staff hours. She opens with that sentence, shows the one chart that proves it, and puts the other thirty-nine in an appendix. The meeting ends with a decision to trial longer evening shifts in five stores.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F24 · Story-wrapped data
**Also known as:** Story-Wrapped Data Formula (Hall); Story-Plus-Fact-Sheet Pairing (Forman)
**Use when:** The audience needs quantitative proof but will scrutinise any number that arrives cold: analytical buyers, executives, donors, finance and engineering committees.
**Avoid when:** The audience has no time for a story and only needs the figure (use T033), or the story would be decoration with no link to the numbers.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a short true story whose turn is the problem your data measures. Use the before–turn–after arc (F01): tell the ordinary situation and the moment it broke, then stop before the outcome.
2. Insert the data at the pause: the key figure, the trend, the proof points, the credentials. Keep it short and tie each number back to the character in the story (T045). Label the number with an honest feeling word if the room is analytical (T044).
3. Return to the story and tell the changed situation, so the last thing the audience holds is the person, not the table.
4. For engineering, science or finance audiences, swap step 3 or follow it with a plain fact sheet: specs, method, sources, with no persuasive language (Forman).
**Common mistakes:** Opening with the numbers and adding the story as an afterthought. Leaving the story unfinished, so the audience never gets the resolution. A story that does not illustrate the figure that follows. Overloading the middle with every statistic you have instead of the few that matter (F23).
**Example:** A hospital fundraiser opens with a night-shift nurse who drove a discharged patient home because no taxi would come in the snow. She pauses the story to show that missed follow-up visits fell by a fifth after the transport programme started, and what one extra van costs a year. She ends with the patient, three months later, walking into the clinic for her check-up on time. Donors receive a one-page fact sheet on the way out.
**Sources:** Hall; Forman

## F25 · Charts designed for memory
**Also known as:** The visualization system / Three types of memory (Parikh)
**Use when:** You must show quantitative data on a slide or in a report and want the viewer to get the point within about ten seconds and carry it into the decision.
**Avoid when:** Readers need to look up exact individual values; give them a table instead (T037).
**Steps:**
1. Catch the eye at once: give one element a pre-attentive cue, such as a contrast colour, a thicker line, a larger size or a big-number callout, on exactly the data that proves the point (T036). Mute everything else to grey.
2. Respect working memory: keep the chart to about four visual chunks. Replace raw grids of numbers with the chart type that matches the data shape (T034). Remove gridlines, decimals, redundant labels and legends; label lines directly where they end (T035).
3. Reach long-term memory: write a full-sentence title that states the message, not the topic (T029). The title and the highlighted element must say the same thing.
4. Test: show the slide to someone for ten seconds, hide it, and ask what it said. If they cannot repeat the title's claim, simplify again or replace the chart with the number (T033).
**Common mistakes:** Topic titles such as a metric name and a date range. Several highlights competing on one chart. Legends that force eye travel. Dense tables pasted from a spreadsheet when one comparison would do.
**Example:** A logistics team shows a grid of on-time rates for six warehouses over twelve months. They redraw it as a line chart with five grey lines and one red line for the warehouse that switched to night loading, labelled at its end. The title reads: Night loading lifted one warehouse from worst to best on-time rate in six months. Viewers name the warehouse and the change without being told.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F26 · Bright spots: contrast top and bottom performers
**Also known as:** Bright Spot Analysis (McKinsey)
**Use when:** You need qualitative evidence for how to raise performance in a role or process, and want a story grounded in what people actually did rather than in opinion.
**Avoid when:** There is no objective performance measure to rank people by, or the group is too small to form distinct top and bottom tiers.
**Steps:**
1. Rank everyone in the role by an objective recent result, such as the last two quarters of sales or resolution time, without asking managers to nominate their stars.
2. Split the ranking into quartiles or quintiles. Check that the top tier already exceeds your target, which proves the goal is reachable. Remove flukes from both ends: one-off windfalls at the top, disruptions outside anyone's control at the bottom.
3. Interview the top group about one specific recent transaction, step by step, in time order: how they found it, first contact, what they used, who decided, how it closed. Ask about a concrete event, not about their best practices (see T017).
4. Run the identical interview, with the same open, non-leading questions, with the bottom group.
5. List the behaviors that appear in the top group's walk-throughs and are missing from the bottom group's. That difference is the story and the training content; tell it through one top performer's transaction (T045).
**Common mistakes:** Interviewing only top performers, which cannot separate what makes them different from what everyone does. Letting managers pick the sample. Asking abstract questions that produce slogans. Leading questions that confirm your hypothesis.
**Example:** A software company wants support agents to resolve more tickets on first contact. It ranks agents on two quarters of data, drops one agent whose numbers came from a single easy product launch, and walks ten top and ten bottom agents through their last difficult ticket. Only the top group reproduced the customer's error on their own screen before replying. That habit becomes a two-minute story told by one agent at the next team meeting and the first module of onboarding.
**Sources:** McKinsey
