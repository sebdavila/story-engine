# Frameworks — finding (F125–F134)

## F125 · Passion-action-commitment storytelling workshop
**Also known as:** Three-Phase Storytelling Workshop for Change: Passion or Inspiration, Action, Commitment (Forman)
**Use when:** Rolling out a new business model, brand promise or culture shift, and technical staff must move from talking about features to telling customer stories in their own words.
**Avoid when:** Senior leaders and line managers have not bought in. In engineering groups where the word storytelling is stigmatized, rebrand the session as a technical innovation workshop and leave the word out (Forman).
**Steps:**
1. Passion: open with a photo exhibit, a video or personal photos to trigger emotional memories. Participants share personal stories about the human domain of the business (for example health or light) in a risk-free setting, then link those feelings to the corporate brand story.
2. Action: a respected line manager demonstrates tailoring the brand story for each stakeholder group. Bring in customer-journey frameworks. Participants interview real customers or role-play scenarios, draft stories with proof points, adapt them for contrasting venues and take peer and customer critique.
3. Commitment: each participant records concrete follow-up promises on start, stop and continue cards. Provide an intranet toolkit with storylines per segment, FAQs and story templates so the practice spreads.
**Common mistakes:** Skipping the customer interview, so stories stay about features; leaving without recorded commitments.
**Example:** A pump manufacturer runs a half-day for its engineers. They begin by bringing photos of a time clean water mattered to them, then interview a farmer whose borehole failed, draft a story from her words, and retell it for a bank audience. Each writes a card promising to open the next client meeting with a customer story.
**Sources:** Forman

## F126 · Daily storyworthy-moment log
**Also known as:** Homework for Life (Dicks)
**Use when:** You want a steady supply of personal story ideas and to recover forgotten memories.
**Avoid when:** You expect quick results; great stories rarely appear in the first weeks, and the lens takes from a month to a year of daily practice to sharpen (Dicks).
**Steps:**
1. Make a two-column spreadsheet: date and story.
2. Each evening spend five minutes asking what single moment made today different from every earlier day, as though you had to tell a five-minute onstage story about it.
3. Record only one or two sentences, never the full story, so the habit stays easy and entries stay sortable.
4. Add older memories whenever the daily reflection triggers them.
5. Scan the log periodically for recurring patterns.
**Common mistakes:** Writing the whole story each night and quitting; judging entries as too small.
**Example:** A teacher logs that a student returned a stolen pencil with a note, and weeks later that a neighbor returned a ladder with a note. Scanning the log, she sees she is moved by quiet apologies, and builds a story on the first one.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F127 · Uncensored free-association memory mining
**Also known as:** Crash and Burn (Dicks); Mind Mapping / Clustering (Apps)
**Use when:** Mining the past for story seeds, anecdotes and essay ideas in a solo session of 10 to 15 minutes, or planning a draft without a rigid outline.
**Avoid when:** You need a structured narrative; this yields raw fragments (Dicks), or a loose skeleton that needs heavy structural revision when used as an outline (Apps). It is also harder to repeat daily than a short log (Dicks).
**Steps:**
1. Dicks: in a quiet space, start from any object or word in the room and write nonstop by hand or keyboard.
2. Rule of no attachment: leave the current thought the instant a new one crashes in, even if the old one feels stronger.
3. Rule of no judgment: write absurd, embarrassing or nonsensical thoughts, ignoring grammar and outline.
4. Rule of keeping moving: when you go blank, write a long familiar list (colors, numbers, countries) until a new association sparks.
5. Apps (alternative): put a central word in a circle on a blank page, draw lines out to circles for whatever memories arrive, branching off the newest ones without judging relevance, about 15 minutes in a workshop.
6. Review the raw output or the web of clusters, and pull out viable stories; with the map, pick one or several connected circles to draft.
**Common mistakes:** Stopping to fix a sentence; clinging to the first strong thread.
**Example:** A cook starts from a wooden spoon on the counter. The stream goes to her aunt's kitchen, to a burnt pot, to a quarrel she had forgotten about money, and then a list of spices. Reviewing it, she circles the quarrel as a seed worth a story.
**Sources:** Dicks; Apps

## F128 · First-last-best-worst memory grid
**Also known as:** First Last Best Worst (Dicks)
**Use when:** Brainstorming story material fast, running a speaking drill, or filling a long drive or date with conversation.
**Avoid when:** The prompts are dull; uninteresting challenge prompts leave many blank cells, though they can still surface forgotten anecdotes (Dicks).
**Steps:**
1. Sketch a grid with the columns first, last, best and worst and the prompt categories as rows.
2. Begin with categories that yield plenty (kiss, car, pet, trouble, injury, gift, travel), or challenge yourself with dull ones (tree, toaster, board game, flag, socks).
3. Put a brief memory label in each cell.
4. Analyze with three checks: any entry that appears more than once is a strong signal of a story; mark full-story candidates S; mark smaller supporting pieces A for anecdote.
**Common mistakes:** Writing whole stories in cells; ignoring repeated entries.
**Example:** A man's grid shows his old pickup under first car, worst car and worst injury, and his brother's name under best gift. He marks the pickup S and builds a story about the winter he and his brother rebuilt it.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F129 · Talk through a memory to find its meaning
**Also known as:** Finding the Frayed Ending (Dicks)
**Use when:** You know a memory matters, or an odd habit feels strange, but cannot yet say why.
**Avoid when:** You want a finished draft; this gives raw emotional clarity and still needs one chosen arc and structural editing (Dicks).
**Steps:**
1. Speak the whole memory aloud from start to finish with no regard for craft, stakes or cuts; spill the messy details so you stand back in the scene.
2. Pay attention to the feeling change or insight that comes up as you relive it.
3. Or take an odd recurring behavior, ask why you do it, and speak the relevant history aloud until the underlying truth emerges.
4. If two meanings appear, choose one for this story and build the story's opposite beginning to match.
**Common mistakes:** Trying to keep both meanings in a single story; editing while talking.
**Example:** A woman keeps her brother's voicemails unplayed. Talking aloud, she recalls the last one, a joke about a borrowed coat, and hears the shift: she is afraid that hearing it would make it final. She chooses that fear as the meaning and plans a story that begins with her confident refusal to grieve.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F130 · Issue or logic tree before the storyline
**Also known as:** Issue Tree and Hypothesis Tree Workflow (McKinsey); Logic tree (Parikh); Branch-level navigation checkpoint (Parikh); Visual self-screening prompt (Parikh); Real-time logic tree markup (Parikh)
**Use when:** Starting a strategy or problem-solving assignment, or an open-ended brief where guessing the audience's focus risks irrelevant content, before opening slides or analyzing data.
**Avoid when:** Scope and stakeholder expectations are already pinned down (Parikh).
**Steps:**
1. Scope the problem: keep asking what impact it has until the scope exceeds the decision-maker's remit, then step back one level (McKinsey).
2. List constraints, organizational, regulatory, financial or mission-based, that rule out unviable solutions (McKinsey).
3. Build the tree: put the problem on the left and break it top-down into up to five branches, each with sub-elements, as an unvalidated hypothesis (Parikh). Make sub-questions mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, in full sentences, finishing each level before going deeper (McKinsey).
4. Convert each question into a testable, decisive statement (McKinsey).
5. Co-create with stakeholders: validate the main branches before any sub-item, ask them to scan each branch and say what stands out or is redundant instead of reading nodes aloud, and mark the shared diagram live: circle kept items, strike rejected ones, dotted line for possible ones. Add missing branches and rank survivors by number (Parikh).
6. Filter hypotheses on impact against ease, plus urgency, strategic fit and capability (McKinsey).
7. Sketch the deck on paper first: full-sentence slide titles as dots, supporting facts and exhibits as dashes (McKinsey).
8. Test hypotheses, including searching for disconfirming evidence, and update the tree and sketch (McKinsey).
**Common mistakes:** Treating the process as linear; skipping stakeholder ranking; overlapping branches.
**Example:** A cafe chain owner asks how to grow profit. The analyst splits it into higher revenue and lower cost, each with sub-branches, and reviews it in a half-hour call with the owner and two managers. They strike the rent branch, as the leases were just renegotiated, and rank loyalty offers first. The deck outline is sketched around that ranking.
**Sources:** McKinsey; Parikh

## F131 · Top-versus-bottom performer comparison
**Also known as:** Bright Spot Analysis (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Gathering qualitative data to test hypotheses on how to raise human or organizational performance.
**Avoid when:** You would interview only the top group; that cannot separate true differentiating behaviors from habits the bottom group shares (McKinsey).
**Steps:**
1. Stack-rank people on recent objective results, such as the last two quarters, not on managers' opinions, which carry bias from relationships and halo effects.
2. Split into quartiles or quintiles, check the top tier beats your target, and remove outliers from both ends (one-off windfalls, uncontrollable disruptions).
3. Ask about ten top performers to walk through one specific recent transaction step by step: prospect, first contact, resources, decision-makers, close. Avoid asking for abstract best practices.
4. Run the identical step-by-step interview with the bottom group, using non-leading, open questions.
5. Isolate the behaviors in the top workflows that are absent from the bottom ones, and codify that difference to train the organization.
**Common mistakes:** Letting the interviewer lead answers; mixing in windfall results.
**Example:** A clinic wants fewer missed appointments. It ranks front-desk staff by no-show rates over two quarters, drops one with a temporary flu-season spike, and has top and bottom staff each narrate their last reminder call. The top group alone confirms the time and asks how the patient will get there.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F132 · Writing habit system
**Also known as:** Four Laws of Behavior Change, Storytelling Habit System (Lazer); Creating Self vs. Judging Self and Overcoming Procrastination (Apps)
**Use when:** You struggle with consistency in a newsletter, social cadence, book or team storytelling routine, or you obsess over openings and wait for inspiration.
**Avoid when:** Used alone; it does not say what to write and needs craft frameworks so volume turns into quality (Lazer).
**Steps:**
1. Start from identity: define yourself as someone who writes a set amount daily rather than someone hoping for a bestseller (Lazer).
2. Make it obvious: tie writing to a clear calendar or environmental cue, such as a fixed daily block on your calendar, even 30 minutes (Lazer; Apps).
3. Make it attractive: pair the session with a small enjoyable ritual (Lazer).
4. Make it easy: remove distractions (Apps checks email at most hourly), set a clear stop signal, and timebox the draft (Lazer). Avoid high-pressure promises such as a finished family history by next month's reunion (Apps).
5. Silence the judging self: write nonstop for 10 to 15 minutes on a timer, or type that you cannot think of anything until an idea comes (Apps).
6. Treat the first draft as raw material and push to the end without editing (Apps).
7. Make it satisfying: log daily word counts, share progress with peers or take audience feedback (Lazer).
**Common mistakes:** Rewriting the opening for months; waiting for the mood.
**Example:** A nurse decides she is someone who writes a hundred words before her shift. She keeps her notebook by the kettle, allows 15 minutes, ticks a calendar and texts a friend each Friday. Her first drafts are rough, and she finishes a short story in two months.
**Sources:** Lazer; Apps

## F133 · Childhood floor plan memory trigger
**Also known as:** House Plan (Apps)
**Use when:** A writer says they cannot remember enough of their childhood to start.
**Avoid when:** The home holds painful memories; the drawing can trigger trauma, so sketch a safe refuge instead, such as a backyard tree house, or decline the topic (Apps).
**Steps:**
1. On a large sheet, draw the floor plan of the home you lived in at about ages ten to twelve.
2. Add furniture and wall pictures, and label which family members belong with each room.
3. Annotate with remembered smells and feelings tied to rooms, about 10 to 15 minutes in all.
4. Pick a single room and write up one particular incident that took place in it.
**Common mistakes:** Writing about the whole house instead of one incident.
**Example:** A man draws a two-bedroom flat, with the radio in the kitchen and his father's boots by the door. Beside the hallway he writes smell of diesel. He picks the hallway and writes about the night his father came home early and said nothing.
**Sources:** Apps

## F134 · Lifeline memory mining
**Also known as:** Thinking Backward and Feeling Backward (Maguire); Crossroads Lifeline and Story-Translation Options (Maguire); Turning Points in Life (Apps)
**Use when:** You are stuck in vague nostalgia or cannot recall specific moments, are choosing the highest-impact moments for a memoir, or turning messy biography, regrets or private wounds into a tellable story.
**Avoid when:** You use it alone; it focuses on major inflection points, so pair it with everyday memory triggers so quieter stories are not missed (Apps). Living people and sensitive wounds call for care in what you shape (Maguire).
**Steps:**
1. Apps, simple form: draw a horizontal line from your birth date to today, mark major turning points (graduations, marriage, divorce, births, illness, bereavement, decisive encounters) and write one story of what happened and how it changed your path.
2. Maguire, thinking backward: pick a catalyst (an object, a milestone, or one emotion from a 60-emotion grid), start at age 16 or the present, and step back in fixed intervals of one to two years, or one, two, five and ten years. Record the concrete scene at each stop without judging worth, then ask four reflection questions to find the meaning.
3. Maguire, crossroads: draw a vertical lifeline in five-year segments; mark paths taken with right-pointing arrows and paths not taken with left-pointing arrows; probe the context of each decision with five questions and study the road not taken.
4. Reshape awkward, incomplete or overexposed material with one of four story-translation options, shifting standpoint as needed, until it becomes emotionally truthful.
**Common mistakes:** Summarizing instead of recalling a time-anchored scene.
**Example:** A pilot draws her lifeline and marks a right-pointing arrow at moving abroad at 24 and a left-pointing arrow at the conservatory place she declined. She writes the scene of the phone call declining it, and retells it from her mother's standpoint to soften the regret.
**Sources:** Maguire; Apps
