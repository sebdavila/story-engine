# Frameworks — finding (F115–F124)

## F115 · Audience Listening Before Drafting
**Also known as:** Buyer persona and Typeform customer survey (Mastropierro); Audience Immersion and Listening Sequence (Creek); Seek First to Understand (Maestas)
**Use when:** Before writing, entering a niche or a community whose daily life differs from yours, choosing between creative angles, or when you lack insight into why customers bought.
**Avoid when:** You need a quick result; listening takes real time (nearly a year in Maestas's case) and yields action only if people are willing to share. Never drop a promotional piece into a group you have not listened to (Creek).
**Steps:**
1. Admit what you do not know, even if you share the community's background (Maestas). Draft a buyer persona and treat it as a draft to correct (Mastropierro).
2. Go where real buyers gather. Lurk silently in tightly niched online groups for about two weeks without posting or selling (Creek). Offline, run a quiet learning tour with no cameras, media or entourage (Maestas).
3. Guarantee anonymity so no answer traces to one person or site (Maestas). Ask participants to list challenges and opportunities for growth.
4. Run a short customer-feedback survey on pre-purchase problems, brand values and emotions, sent by link, email, chatbot or web page (Mastropierro).
5. Join in as a member with no sales agenda, then float rival angles or run polls and livestreams to see what draws the strongest reaction (Creek).
6. Keep only themes and anecdotes that recur across all interviews or responses (Maestas), and build the message from the audience's own words and pains (Mastropierro).
7. Work backward from the proven desire to the creative execution (Creek).
**Common mistakes:** Treating the persona as fact; selling during the listening phase; keeping vivid one-off stories that did not recur.
**Example:** A coach for night-shift nurses reads two nurse forums for two weeks and notes the same complaint: nobody understands why they cannot sleep after a shift. She then posts two possible hooks as a poll, and the one about sleep wins by a wide margin. Her welcome email uses the nurses' own phrase for it.
**Sources:** Mastropierro; Creek; Maestas

## F116 · Living for story material
**Also known as:** A Recipe for Creating Interesting Stories (Leitman); Storystalking (Maguire)
**Use when:** You think nothing interesting happens to you, your past stories are used up, or you want fresh timely material.
**Avoid when:** The plan involves physical danger or a staged stunt done only to have a story; stay open to real experiences (Leitman). Do not let self-consciousness during the experience replace real presence (Maguire).
**Steps:**
1. Notice where comfort and routine have replaced risk in your life (Leitman).
2. Take a beginner's-mind look at routine surroundings, such as your own street or a pet (Maguire).
3. Log oddities and magic moments as they happen, in real time (Maguire).
4. Say yes to unfamiliar invitations, events and conversations with strangers, keeping an exit plan ready (Leitman).
5. Set yourself small comfort-zone challenges as story assignments and make active choices instead of waiting for things to happen (Leitman; Maguire). Pay close sensory and emotional attention while it unfolds.
**Common mistakes:** Manufacturing a stunt; letting self-consciousness crowd out presence during the experience.
**Example:** A quiet accountant sets a weekly assignment: eat lunch at a different market stall and ask the cook one question. In week three the cook gives him a recipe card and a scolding about salt. He writes both lines in his phone that evening and later builds a short story on them.
**Sources:** Leitman; Maguire

## F117 · Genre Exemplar Analysis
**Also known as:** Three-Question Assessment Model (Cleland)
**Use when:** Starting a project in a genre new to you, or finding out why a draft's middle sags or drifts.
**Avoid when:** You would copy the pattern as a template; it guides choices, it is not a mold (Cleland).
**Steps:**
1. Pick enduringly popular or best-selling stories in your genre.
2. For each, identify who longs for what.
3. Identify what those characters will do to get it.
4. Identify who or what opposes them, and check that the opposed goals make simultaneous victory impossible.
5. Compare the patterns across exemplars and use them to guide your own conflict.
**Common mistakes:** Choosing a story because it is recent rather than lasting; opposition that lets both sides win.
**Example:** A writer planning a lighthouse mystery reads three classics. In each, a keeper wants his post kept, will lie to keep it, and an inspector wants the post closed, so only one can win. She notices her own draft has an inspector who merely disapproves, and makes his goal the keeper's dismissal.
**Sources:** Cleland

## F118 · Drawing stories out of others
**Also known as:** Six Conditions for Stories to Be Told (Callahan); The Pretend-Insider Prompt (Callahan); STORY Active Story-Listening Framework (Lazer)
**Use when:** Running discovery calls, customer or staff interviews, retrospectives, check-ins or podcast conversations where you want real stories rather than abstractions.
**Avoid when:** Used as a rigid script; the method fails if you stop being present to where the speaker goes (Lazer). Pretending you are an insider yields the story but may not give you its technical meaning (Callahan).
**Steps:**
1. Check the six conditions and fix the missing one (Callahan): a curious, caring listener; free time without a rigid agenda; common ground; a short story of your own first; objects such as maps, whiteboards or photos that cue memory; trust that the story will not be misused.
2. If common ground is missing, ask the speaker to pretend you are an experienced person in their field (Callahan).
3. Synchronize: mirror the speaker's emotional tone with a relaxed posture and steady, attentive face (Lazer).
4. Build trust up front: state your intent, offer opt-outs or anonymity, or share a brief human story (Lazer).
5. Ask open questions about feelings, not yes-or-no facts (Lazer).
6. Reflect: paraphrase emotions, reframe a key phrase more sharply, or gently point out an inconsistency (Lazer).
7. Yield space: drop the question list, stay present and hold silence when the speaker pauses (Lazer).
**Common mistakes:** Interrogating instead of offering a story; interviewing away from the objects that hold the memories; filling every silence.
**Example:** A recruiter wants stories from a hospital pharmacist who answers only in policy terms. She says to pretend she is a pharmacist new to the ward, tells a short story of her own about a mislabeled bottle, and asks how that night felt. He describes the shift when a dose was nearly doubled. When he stops mid-sentence she waits quietly.
**Sources:** Callahan; Lazer

## F119 · Structured customer story collection
**Also known as:** Curating Customer Stories: Two Rules (Hall); Three-Question Hero Screening Questionnaire (Hauge)
**Use when:** Building a bank of customer stories for a site, ads, social posts or sales material, or screening a customer list to choose a hero for a case story.
**Avoid when:** You plan to over-edit or script the replies; customer stories belong to the customer and heavy editing destroys credibility (Hall). The three-question screen is not enough alone for sensory and emotional depth (Hauge).
**Steps:**
1. Ask after the experience: several days after the customer has received and used the product (Hall).
2. Make the request come from a named person rather than an automated sender or an invoice link, and offer a small gift for video replies (Hall).
3. Ask questions that mirror the arc: what they used before and why they switched, what trying it was like, how things are now, and where they were when they first tried it (Hall).
4. For screening a list, ask three things: what circumstances led them to you, what the journey felt like including setbacks, and how life or business changed (Hauge).
5. Narrow to roughly the top half-dozen answers and follow up personally for missing detail (Hauge).
**Common mistakes:** Long forms sent as a favor; settling for star ratings; asking before the customer has used the product.
**Example:** A software firm's customer-success lead texts new clients ninety days after go-live, from her own phone rather than the billing system. She asks what tool they used before and why they switched, what the first week felt like, how month-end closing goes now, and where they were when the first report ran, and offers a conference pass for a short video answer. From forty replies she keeps the six richest and calls a warehouse controller to fill in the setback he skipped.
**Sources:** Hall; Hauge

## F120 · Prompted personal story inventory
**Also known as:** Finding Stories in Two Steps (Hall); Two Approaches to Finding Stories, Work and Non-Work Tables (Dolan); The Story Wheel (Dolan); 10 Personal Repertoire Occasions (Buster); Seven Techniques for Finding Stories (Simmons); Six Ways to Find Great Stories (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** You believe you have no stories, are building a go-to story bank, or need the right story for a given pitch, talk or meeting.
**Avoid when:** You stop at collecting in a business setting; skipping choosing means telling any story instead of the right one (Hall). Do not critique young stories harshly; appreciate what works first (Simmons).
**Steps:**
1. Collect without judging: list concrete nouns (people, places, objects, events), firsts, customer objections and questions, moments you saw your message in action, and daily observations (Hall).
2. Work-related table: list every job you have had and note triumph, tragedy, tension and transition for each. Life table: list experiences from childhood onward, tick one or two of those four types per memory (Dolan). Triumph is pride and achievement told with humility; tragedy is disaster or regret with the lesson; tension is a clash of values, loyalties or obligations; transition is a significant turning point that was forced, chosen or instigated (Dolan).
3. Count stories per type and mine more memories for the thin ones, so you are not all boast or all stress (Dolan).
4. Use more prompts: ten recurring occasions such as secret self-revelation, favorite childhood story, first heartbreak, a defining cultural or sporting event, a witnessed transformation, a personal passion (Buster); and Simmons's seven searches for patterns, consequences, lessons, utility, vulnerability, imagined futures, and a favorite film or folktale retold from your own view.
5. Keep collecting daily: antennae up, read outside your field, interview employees or customers about feelings, journal milestones, and tag every story in a notes app (Biesenbach). Link to goals or key messages as a filter.
6. Choose: name the audience and what they should think, feel, know or do, then shape the matching scrap into the story that serves it (Hall). Each story must be true and have a purpose (Dolan).
**Common mistakes:** Tagging a memory with three or four types so its focus blurs; a hero who is never wrong; ignoring the audience's concerns (Biesenbach).
**Example:** A cafe founder lists nouns (grandfather's scale, the first rent check, a customer who complained about foam). Her table is all triumphs, so she digs for a tragedy and finds her first failed pop-up. For a bank loan meeting she chooses the failed pop-up, because the audience needs to see she learns.
**Sources:** Hall; Dolan; Buster; Simmons; Biesenbach

## F121 · Story mastery lifecycle
**Also known as:** The Story Mastery Process / Four Disciplines of Story Mastery (Callahan)
**Use when:** Developing and maintaining a person's workplace storytelling skill over time.
**Avoid when:** Working on only one phase; improving delivery without discovering new stories or retiring stale ones stalls the habit (Callahan).
**Steps:**
1. Discover: tell real stories from non-stories, notice everyday moments, elicit stories from others, trigger new ones through remarkable action.
2. Remember: lock stories into memory through meaning discussions, spaced retellings and a tagged story bank.
3. Share: deliver conversationally, with a relevance statement, topic stories and structured patterns.
4. Refresh: rest, swap or retire a story when it loses impact, bores you, repeats to the same audience or projects an unhelpful identity.
**Common mistakes:** Telling the same story to the same room; keeping a story after it paints you wrongly.
**Example:** A consulting partner has told the same failed-launch story at every quarterly meeting, and at the last one a colleague mouthed the ending. She rests it, picks a story about a late supplier shipment from her tagged bank, and after telling it notes the lesson the team drew in the bank entry.
**Sources:** Callahan

## F122 · Mining a personal turning point
**Also known as:** The Story Behind the Story, Threshold Arc (Buster); The Antagonist, Force of Nature, Moral Inventory (Buster)
**Use when:** Giving a familiar subject a new angle, presenting a brand or leader, raising funds, enlivening a dry, fact-laden talk, or when you think nothing dramatic ever happened to you.
**Avoid when:** The teller adopts a victim stance, or replaces a human antagonist with an impersonal event such as a car crash (Buster).
**Steps:**
1. Look past who, what, when, where, why and how to the private crossroads that demanded courage.
2. Choose a daring angle that reveals something vulnerable, mundane or embarrassing about the protagonist.
3. Link that private struggle to a universal emotion such as loneliness, fear of failure, grief or longing.
4. Show the concrete action that followed the crossing.
5. For a personal story, name one specific person, such as a mentor, boss, teacher, lover, parent or rival, who forced a change in you.
6. Take a moral inventory of who you were before: your greatest fear and one concrete moment of paralyzed inaction.
7. Pick one gleaming detail and its dominant sense; fix time, place and context; identify what the antagonist did, or neglected to do, that set off your decision.
8. Show the everyday action you took, not a summary lesson, and close by showing the new you through action or contrast.
**Common mistakes:** Avoiding the personal frame for fear of looking self-centered; ending with a moral instead of a changed behavior.
**Example:** A hospital fundraiser skips bed counts and mortality rates. She names her night-school teacher who once said her essay was not worth reading, describes the cold classroom where she froze, and shows her next week: rewriting it in the library until closing. The donors hear the fear before they hear the cause.
**Sources:** Buster

## F123 · Collective organizational story from interviews
**Also known as:** MIT Seven-Stage Organizational Storytelling Method (Salmon); Two-Column Polyphonic Drafting (Salmon); Three-Data-Type Balancing (Salmon)
**Use when:** Capturing lessons from a major internal change, such as an office move or software rollout, that carried friction and unwritten learning, without blaming individuals.
**Avoid when:** Time and budget are tight; the method is slow and costly, and automated story-indexing tools tend to flatten spontaneous speech into scripted corporate language (Salmon).
**Steps:**
1. Scope: set audiences, learning goals, boundaries, key questions and a management commitment.
2. Interview 10 to 20 participants in anonymous, open-ended ethnographic interviews, fully transcribed.
3. Code the transcripts, group candidate stories on one timeline, and balance three data types: facts for credibility, vignettes for human interest, transformational messages for impact.
4. Reduce in weekly team reviews, for example hundreds of pages down to a short narrative, formatted in two columns pairing the observer's synthesis with the participants' own voices.
5. Validate chronology, quotes and interpretation with the contributors.
6. Circulate the validated narrative inside the company.
7. Publish externally if the aim is a reputation for management innovation.
**Common mistakes:** Naming individuals as the cause of problems; dropping the participants' voices from the synthesis.
**Example:** A food-bank network switches from pre-packed boxes to a pantry where clients choose their own groceries. Fifteen volunteers, drivers and clients are interviewed anonymously, and the team lays their stories on one timeline from the first pilot to the sixth month. The two-column draft sets the coordinator's synthesis beside volunteers' own lines about the first chaotic Saturday. Contributors check the dates and quotes before the story reaches the board.
**Sources:** Salmon

## F124 · Four-prompt employee story seeding
**Also known as:** Topics-and-Questions Approach, I am FedEx campaign (Forman)
**Use when:** Getting a large or global workforce, including story-reluctant people, to contribute personal stories as videos, photos, captions or blogs that together express a culture and brand.
**Steps:**
1. Me: invite a personal passion, hobby or defining life moment, and how it connects to their work.
2. My work: ask them to describe their job, how they work with teammates, and a concrete story of going beyond the call for a customer.
3. My company: ask what makes them proud of the company and a favorite story of how it helped a person, business or community.
4. My commitment: ask how they or their team volunteer, give back or protect the environment.
5. Collect the replies as short unscripted pieces, and let the accumulation build the culture and brand narrative.
**Common mistakes:** Scripting replies.
**Example:** After a merger, a software company's HR director sends the four prompts to 600 support engineers in three countries as an open blog template with no script. One engineer begins with translating for her grandmother at clinic visits, moves to the night she restored a hospital client's booking system, then to the company's free licences for rural clinics, and ends with her team's e-waste drive. The director posts forty such entries on the merged intranet unedited. New hires read them as the shared culture neither old firm had ever written down.
**Sources:** Forman
