# Frameworks — structure (F056–F066)

## F056 · Scene beat analysis
**Also known as:** Scene Structure and Beats (Simson)
**Use when:** Breaking down a dialogue scene to plan staging, performance and coverage, or checking that conflict builds to a shift.
**Avoid when:** A short piece relies on one reveal; do not force a long multi-beat arc.
**Steps:**
1. Identify the setup: the characters enter with conflicting immediate wants.
2. Split the exchange into beats, each a moment where a character changes intention after meeting resistance.
3. Find the turning point, roughly halfway through the crisis, at which one character gets the upper hand or status reverses.
4. Trace the resolution, where the new state pushes into the next scene.
**Common mistakes:** Beats with no resistance; no status flip; a resolution that does not lead anywhere.
**Example:** A tenant tries to return a key to her landlord, who keeps finding reasons to refuse it. The turning point comes when she reveals she has already paid the deposit to a competing building, and the landlord goes from dismissive to pleading.
**Sources:** Simson

## F057 · Scene-card outline
**Also known as:** The Step Outline (Simson); Retroactive Starter Outline (D'Costa); Dramatic Bloc Grouping (Simson)
**Use when:** Inspecting flow, time and gaps in a script or draft before rewriting.
**Avoid when:** There is nothing written yet to summarize.
**Steps:**
1. Summarize the plot in prose as a short story to see what really happens (Simson). Or scan a finished discovery draft and summarize each scene in one or two sentences (D'Costa).
2. Put each scene on its own card with its heading, a day or night marker and a short title for how it advances the plot.
3. Lay the cards on a wall or table and check each transition for therefore or but links (see F004) and for time progression.
4. Group consecutive scenes sharing one timeframe into a block (Simson).
5. Add, remove or reorder cards where gaps or weaknesses appear.
**Common mistakes:** Cards that describe topics, not what happens; ignoring time markers.
**Example:** A director cards a heist short and sees that scenes 4 to 6 all happen at night, forming a block, but scene 5 repeats scene 3's information, so she cuts it and moves the alarm reveal up.
**Sources:** Simson; D'Costa

## F058 · Character interrogation
**Also known as:** Stanislavski and Dee Cannon's Ten Character Questions (Simson)
**Use when:** Preparing character breakdowns, margin notes or actor briefings before rehearsal or shooting.
**Avoid when:** It would push an actor to play the ending; play only the present scene's intentions.
**Steps:**
1. Who am I: backstory and memories.
2. Where am I: how the space reflects the character.
3. When is it: time of day, weather, place in the arc.
4. Where have I just come from: the emotional energy carried from the previous scene.
5. What do I want: the long-term objective tied to theme.
6. Why do I want it: the wound or backstory trigger.
7. Why now: the scene's urgency.
8. What happens if I do not get it now: the consequences.
9. How will I get it, doing what: concrete physical and verbal tactics.
10. What must I overcome: inner doubts and outer resistance.
**Common mistakes:** Answering only the first questions; vague tactics.
**Example:** For a nurse asking her supervisor for a transfer: she wants out of night shifts because of a past medication error, needs it before schedules post today, and will lose her confidence if refused. She tries politeness, then a list of her records, and must overcome her fear of reopening the error.
**Sources:** Simson

## F059 · Character contradiction chart
**Also known as:** The COW Chart and Character Breakdown (Simson); Two-Read Audition (Simson)
**Use when:** Preparing to cast a role or define the internal contradictions of a character.
**Avoid when:** You would cast by surface look alone; meet the actor to test chemistry and trust.
**Steps:**
1. Draw three columns: C, how the character sees themself; O, how others see them; W, how the writer sees them.
2. Fill each with specific behavior, outward defenses and hidden vulnerabilities.
3. Distill them into a short, enticing breakdown: age, background, core dilemma.
4. Match the traits to actors' real attributes, not just physical types.
5. In auditions, hear each actor's raw first read, then give one contrasting note or fact for a second read to test adaptability, without full blocking.
**Common mistakes:** Columns that repeat each other; a breakdown listing only looks.
**Example:** For a bakery owner: C says she is just stubborn; O says she is a cold boss; W says her sternness hides terror of losing her late husband's shop. The breakdown seeks a warm actor who can play guarded.
**Sources:** Simson

## F060 · Character, struggle, turning insight, change
**Also known as:** Character, Conflict, Spark, Change, Takeaway (Karia); Five-Element Story Model / PHAAT Model (Maxwell); The Story Cube (James)
**Use when:** Building a talk, keynote, pitch or business story so the audience gets a usable insight, or checking whether an event is a story or a list of happenings.
**Avoid when:** The piece is short and viral; Maxwell keeps only the insight element there and lets prior knowledge fill the rest.
**Steps:**
1. Character: a relatable, vulnerable protagonist with a clear desire. Give enough visual detail to picture them (Karia; James). Maxwell adds the narrator's own passion as the opening element.
2. Setting: anchor a specific time and place (James).
3. Struggle: a strong obstacle or opposing force with an uncertain outcome (Karia; Maxwell as antagonist).
4. Pursuit: show active, goal-directed choices (James).
5. Spark: the insight, mentor advice, method or small detail that unlocks it and hands the audience a tool (Karia; Maxwell's awareness).
6. Pivot: a turn that surprises yet makes logical sense afterward (James).
7. Change: show the visible shift in the protagonist and world (Karia; Maxwell).
8. Takeaway: state one short phrase the audience can repeat (Karia).
**Common mistakes:** Stopping at the first four sides, which reads as a case study; supplying the payoff through outside commentary.
**Example:** A logistics manager, afraid of failing a team of new drivers, faces a snowstorm that closes the depot. A driver's remark about following the plows becomes the spark, the team reroutes, and she changes from controlling to trusting. Takeaway: follow the plows.
**Sources:** Karia; Maxwell; James

## F061 · Five-second moment with opposite beginning
**Also known as:** The Five-Second Moment & Opposite Beginning Arc (Dicks)
**Use when:** Deciding whether an experience is a true story and choosing where to open and close.
**Avoid when:** An event carries two meanings; tell them as two stories. A moment you were unconscious for cannot be your moment of change.
**Steps:**
1. Find the five-second moment, the instant something fundamentally changes (a realization, decision, forgiveness, triumph or failure).
2. Place it at or near the very end and cut whatever does not clarify it.
3. State it in one plain sentence: I once felt or thought X, now Y.
4. Choose the beginning as the emotional or intellectual opposite of that ending.
5. Start as close to the ending as possible in time and space.
**Common mistakes:** Ending on the dramatic event instead of the change; a long setup.
**Example:** A man's moment is realizing at his daughter's recital that he has been the one avoiding her. The opposite opening: he believes she avoids him. He begins at the school parking lot that afternoon, not at her birth.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F062 · Five stakes-raising devices
**Also known as:** The Five Stakes Strategies (Dicks)
**Use when:** The middle of a story may lose attention, or you are building toward a turn.
**Avoid when:** The core premise is dull (tools cannot fix it) or the story already has natural peril, which needs only the first device.
**Steps:**
1. Elephant: name the central want, problem or mystery in the first 30 to 60 seconds. You may change its color midway to reveal a deeper theme.
2. Backpack: share your exact plan, hopes and fears just before an attempt, best when the plan fails.
3. Breadcrumb: drop a concrete hint of a coming twist without the answer.
4. Hourglass: before a long-awaited payoff, slow everything down with a lower voice, extra sensory detail and brief summary.
5. Crystal ball: voice a plausible, exciting false prediction you made at the time.
**Common mistakes:** Implausible predictions; stretching the hourglass with filler.
**Example:** A hiker states at once that she is lost with one bottle of water, shares her plan to follow a stream, notes a strange smell, slows down at the ridge top, and says she was sure that was a ranger's cabin.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F063 · Engineered surprise and planted setups
**Also known as:** Preserving Surprise & Planting Bombs (Dicks)
**Use when:** Designing reveals, emotional turns and setups for any story.
**Avoid when:** Planting would require inventing false clues; the detail must appear naturally in the timeline.
**Steps:**
1. Never open with a thesis or summary of the twist. Give concrete evidence first and state meaning after, if at all.
2. Build sharp contrast just before the surprise.
3. Raise stakes beforehand with the devices in F062 so the listener expects one outcome.
4. Plant key setup details as far from their payoff as possible, buried in a list of mundane details and wrapped in a laugh, so their importance is missed until the reveal.
**Common mistakes:** Announcing the lesson early; placing the setup next to the payoff.
**Example:** A bride mentions, among a list of errands and a joke about the florist, that she asked the caterer to hold a table. Later the table is where her estranged father sits, and the reveal lands.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F064 · Situation-complication-resolution business arc
**Also known as:** SCQA (McKinsey); SCR Framework (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Introducing a memo with SCQA, or structuring a whole deck with SCR to persuade decision-makers, especially when they underrate the problem.
**Avoid when:** The audience is very anxious (open with the resolution if they will accept it) or very complacent (open with the complication). SCQA is for written intros, not full decks.
**Steps:**
1. Situation: state the accepted current state and how we got here, naming the protagonist (such as profit) and key levers.
2. Complication: introduce the disruption and its consequences, projecting how trends hit those levers.
3. Question (SCQA only): note the why or how the complication raises, often unwritten.
4. Answer or Resolution: give a prioritized set of non-overlapping actions, conservative first, radical later.
**Common mistakes:** Treating SCQA as the whole pyramid; a situation the reader disputes. The arc recasts F001 for business.
**Example:** A café chain memo: sales were steady for years (Situation), but a new lease raises rent 40 percent and weekday traffic is falling (Complication). How do we protect margin? Answer: renegotiate hours first, then trim the menu, then close the weakest store.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F065 · SMART presentation title
**Also known as:** SMART Title Framework (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Titling a persuasive presentation or writing a testable hypothesis.
**Avoid when:** A fixed project name is mandated; then deliver the objective in the opening talk or on an objective slide.
**Steps:**
1. Specific: name the exact entity or domain.
2. Measurable: include a target or directional outcome.
3. Action-oriented: start with an -ing verb.
4. Relevant: choose an outcome that matters to the decision-makers present.
5. Time-bound: add the deadline.
**Common mistakes:** A bland noun-phrase title that names a topic only.
**Example:** Instead of Regional Clinic Plan, write: Cutting Northside Clinic Wait Times by Half Within Eighteen Months.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F066 · Depth-first slide sequencing
**Also known as:** Depth-First Tree Traversal (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Ordering slides within an act, after a summary slide of the section's drivers exists.
**Avoid when:** Drilling deeper than the level you will build on later; stop there, usually two levels down.
**Steps:**
1. Present the section's root claim.
2. On the next slide, answer its why or how with a short list of three to five non-overlapping drivers.
3. Drill down the first driver's sub-causes, answering each slide's question on the next.
4. If you went three or more levels deep, repeat the summary slide with the next branch highlighted.
5. Move across to each remaining branch in order and repeat.
**Common mistakes:** Jumping across drivers before finishing one, which forces the audience to rebuild context.
**Example:** A slide says customer churn doubled; the next lists three drivers: price, support, onboarding. Two slides cover price, then the summary reappears before support and onboarding, each drilled once.
**Sources:** McKinsey
