# Frameworks — persuasion

## F32 · Story before argument
**Also known as:** Influence Story Pattern (Callahan); Influence Stories (Callahan); Decision-Influence Stories (Callahan); Psychological Baby Steps for Changing Minds (Simmons); Story-first, facts-second sequencing (Simmons); The 1:3 Story-to-Argument Ratio (Callahan); Ten Situations Where Facts Are Inferior to Story (Simmons)
**Use when:** The listener already holds a strong opinion, a cynical counter-story is circulating, you must stop a bad proposal, refuse a request, correct a superior, or answer a trap question.
**Avoid when:** The counter-story people tell is true. Storytelling cannot fix it; change the behavior first so a true new story exists (Callahan).
**Steps:**
1. Relevance line: state your position in one sentence, or name the doubt they are holding back (S11, T006).
2. Connect: if the gap is wide, begin with an experience you and the listener share and feel the same way about (Simmons; T072).
3. Story: tell the concrete story before any argument. To stop a bad idea, tell a negative story of it failing, then a positive story of the alternative working. To move an entrenched belief, tell the sequence of specific experiences that changed your own mind, one small step at a time.
4. Argument: once defenses are down, give the logic, data and business implications.
5. Point: finish with the recommendation. Stop before preaching; let them draw the last inference.
6. Calibrate: across a talk or a meeting, keep roughly one part story to three parts argument (Callahan).
7. Where direct statements backfire (refusals, bad moods, things you cannot say outright, telling a boss he is wrong), use the indirect forms: a parallel story about someone elsewhere (T084), a teaser to break a sour mood (T071), a past disaster told before the no (Simmons).
**Common mistakes:** Leading with the facts, which locks listeners into their existing reading. Trying to flip a belief in one meeting. Ending with a sermon that undoes the story's work.
**Example:** A product lead wants to stop her peers from adding a discount pop-up to checkout. She says she thinks it will cost more than it earns, then tells how a sister brand ran the same pop-up and saw returns rise because buyers chose items they did not want just to reach the threshold. She follows with the story of a store that added a free-returns badge instead and kept its margin. Only then does she show the numbers and recommend the badge test.
**Sources:** Callahan; Simmons

## F33 · Stories for resistant emotions
**Also known as:** Selling Justice: Two-Step Process for Resistant Audiences (Simmons)
**Use when:** A group is labelled unwilling or unmotivated and their resistance protects them from a past unfairness, real or felt.
**Avoid when:** The person shows no conscience at all; no story will reach them and the situation needs rules or control (Simmons).
**Steps:**
1. Reframe: say aloud that their intentions are honourable and that the past inequity was real. Then place their grievance inside a larger story of what everyone stands to gain or lose together.
2. Diagnose the protective emotion and tell the matching story:
   - Cynical: a verifiable story of leaders doing what they said, with the exact order of events, not a promise.
   - Resentful: a story in which making the first peace move is the heroic act.
   - Jealous: a fable showing how internal turf fights hand the prize to an outsider (S13).
   - Hopeless: first restore your own hope, then tell how one small, unplanned act started a larger change.
   - Apathetic: assume over-concern that burned out; acknowledge self-interest and show that working together feels better than standing alone.
   - Greedy: a story about what money and power cannot buy, such as belonging.
3. Leave time: these stories work through later reflection, not instant agreement.
**Common mistakes:** Arguing with the emotion. Telling a cynic an inspiring vision instead of evidence. Pretending the past injustice did not happen.
**Example:** A new operations head inherits a warehouse crew that ignores every improvement plan after two broken bonus promises. He admits the promises were broken and that the crew kept the site running anyway. To the cynics he then tells, with dates, how he let the night shift pick the new scanner model last month and bought the one they chose, even though it was not his choice.
**Sources:** Simmons

## F34 · Name the antagonist
**Also known as:** Inventing the Disease (Antagonist Definition & Evolution) (Maxwell); Color-Coordinated Pre-Launch Education (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Launching a product or a change whose problem the audience ignores, accepts as inevitable, or is embarrassed to admit; reviving a stalled market.
**Avoid when:** You would have to exaggerate the threat or cast a person or competitor as a villain; straw villains can turn into real opponents and stop you learning from the other side (Maxwell).
**Steps:**
1. Diagnose the barrier: does the audience see the problem as an unavoidable part of life, ignore it because nothing could be done, or avoid it from embarrassment or lack of awareness?
2. Name it: give the problem a specific, non-frightening name that describes the exact mechanism your solution addresses. Introduce the name shortly before the solution is available, so people feel able to act rather than stuck.
3. Prove it: pair the data that gives analytical people permission to act with three to five human cases that give the emotional trigger (T045).
4. Make it recognisable: use the same visual identity in the problem education as in the coming solution, so buyers connect the two later (Maxwell).
5. Evolve it: reframe the antagonist every so often, for example from a defect to a manageable condition to a performance issue, so the story never goes stale.
**Common mistakes:** A vague enemy such as inefficiency. Naming the problem long before there is a fix, which breeds helplessness. Data without faces, or faces without data.
**Example:** A payroll software firm finds small business owners shrug off late filing penalties as part of running a company. It names the problem calendar drift: deadlines that move with each new hire's start date. It publishes a short study of penalties paid in its region with four owners describing the letter that arrived, in the same colours as the reminder feature launching the next month.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F35 · Objection handling
**Also known as:** The Dance: Five Tools for Overcoming Objections (Livesay); Feel, Felt, Found (Livesay)
**Use when:** A buyer, investor or stakeholder raises an objection or a hard question during a conversation or Q&A.
**Avoid when:** The objection is a request for a plain fact; answer it directly and confirm (T065).
**Steps:**
1. Pause before answering and restate the concern, which slows you down.
2. Keep your pace calm and your voice lower, so the other person matches your state.
3. Talk less: keep the back-and-forth rhythm instead of launching a long defence.
4. Clarify: check you understood with a question that repeats their concern, then answer.
5. Answer with Feel, Felt, Found: acknowledge how they feel without agreeing the objection is right, say that other clients felt the same at first, and tell the specific story of what those clients found (S09, S11).
6. Close the loop: ask whether that answered the concern and where they want to go next.
**Common mistakes:** Speeding up and talking over the buyer. Answering a question you assumed instead of the one asked. A generic found with no named result. Treating the objection as an attack (see T083).
**Example:** A facilities buyer says the cleaning robot is too expensive for one office floor. The rep pauses, asks whether the concern is the upfront price or the payback time, and hears it is payback. She says a dental group with the same floor size worried about that too, and found the robot paid for itself in eleven months because night cleaners could be moved to restocking. She asks if that addresses it and what the buyer would need to see next.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F36 · Buyer types
**Also known as:** Three Personality Types of Buyers (Livesay); Three Emotional Categories for Buyers: Believer, Builder, and Bean Counter (Livesay)
**Use when:** Preparing a sales or investor conversation with named decision-makers, or adjusting angle during discovery.
**Avoid when:** You have no signals yet; ask discovery questions first rather than guessing a type.
**Steps:**
1. Read communication style from words and surroundings. Feeling people talk about comfort and show family photos: build rapport with warmth and light self-deprecating stories (T090). Information people are punctual and want detail: state the time box, be organised, bring data. Big-picture people talk trends and visionaries: speak about the future and link them to it.
2. Read the decision trigger. Believers buy the vision and the team: paint the future you will build together (T077). Builders want to shape it: invite their input and leave room to co-create (T076). Bean counters need numbers: tick every requirement and compare the return of acting with the cost of doing nothing (T081).
3. Combine the two readings for each person; one buyer can be, for example, a bean counter with a big-picture style.
4. In a group, give each decision-maker one moment addressed to their type.
**Common mistakes:** Asking an information buyer how an offer feels. Burying a big-picture buyer in demographics. Treating the two typologies as one.
**Example:** A solar installer meets a farming couple. The husband asks for kilowatt-hour figures and payback months; the wife talks about leaving the farm to their children. The installer gives him a one-page model of savings against doing nothing, and asks her where on the land she would want the panels so the view from the house stays open.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F37 · Prospect perception ladder
**Also known as:** Invisible to Irresistible Ladder (Livesay)
**Use when:** Planning outreach, judging where you stand with a prospect, or tracking progress across a long sales cycle.
**Avoid when:** The prospect has already asked for terms; move to closing.
**Steps:**
1. Diagnose the rung: invisible (they do not know you exist), insignificant (they have seen you but see a commodity), interesting (they pause and want to hear more), intriguing (they want to know how it would work for them), irresistible (you are the only logical choice).
2. Pick the move for the next rung only. Invisible to insignificant: show up where they already pay attention. Insignificant to interesting: empathy and an insight about their own industry. Interesting to intriguing: future pacing and what-if scenarios (T077), mirroring their values (T079). Intriguing to irresistible: a customised vision plus value beyond ROI (T081).
3. Re-diagnose after each contact and log the rung.
**Common mistakes:** Asking for the close while still invisible or insignificant. Sending the same pitch at every rung.
**Example:** A cybersecurity firm wants to sell to regional credit unions that have never heard of it. It hosts a short monthly briefing on fraud patterns and invites credit union risk officers as guest speakers. After six months several officers ask how the firm's tools would handle their own member data, and only then does the firm propose an assessment.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F38 · Logos, ethos, pathos
**Also known as:** Aristotle's Persuasion Model (Logos, Ethos, Pathos) in Job Interviews (Dolan)
**Use when:** Preparing for a job interview or any situation where you must persuade someone to choose you over people with similar qualifications.
**Avoid when:** The decision is purely technical and the evaluator only scores facts; still keep one ethos story ready.
**Steps:**
1. Logos: list the facts, qualifications and practical steps that show how you solved relevant problems.
2. Ethos: for each trait you would claim, such as integrity or resilience, prepare a short true story where it was tested (T027, S07).
3. Pathos: prepare a mix of work and non-work stories that show personality, passion and fit (F20).
4. In each answer, give the fact, then the story, then link back to the role.
**Common mistakes:** Reciting the CV, which makes you forgettable. Claiming traits without proof. Only work stories, so the interviewer never meets the person.
**Example:** A candidate returning to work after caring for a parent applies to coordinate volunteers at a food bank. She covers the scheduling software she learned. Asked about pressure, she tells how she organised a rota of eleven neighbours to cover her father's care for a winter. The interviewer, who runs a rota of eighty, asks her to start the next month.
**Sources:** Dolan
