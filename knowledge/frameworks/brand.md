# Frameworks — brand

## F39 · Corporate brand story frame
**Also known as:** Corporate Brand Tagline as Story Frame and Filter (Human Energy Model) (Forman); North Star Scenario Process for Brand Story Creation (Forman); Corporate Brand Stories (and Overarching/Signature Brand Story) (Forman)
**Use when:** Building or refreshing a company-level brand story, especially in a technical, commodity or low-trust sector, or when a business is moving away from an old commodity identity.
**Avoid when:** You need a single product campaign; use a product story (S21) instead.
**Steps:**
1. Name the industry's big problem in plain language, the challenge everyone in the sector faces.
2. Poll perceptions: benchmark competitors and interview leaders, industry influencers and the public to find strengths stakeholders already believe are true of the company.
3. Co-create with leaders: gather senior executives to share personal stories about the core domain, draft a guiding future scenario, and test it with a wider leadership group against strategic questions (customers, capabilities, risks, timeline) to choose candidate storylines (S19).
4. Validate outside: test the candidate storylines with opinion leaders, regulators and buyers through surveys and interviews.
5. Write a tagline that gives every story the same moral, and build pillars and toolkits with proof points for each business segment.
6. Fill the frame with concrete stories that have human protagonists in areas where the company is genuinely strong. Vet every claim with experts, strategy, government affairs and legal (T107).
7. Give narrators a voice guide that removes jargon and balances company tone with personal voice. Publish factual policy pages next to the stories.
8. Use the frame as a filter, not a censor: about one story in five may sit outside it, such as heritage or regional stories (Forman).
**Common mistakes:** A tagline promise with no human stories behind it. Claims that no one verified. Rejecting every legacy story that does not fit the new direction. Writing the story only inside headquarters.
**Example:** A water utility seen as a faceless bill-sender names the regional problem as old pipes meeting hotter summers. Its leaders share memories of droughts, choose a storyline about every drop arriving, and test it with mayors and farmers' associations. The tagline frames a series of short films: a leak-detection engineer, a school that cut its usage, a pensioner whose bill fell after a free audit. Each film's figures are checked by the engineering and legal teams before release.
**Sources:** Forman

## F40 · Employee-sourced culture stories
**Also known as:** The Grapevine Model for Values and Vision (Dolan); Topics-and-Questions Approach (I am FedEx) (Forman); Digital and Viral Stories (Forman); Writing Their Own Lottery Ticket (Co-Creation) (Guptara); Three-Day Values Jamming (Forman); Scheduled Grassroots Story Briefings (WOW Stories) (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Launching, refreshing or embedding company values and vision, onboarding acquired staff, or rebuilding trust after layoffs.
**Avoid when:** People around employees visibly fail to live the stated values; fix that first, or advocacy will fall (Dolan).
**Steps:**
1. Personal alignment: each leader decides what each value means to them and crafts a one-to-two-minute personal story, including a time they failed to live it and regretted it (S18).
2. Seed through influencers: gather a cross-section of influential staff from all levels in a safe workshop to mine, tell and record their own stories for each value (F16).
3. Co-create: let employees help write the values or the story itself, for example through a multi-day online jam opened by the CEO, and lift the best posts into company channels. Ownership multiplies when people write it themselves (Guptara).
4. Give everyone simple prompts for short videos or posts: me (a personal passion and how it links to work), my work (a time I went beyond for a customer), my company (what makes me proud), my commitment (how we give back) (Forman; T097).
5. Plan the peer conversations: list the questions teams will ask returning participants and the stories they will tell in reply.
6. Keep a rhythm: read frontline service stories at shift briefings several times a week and follow up with recognition, including a personal note from an executive (Maxwell; S20).
**Common mistakes:** Posters and mugs instead of stories. Leaders telling only success stories. A one-off launch with no recurring collection. Scripted employee videos.
**Example:** A hotel group rewriting its values asks twenty housekeepers, chefs and managers to each pick a value and tell a story about it; one tells of sending a forgotten teddy bear to a child by courier, another of refusing to hide a kitchen mistake. The recordings play at onboarding. Every Monday, Wednesday and Friday, the morning briefing opens with one new guest story, and the general manager writes a note to the employee in it.
**Sources:** Dolan; Forman; Guptara; Maxwell

## F41 · Embedding storytelling across an organization
**Also known as:** The Magic Triangle of Business Storytelling (Callahan); Three Key Factors for Storytelling in Organisational Change (Dolan); Framework for Organizational Storytelling (Forman); Three-Phase Storytelling Workshop for Change (Forman)
**Use when:** Designing a company-wide storytelling or culture-change programme, or diagnosing why corporate stories fail to persuade.
**Avoid when:** Leadership will not own the strategy story or show vulnerability; start with executive coaching instead.
**Steps:**
1. Foundation: make every story credible and fact-checked, and start by listening to employees, customers and communities (F17, T107). Earn trust before trying to persuade.
2. Strategy story: executives co-create and own one explicit story of where the company is going, built on real trade-offs (F08, F09).
3. Story skills: train managers before or alongside their staff over several months, with deliberate practice and coaching (Callahan; F31). Include HR and communications staff (Dolan).
4. Role-modeling: the CEO and top team visit teams to tell stories and to listen to frontline ones (Dolan).
5. Workshop in three phases: passion (participants share personal stories and photos about the human domain the business serves), action (a line manager shows how to adapt the brand story, participants interview real customers and draft stories with proof points), commitment (each writes what they will start, stop and continue, backed by templates and toolkits). Rename the session where the word storytelling meets resistance (Forman).
6. Success-story routines: build rituals such as pre-shift huddles or monthly calls where supervisors ask staff for a recent customer story, and broadcast the best ones (F40).
**Common mistakes:** Training staff while their managers still reward slide-heavy reports. A strategy story written by communications alone. Leaders who tell but never listen. A culture where admitting failure is unsafe.
**Example:** An insurer shifting from selling policies to preventing claims trains its 300 team leaders first, then their staff. Workshops open with participants' own photos of a home that mattered to them, then each team interviews a customer who avoided a flood claim. Branch managers start every Friday call by asking for one prevention story, and the CEO spends a day a month in call centres listening.
**Sources:** Callahan; Dolan; Forman

## F42 · Personal brand through stories (SEA)
**Also known as:** SEA Your Brand (Personal Brand Model) (Dolan)
**Use when:** Choosing which personal stories to tell as a leader, on professional networks, when networking or when repositioning a career.
**Avoid when:** Never skip any of the three elements; dropping one damages the brand (Dolan).
**Steps:**
1. Strategy: decide what you stand for, what you want people to feel about you, and which roles you want next. Select stories that serve that position (F20, S07).
2. Evolution: review your story set when ambitions change, and drop stories that describe who you were long ago rather than who you are becoming (F31).
3. Authenticity: keep only stories that reflect who you really are and that you could live up to every day (T107).
4. Listen for the stories others tell about you when you are not there, and act so the ones you want are true (T093).
**Common mistakes:** Authentic and current stories with no strategy, which do nothing commercially. Strategic stories that are out of date. Strategic, current stories that are not true to you, which destroy credibility.
**Example:** A newly promoted engineering director wants to be known for developing people, not for firefighting. She stops telling the story of the all-night outage she fixed alone and posts instead about the junior engineer she let run the next incident while she watched and stayed silent. Within months, new hires ask to join her team because they heard that story.
**Sources:** Dolan

## F43 · Asymmetric viral story
**Also known as:** Asymmetric / Viral Storytelling Model (Maxwell); Asymmetric / Viral stories (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Marketing an idea or product with little time or budget, and the product is genuinely good.
**Avoid when:** The product is weak; disappointed customers spread bad word of mouth further than happy ones spread good (Maxwell). Also avoid when the audience knows too little about you to fill the gaps; run conventional stories first (T086).
**Steps:**
1. Tie the campaign to a concrete event with a place and a deadline, such as a pop-up or a draw.
2. Confirm the community already knows enough about the brand to complete the story themselves.
3. Attach it to a live topic, mild controversy or competition already in the culture.
4. Strip the story to one intriguing element and repeat it in an incomplete pattern over five or six encounters, so people want to solve it (T071).
5. Pack the message into one visual icon and launch it inside a bounded, self-identified community, such as a neighbourhood, a campus or one online forum, where encounters bounce back and forth.
**Common mistakes:** Explaining everything, which kills the puzzle. Spreading across a broad market where encounters never repeat. No deadline.
**Example:** A new bakery on a university campus has no ad budget. For the week before opening, single paper bags stamped with a gold key appear on library desks, each holding one warm roll and a date. Students post photos asking what the key opens. On opening day the shop door has the same key painted on it, and the line reaches the bus stop.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F44 · Spatial storytelling
**Also known as:** Ron Pompei's 3 Cs & Elemental Spatial Storytelling (Maxwell); Nonverbal / Spatial discovery stories (Maxwell); The Odd-One-Out Discovery Wall (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Designing a store, exhibit, office or digital space that should tell a story without words and turn visitors into people who share it.
**Avoid when:** The space must move people through a fixed transaction as fast as possible, such as a ticket barrier.
**Steps:**
1. Entry: make the first space expansive and uplifting so visitors slow down, look up and breathe.
2. Focal point: hang a warm, circular visual centre near the entrance that invites wandering instead of a march to the counter.
3. Ground: choose tactile, authentic local materials underfoot that make the visitor feel like the explorer.
4. Paths: lay out loose, river-like routes full of small object discoveries that lead to an intimate social hub where people talk.
5. Discovery: build contrasts that let visitors find their own insight, for example a wall of similar objects with one unmarked anomaly to spot. Make it easy to share that moment (T095, T096).
6. Measure time spent, conversation in the hub and shared posts, not only sales per visit.
**Common mistakes:** Putting the till at the centre of attention. Labelling every discovery so nothing is left to find. A hub with nowhere to sit or talk.
**Example:** A bike shop redesign removes the low ceiling at the entrance and hangs a large ring of old wheels lit from within. The concrete floor is replaced with reclaimed track boards. Winding aisles lead past a wall of identical vintage bells where one is a tiny trumpet, and end at a repair bench with stools where customers watch mechanics and swap route tips.
**Sources:** Maxwell
