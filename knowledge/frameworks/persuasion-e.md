# Frameworks — persuasion (F268–F278)

## F268 · Situation-opportunity-resolution pitch
**Also known as:** The Situation-Opportunity-Resolution (SOR) Framework (McKinsey)
**Use when:** The audience already feels its problem and needs a constructive vision of the benefits it can capture, or a good position can be lifted higher. Tension comes from reward, not fear.
**Avoid when:** The audience is in denial or unaware of a serious problem; use F064 to create urgency. SOR is less emotionally intense.
**Steps:**
1. Situation: set out the history and current challenges of the organization or industry.
2. Opportunity: prove a specific avenue is viable and lucrative. Lay out the options without overlap or gaps, prioritize the best subset and state what it takes (resources, time, business-model change).
3. Resolution: prescribe the actions the audience must take to capture it, embedding a short F064 for the client if you shift from industry benchmarks to their case.
**Common mistakes:** Using it on an audience that does not yet see the problem.
**Example:** A children's hospital already ranks first in its region and wants to rise further. The board presentation reviews the hospital's growth and its current strain, with families driving hours for routine follow-ups. It then compares remote follow-up visits, satellite clinics and a research partnership, and ranks remote visits first, with the nurses and software they need. It closes by asking the board to approve the hiring and a spring rollout.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F269 · Pilot-results-scale update
**Also known as:** The Pilot-Results-Scale (PRS) Framework (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Returning to decision-makers after a minimum-viable-product pilot, or turning a recurring status update into a call for action.
**Avoid when:** No pilot has run yet. To win approval for the pilot itself, use F064, with the complication naming the untested hypotheses and the resolution proposing scope and KPIs.
**Steps:**
1. Pilot: remind the audience of the business objective, the hypotheses tested and the agreed success metrics.
2. Results: report plainly which hypotheses held, which failed and what surprised you.
3. Scale: request specific time, money and headcount with a business case stating scope, timeframe, resources, expected return and KPIs.
**Common mistakes:** Sugar-coating failures; ending a status update with no request.
**Example:** A regional airline tested self-service bag drop at one airport. The team recaps the goal of cutting queue time by a third and the two hypotheses tested. Queues did shrink, but staff could not be cut, because older travellers still needed help, and the surprise was that freed-up agents sold more seat upgrades. The team asks for kiosks at three more airports with current staffing, with queue time and upgrade revenue as the KPIs.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F270 · Vulnerability loop
**Also known as:** The Vulnerability Loop (Lazer)
**Use when:** Leading a team, speaking, writing a personal essay or building rapport where polished bragging creates distance.
**Avoid when:** The disclosure does not suit the setting, has no real stakes or change, or is shock for its own sake or a humblebrag.
**Steps:**
1. Person A risks a little by confessing a flaw, error, doubt or painful story.
2. Person B picks up on that signal and answers with a disclosure of their own or an empathetic reply.
3. Person A acknowledges that reciprocation.
4. Both feel deeper trust and closeness.
**Example:** A new engineering manager opens her first meeting by admitting she misjudged a launch date last quarter and lost a weekend to fixing it. A developer says he skipped tests on a patch for the same reason. She thanks him and asks the team how to prevent it.
**Sources:** Lazer

## F271 · Story Spine for rallying a team
**Also known as:** The Story Spine (Lazer)
**Use when:** Leading a pivot, ending a losing streak, introducing disruptive technology or repairing team morale.
**Avoid when:** Leadership would hand all adaptation work to staff without support; the consequences step then backfires.
**Steps:**
1. Once upon a time: bring the team back to the world and beginnings they have in common.
2. Every day: describe the routine and strengths.
3. But one day: introduce the outside disruption.
4. Because of that: lay out the concrete consequences and the joint plan, with leadership sharing the burden.
5. Until finally: reveal the climax and winning future.
6. Optional: and ever since then, the lasting change.
7. Optional: the moral.
**Common mistakes:** Making step 4 a list of demands on employees.
**Example:** A bookshop owner gathers staff after a competitor opens next door. Once upon a time, a neighbourhood read here; every day we hand-picked shelves; but one day a chain arrived. Because of that, she will cover Saturday shifts herself while the team launches a reading club. Until finally the club fills the store each Thursday.
**Sources:** Lazer

## F272 · Compressed hero's journey for pitches
**Also known as:** The Hero's Journey for Sales (Lazer)
**Use when:** Pitching investors or buyers, telling a founder origin, interviewing or writing a career narrative that champions can retell without you. For the full form see F005.
**Avoid when:** There is no human protagonist; a list of product features cannot replace the hero's struggle.
**Steps:**
1. Act 1, ordinary world: humble, relatable roots and a vulnerable flaw.
2. Act 1, call to adventure: the sensory moment that sparked the mission.
3. Act 2, obstacles: the high-stakes hole and rejections that threatened failure.
4. Act 2, mentor or helper: the unexpected ally or break that unlocked progress.
5. Act 3, tests, allies and enemies: grit against rivals and early execution trials.
6. Act 3, transformation: the breakthrough and how hero and customers changed.
**Common mistakes:** Listing product features in place of the hero's struggle.
**Example:** A candidate for director of a city recycling program tells the hiring panel her story. She grew up sorting cans for deposit money and admits she once quit a job in a rage. The call came when a landfill fire shut her school for a week. Two grant bids failed and a council vote went against her until a retired engineer taught her to read waste contracts, and her first pilot street cut its landfill tonnage in half.
**Sources:** Lazer

## F273 · Six-move head-heart-will talk
**Also known as:** The Kung Fu Lesson (James)
**Use when:** You are building a speech or lesson aimed at breaking down resistance and moving people to act.
**Avoid when:** You would cram too many stories into one slot; use only the moves the talk needs.
**Steps:**
1. Surprise: open with a hook that ignites curiosity.
2. Humor: add truth-based or self-effacing humor.
3. Head: anticipate objections and challenge the intellect.
4. Gut: deliver an unvarnished truth.
5. Heart: touch emotion through relatable struggle, not sentiment.
6. Will: close with a call to action.
**Common mistakes:** Forcing all six or a fixed order; preaching instead of letting the story land.
**Example:** A fire marshal tells shop owners to clear their back exits. He opens with "the safest door in your store is the one you never use," jokes about his own cluttered garage, rebuts the cost objection, recounts a blocked-door fire, and asks each owner to move one pallet today.
**Sources:** James

## F274 · Think-feel-do gap mapping
**Also known as:** Think, Feel, Do (and Mind the Gap) (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** Starting a data story, especially when one dataset must persuade different stakeholder groups.
**Avoid when:** Targeting vaguely "the general public".
**Steps:**
1. Segment the audience into specific groups and note where each meets the story.
2. For each, record what they currently think, feel and do, using interviews, surveys or social listening.
3. Define what you want them to think, feel and do.
4. Tabulate current versus target in a gap table.
5. Work backwards to the key messages each segment must receive and believe.
6. Choose guided, linear delivery for a directed action, or exploratory, interactive delivery for an open-ended one.
**Common mistakes:** One message for all segments. Trust in the publisher and strong prior beliefs filter engagement, especially on charged topics.
**Example:** A university library uses its usage data to argue for longer opening hours. It segments students, faculty and the budget committee. Students currently think the library closes too early, feel frustrated and study in cafes; the target is to think late hours are coming, feel heard and fill the evening seats. The budget committee currently thinks hours are a cost, and the target is to see them as a retention tool, so each group gets its own message and format.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F275 · Positioning the business hero
**Also known as:** Four Crucial Functions of a Corporate Hero (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Picking a protagonist for a case study, a brand spokesperson or a personal position within a pitch.
**Avoid when:** Casting a cynical antihero, unless the opponent is so vivid and lethal that the audience forgives the flaws.
**Steps:**
1. Create equality: build a common-denominator bond so listeners feel their fortunes move with the hero's.
2. Stand their ground: show command of a territory of real expertise.
3. Keep the capacity to surprise: present a many-sided human with minor flaws, so the final moment of awareness is prepared.
4. Play the good guy: show a public commitment to the common good and to product quality by hearing customers out and giving them what they ask for.
**Example:** A family-run roofing firm picks its foreman Dalia as spokesperson. She rides along on estimates, explains flashing the way a neighbour would, admits she once misjudged a leak, and ships the exact fix homeowners told her they wanted.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F276 · Naming and evolving the antagonist problem
**Also known as:** Inventing the Disease, Antagonist Definition and Evolution (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Launching a product, pitching change upward or reviving a stagnant market.
**Avoid when:** The enemy would be an exaggerated straw villain, or demonized into pure animus, which cuts contact and blocks learning from the opponent.
**Steps:**
1. Diagnose why the audience ignores the problem: it seems an unavoidable part of life, there was no cure so it was ignored, or they are embarrassed or unaware.
2. Name the enemy at the level your product wins, framed around the exact mechanism it fixes, shortly before launch so it empowers rather than frustrates.
3. Pair statistics with three to five personal cases: data for critical thinkers, stories for emotion.
4. Keep reframing the antagonist over time so the story never goes static.
**Common mistakes:** Naming a problem with no solution in sight; stats with no human cases.
**Example:** An IT lead wants executives to fund single sign-on. Staff accept slow logins as part of office life, so she renames the problem from slow laptops to eleven minutes lost each day to repeated sign-ins, the exact thing the product removes. Her proposal pairs a time study with three short stories: a sales rep who missed a client call, a recruiter locked out during an interview, and a manager who gave up on a report. Next quarter she reframes the enemy as the password-reset tickets clogging the help desk.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F277 · Improv-led co-creation to rescue a failing change
**Also known as:** Mixology (Cody)
**Use when:** A leader-driven program flops at launch because the people expected to run it were never consulted.
**Avoid when:** Senior leaders are unwilling to hear uncomfortable criticism or share ownership of the plan.
**Steps:**
1. Diagnostic improv: welcome participants with live music and have actors play out their frustrations as a light metaphor so they relax and speak candidly.
2. No-negativity brainstorm: ideate on the surfaced pain points, forbidding rejection of any idea as too wild or costly, and write a success outline.
3. Joint roadmap workshop: spend a day with frontline staff and the program's architects turning all feedback into an implementation roadmap.
**Example:** A city library's new self-checkout rule is ignored by branch staff who were never asked. Actors stage a dinner party where the host swaps every guest's plate halfway through the meal, and the laughter opens up the real complaints. Staff then pitch fixes in a session where no idea may be called too costly, and a day later the branch heads and the rule's authors build the revised rollout plan together.
**Sources:** Cody

## F278 · Situations where a story beats facts
**Also known as:** Ten Situations Where Facts Are Inferior to Story (Simmons)
**Use when:** Interpersonal impasses where naked facts or orders trigger denial or malicious obedience.
**Avoid when:** You would state the facts or the refusal first; the story must come before them, or listeners lock into their old defensive reading.
**Steps:**
1. Flat role: share a personal family anecdote before a technical talk to become three-dimensional.
2. Bear-trap question: answer a binary gotcha with a story that honors both sides.
3. Denial: portray an extreme reality with vivid consequences.
4. Delicate warning: tell of a friend at another company to warn or test whether a rule break would be forgiven.
5. Disputing subordinates: tell how two sides see different halves of one reality.
6. Intangible product: narrate a named client's before and after as a demo.
7. A wrong boss: slip in an indirect story about a micromanaged talent.
8. Resisting orders: use a compact metaphor story to redirect priorities.
9. A coming no: tell disaster stories first so the refusal brings relief.
10. Sour mood: drop a curious teaser and a funny self-deprecating story before the hard work.
**Common mistakes:** Facts first, story second.
**Example:** A rooftop-solar installer cannot easily explain what a ten-year payback feels like. Instead she narrates the story of a named customer, Mr. Okafor, whose January bill fell from 310 dollars to 90 after the panels went up in March. She gives the dates and the figures, and the prospect can picture the same change.
**Sources:** Simmons
