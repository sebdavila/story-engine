# Frameworks — persuasion (F235–F245)

## F235 · Investor pitch deck sequence
**Also known as:** 12-Slide Irresistible Investor Pitch Deck (Livesay)
**Use when:** Pitching angels, venture investors or an executive buying committee.
**Avoid when:** Giving a standard B2B sales presentation, where the financial and funding slides should be adapted or dropped.
**Steps:**
1. Title: product name plus a short tagline promising the big idea.
2. Summary: patent status, market size, traction or revenue, endorsements and one strong testimonial.
3. Problem: three distinct customer problems, one image each.
4. Solution: three solutions in the same order as the problems.
5. Product: a demo or diagram of what makes it work better than alternatives.
6. Business model: target customers, market size, revenue model and customer commitments so far.
7. Go-to-market: how you reach the decision-maker and every influencer in the purchase.
8. Financials: a simple three-year forecast of sales, revenue, EBITDA, conversion and monthly burn.
9. Competition: an X/Y grid with you in the upper-right quadrant.
10. Team: photos, titles and relevant experience.
11. Funding request: stage, past funding, exact amount, use of funds and revenue goal timeline.
12. Close: contact details and one or two strong testimonials, ending on an invitation to join; keep backup slides and a printed packet for deep questions.
**Example:** A founder of a rooftop-garden irrigation controller titles her deck Water Less, Grow More. Slide three shows three problems, each with a photo: overwatering, missed rain forecasts, and wasted evenings. Slide four answers them in the same order. Slide nine puts her in the upper-right of the competitor grid, and the close carries a restaurant chef's testimonial and an invitation to tour a rooftop.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F236 · Three kinds of client return
**Also known as:** Three Types of Client Return: ROI, ROA, ROW (Livesay)
**Use when:** Pitching a major campaign, partnership or high-ticket solution and justifying a premium.
**Steps:**
1. Return on investment: show net profit against spend, such as measurable traffic, leads or revenue.
2. Return on attention: show how you cut through clutter to win press buzz and the notice of customers, boards and investors.
3. Return on wisdom: show strategic insight into the customer's journey that makes you an advisor worth a premium.
4. Present all three in the pitch, so the buyer reaches the top rung of F227.
**Example:** A software studio bids to rebuild a regional airline's booking app. It projects the extra direct bookings that would cover its fee within a year, shows how a redesign launch could earn coverage in travel media and the attention of the airline's board, and shares a finding from its user tests: most travellers abandon the app at seat selection, so the airline should rethink how it sells seats. The airline's procurement team ranks the studio as an advisor, not a vendor, and accepts the higher price.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F237 · Story set for unspoken stakeholder questions
**Also known as:** Your 4 Ws (Guptara); The Six Stories You Need to Know How to Tell (Simmons)
**Use when:** Networking, introductions, building trust, or leading and selling where authority, money or data cannot compel belief.
**Avoid when:** The stories would be a front disconnected from how you behave offstage; listeners notice the mismatch.
**Steps:**
1. Who: connect your background and passions in a way that makes you likeable; for Simmons, reveal a flaw, a formative struggle or an admired mentor.
2. Why you are here: state what you gain openly, or prove selfless motives through a concrete sacrifice, so no hidden agenda is imagined.
3. What: a customer-focused hook, shaped as: many customers want a desire but struggle with something; after my credentials, I help them reach a result.
4. How: show how your way works through an experience, an industry contrast or an analogy.
5. Vision (Simmons): paint a sensory picture of a shared future that gives present struggle meaning.
6. Teaching (Simmons): put the how and why of a skill inside a story with realistic complexity and consequences.
7. Values in action (Simmons): show an abstract value through one real event.
8. Pre-empt objections (Simmons): name the audience's private doubts inside a story before any ask.
**Common mistakes:** Treating the what and how as full stories; they can be a hook or analogy that leads into a customer story (see F238).
**Example:** A freelance bookkeeper at a networking breakfast mentions helping her uncle keep the ledger at his tire shop (who). She says many shop owners want a clear view of cash but dread spreadsheets, and after ten years in a finance office she helps them close the month in a day (what). She admits she earns a flat monthly fee, so she wants clients to stay (why). Most bookkeepers wait for year end, she says, while she reconciles weekly (way).
**Sources:** Guptara; Simmons

## F238 · Customer success story
**Also known as:** Sales Story Framework / Model the Sale (Guptara); Success Story Pattern, Meet Bob (Callahan); Six Step Success Stories (Hauge)
**Use when:** Selling or pitching with a story of a customer who resembles the prospect, rather than a company-centred case study.
**Avoid when:** The prospect answers with their own problem story; stop and move to planning with them.
**Steps:**
1. Introduce one named customer like the listener, with a role and, if needed, a declared pseudonym, and what they wanted (Setup).
2. Show a concrete painful moment of the problem; Hauge adds a wake-up call, a measurable goal, doubts and failed earlier fixes (Crisis).
3. Show how they met you, with you cast as guide, not hero.
4. Show the plan made together and how objections were met; Hauge adds the first reaction and two or three distinct actions (Pursuit).
5. Show what nearly went wrong without help; Hauge adds setbacks, unsupportive people and doubts overcome (Conflict).
6. Show the peak moment of success (Climax), then the outer gain and the inner relief, the opposite of step 2.
7. Hauge adds a later after picture with ripple effects on others (Aftermath).
8. Leave the final appeal unspoken so the listener infers it.
**Common mistakes:** Telling it from the company's view or as plain problem, solution, outcome.
**Example:** A kitchen-fitter tells a homeowner about Dana, who stopped cooking when her cabinets jammed. A neighbour recommended the firm, they planned the layout together, and a flood nearly ruined the plan. Months later Dana hosted her first dinner and said nothing about the price.
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan; Hauge

## F239 · Strategy story: before, change, now, next
**Also known as:** The X Story / Four Ps (Guptara); Clarity Story Pattern, Strategy Stories, Focus Stories, Costanza Manoeuvre (Callahan)
**Use when:** Explaining a strategy, change, tough decision such as layoffs, or a full pitch, so people do not invent rumors.
**Avoid when:** You would put the outcomes first, which turns the story into an argument; do not outsource the story to a strategy team or agency.
**Steps:**
1. Test each strategic choice by stating its opposite; if no one would choose the opposite, the choice is a platitude (Costanza).
2. Build in order: define the present decision, then what changed, then how things were, then the future.
3. Tell it as: in the past; then something happened; so now; in the future.
4. Anchor each part with a specific first-hand moment; for the future, use a pocket of the business where it already exists or an Imagine scenario.
5. Pick one through-line and address any counter-stories.
6. For a Focus Story, narrow to one priority theme seen in frontline work.
**Common mistakes:** Staying abstract so frontline staff find it too meta.
**Example:** A hospital chief explains moving outpatient care to clinics. Before, patients crowded the main building. Then a mother waited six hours with a feverish child. So clinics now open in three suburbs. In the future, the first suburb clinic already sees most cases in under an hour.
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan

## F240 · Turn strategy into narrative before rollout
**Also known as:** The Four Steps of Strategy (Guptara)
**Use when:** Developing and rolling out strategy across an organization.
**Steps:**
1. Develop the strategy through rational analysis.
2. Formulate it: turn the analysis into a story structure people can remember and act on (see F239).
3. Articulate it to leaders and staff.
4. Implement and monitor on the frontline.
**Common mistakes:** Jumping from development straight to a slide deck and skipping formulation.
**Example:** A logistics firm finishes its plan to cut depots from nine to five. Before the town hall, the COO drafts the story of why, then presents it, instead of reading the analysis slides.
**Sources:** Guptara

## F241 · Let the audience reach the point
**Also known as:** Three Approaches to Communicating a Message / Two Plus Two (Guptara); Curiosity-Based Questions (James)
**Use when:** Raw data would disorient listeners and a direct assertion would invite argument, or when you want listeners to change.
**Avoid when:** You want diverse views to challenge yours before a big decision, where raw facts suit; for unfamiliar technical subjects some direct explanation is still needed.
**Steps:**
1. Choose the approach: give only the dots (facts), connect them yourself (state the conclusion), or help them connect them.
2. Arrange story elements in deliberate order so the audience fills the gap and owns the conclusion.
3. Ask yourself how listeners can discover the truth, not how you can explain it.
4. Ask how they can experience it, not how you can clarify it.
5. Ask how you can equip them to teach it to others.
**Common mistakes:** Telling the feeling outright instead of showing the cue.
**Example:** To persuade a team that meetings run long, a manager does not say so. She shows a calendar where Tuesday's agenda has seven items and a clock reading 6 p.m. beside an empty office.
**Sources:** Guptara; James

## F242 · Depth levels of influence
**Also known as:** The Hierarchy of Influence (Guptara)
**Use when:** Facts are failing to change behavior or are provoking defensiveness.
**Steps:**
1. List the levels from the top: behavior, belief, feeling, experience or experienced story, sense-making narrative.
2. Spot where your effort sits; arguing with facts targets belief and can make people feel foolish.
3. Aim deeper: use a story to shift feelings through experience, and a sense-making narrative to change how experiences are read.
4. Let feelings realign belief and behavior.
**Common mistakes:** Aiming only at the behavior tip.
**Example:** A woman wants her elderly father to stop driving at night. Quoting crash rates only makes him argue that he has driven for fifty years. She stops debating and tells him about the evening she sat beside him and he missed a cyclist by a hand's width, and how her hands shook for an hour afterwards. His fear for others shifts first, and a week later he hands her the keys after dark.
**Sources:** Guptara

## F243 · Sticky, retellable stories
**Also known as:** SUCCESs (Guptara; Sandler); Characteristics of a Retold Story (Callahan); Blank-Speech-Bubble Cartoons and Rotating Artworks (Callahan)
**Use when:** A message must survive and spread, such as cultural norms, safety lessons or strategy across a company.
**Avoid when:** The story has narrow technical value only; it will stay within a niche unless tied to a broad life theme. An idea need not hold all six factors.
**Steps:**
1. Make it simple, unexpected, concrete, credible and emotional, and carry all five in a story.
2. Trigger a high-arousal emotion (awe, excitement, amusement, anger, anxiety) or a moral violation.
3. Give social currency to teller and listener, such as insider how-to-get-ahead stories.
4. Add triggers: recurring cues, leaders' remarkable actions, artworks; hang cartoons of real stories with blank speech bubbles and no plaque so people ask for the story.
5. Make it public in inductions, huddles and town halls.
6. Show practical action tied to a universal theme, and reinforce an identity people are proud of.
**Example:** A senior producer at a public radio station tells each new intern how she got her first on-air slot: she pitched a story to the host in the thirty seconds of a stuck elevator. The tale is funny, gives interns an insider route to get ahead, and ties to a theme anyone recognizes, being ready when luck arrives. It is told at every intern induction, and the station hangs a cartoon of the elevator with an empty speech bubble by the coffee machine, so newcomers ask who said what.
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan; Sandler

## F244 · Why stories engage the brain (EASY)
**Also known as:** EASY, The Science of Story (Guptara)
**Use when:** Designing communication to hold attention, build empathy and move people without draining their decision energy.
**Avoid when:** A simple anecdote suffices; not every story needs to stir yearning, effortless sensory appeal can carry a point.
**Steps:**
1. Effortless: let the story spare listeners the micro-decisions of weighing raw data.
2. Appeal: engage many brain areas, which brings attention and empathy, where facts engage only language areas.
3. Senses: add sensory detail, because the brain simulates imagined events like real ones.
4. Yearning: show human struggle with cause and effect, so listeners feel meaning and a wish to contribute.
**Example:** A charity fundraiser describes the smell of damp bedding and the sound of rain on a tin roof in a family's shelter before mentioning any number.
**Sources:** Guptara

## F245 · Start with why
**Also known as:** Start with Why (Kamath); Sinek's Golden Circle and Noble Purpose (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Opening a start-up pitch, writing a brand strategy or About page, or escaping a price war.
**Avoid when:** The purpose claim cannot be backed with operational proof; greenwashing and woke-washing ring hollow.
**Steps:**
1. Why: state the belief or purpose beyond profit, and how it improves customers' lives.
2. How: name the distinctive values, behaviors and process that live out the why.
3. What: present the products and features as rational proof, only after the need has been accepted.
**Common mistakes:** Overpromising distant social or environmental claims.
**Example:** A family bakery rewrites its About page. It opens with a belief: a town's bread should be made in that town, by people who live there. It then describes how it buys flour from two nearby mills and trains apprentices from the local school. Only at the end does it list its loaves, prices and opening hours.
**Sources:** Kamath; Quesenberry
