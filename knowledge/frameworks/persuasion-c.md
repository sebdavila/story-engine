# Frameworks — persuasion (F246–F256)

## F246 · Emotional and rational routes (ELM)
**Also known as:** Elaboration Likelihood Model and Combined Appeal (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Deciding how much rational proof versus emotion a story should carry.
**Avoid when:** The brand is unfamiliar and the piece is emotion only; viewers may enjoy it and forget who ran it.
**Steps:**
1. Use the peripheral route (emotion, drama, visuals, music) to earn notice and affection if the audience cares little or is guarded.
2. Use the central route (benefits, proof, claims) when people are actively comparing options.
3. For new or lesser-known brands, combine emotional narrative with a clear product fit.
4. Reserve pure-emotion spots for icons with near-universal awareness.
**Example:** A new local hearing clinic films a grandmother laughing as she finally catches her grandson's joke at dinner, aimed at adult children who scroll past health ads. Because few people know the clinic's name, the last seconds add its name, a free hearing test and a 30-day trial. The landing page then lists models and prices for visitors already comparing clinics.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F247 · Plan book and pitch as five acts
**Also known as:** Five-Act IMC Plan Book and Pitch Framework (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Writing a marketing plan or presenting strategy and creative to clients or executives.
**Avoid when:** A live pitch would carry dense text or hand out printouts before you present; save leave-behinds for after.
**Steps:**
1. Act 1, situation: executive summary, brand context, category, competitor audit, target persona.
2. Act 2, conflict and stakes: research insights, SWOT, problems and opportunities, SMART objectives.
3. Act 3, strategic reveal: creative brief, the single most persuasive idea, big idea and tagline.
4. Act 4, channel execution: integrated strategy, executions per channel, PR, social and direct.
5. Act 5, resolution: media mix, schedule, budget, evaluation plan showing measurable return.
**Common mistakes:** Labelling written plan sections as acts; keep functional titles and let a live pitch carry the emotional build.
**Example:** A pitch to a bike-share operator opens on declining weekday rides and a rival's expansion, builds to a one-line idea about the last mile, then shows station, social and radio executions, ending on a budget table with ride targets.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F248 · Attention-to-Connection Sequence
**Also known as:** Gap Connection Model (Paulsen)
**Use when:** Structuring pitches, marketing journeys or onboarding flows.
**Avoid when:** The draft rests on the creator's optimism; test it from the recipient's sceptical first-person view.
**Steps:**
1. Attention: a short, intriguing hook that breaks through noise.
2. Simple message: one core proposition grasped at once.
3. Promise: the specific value or change offered.
4. Brand: credibility and authenticity behind the promise.
5. Connection: a concrete shared next step that cements the relationship.
**Example:** A fitness-tracker company welcomes new users with a one-line push alert that names their step goal (attention) and a single screen saying the watch builds a weekly plan in two minutes (message and promise). A line below cites a clinic study on adherence (brand). The flow ends by asking the user to pick the first walk time and sync it to their calendar (connection).
**Sources:** Paulsen

## F249 · Give Before You Ask
**Also known as:** Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook (Vaynerchuk); Like, Comment, Share, Buy (Creek); Priming vs. Flagship Videos (Creek); Status-Enhancing Framing (Creek)
**Use when:** Running ongoing social marketing where cold pitches get ignored or buried.
**Avoid when:** You would sell before earning emotional buy-in, or draw non-buying followers with free giveaways. There is no fixed jab-to-ask ratio; high-equity brands need fewer jabs than start-ups.
**Steps:**
1. Post repeated no-pitch value: stories, jokes, tips, empathy.
2. Read the engagement to learn what your audience values.
3. Reply to comments to humanize the brand.
4. Earn likes and comments with story value, then earn shares by framing the piece so the sharer looks in-the-know without social risk.
5. Build background knowledge with steady priming videos before a flagship piece.
6. Make one clear ask (see F250) or retarget those who engaged, then return to giving.
**Example:** A neighbourhood pharmacy posts a short video on reading a prescription label, a joke about pill-organizer chaos and a reply to every comment for weeks. Reading the comments, staff see that most questions are about seasonal allergies. The pharmacy then posts one clear offer, a free allergy consultation this Saturday, and goes back to the tips.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk; Creek

## F250 · Clean Conversion Ask
**Also known as:** Three Characteristics of Perfect Right Hooks, one CTA per post, deep links, consistent voice (Vaynerchuk)
**Use when:** Writing any promotional post or call to action.
**Avoid when:** No goodwill has been built first; a flawless ask still fails (see F249).
**Steps:**
1. Make one single-minded, unmistakable call to action; split extra questions into other posts.
2. Check image framing, text legibility and landing pages on a phone as well as a desktop.
3. Follow the platform's native visual style, voice and linking mechanics.
4. Deep-link to the exact product, signup or promo page, not the homepage.
5. Keep slang, humor and tone in the ask itself.
**Example:** A community theatre's Instagram story for its spring musical asks for one thing: pick a Friday seat. The team previews it on an old phone to make sure the countdown sticker and the pun-heavy caption, in the theatre's usual voice, are not cut off. The link sticker opens the seat map for Friday's show, not the theatre's home page.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk

## F251 · High-arousal emotions for sharing
**Also known as:** 17 Primary Emotions and Sharing Hierarchy, Strong-Negative vs. Strong-Positive (Creek); Activating-Emotion Virality Model (Gottschall)
**Use when:** Checking a concept's emotional arc, diagnosing low interaction on high-view videos, or designing word-of-mouth campaigns.
**Avoid when:** The aim is calm content such as meditation; it travels by later offline referral, not feed sharing. Heavy reliance on arousing negativity can slide into panic or misinformation.
**Steps:**
1. Aim for at least one top-tier emotion: hilarity, awe, anger, sadness or exhilaration. Anger and sadness spike fast and fade fast; the positive ones build and last.
2. Or layer middle-tier emotions: inspiration, surprise, happiness, shock.
3. Avoid disgust, irritation, astonishment and amusement, which people feel but will not post.
4. Eliminate calmness, discomfort, frustration and boredom.
5. Frame the stakes as happening now, and give an explicit reason or duty to share.
6. Add a defense against rival stories.
**Example:** A flood-relief clip builds from a quiet street, to a neighbour's rescue, to a child's wave, aiming at awe and exhilaration, and ends with a plain line: send this to someone who would help.
**Sources:** Creek; Gottschall

## F252 · Neurochemical Hook Balance
**Also known as:** The Neurotransmitter Balance (Kamath)
**Use when:** Writing hooks, choosing examples and setting tone in high-stakes or dry corporate settings.
**Avoid when:** Humor would not suit the gravity of the subject.
**Steps:**
1. Empathy first: centre one relatable person's struggle to earn trust.
2. Reward attention: open information gaps, build anticipation, resolve with clear answers.
3. Lower resistance: speak at an unhurried pace and add well-timed humor.
**Example:** A security vendor pitching a bank's board opens on Dev, a payroll clerk who opened a fake invoice the week before his daughter's wedding. It holds back what the attachment did until the third slide, then shows it quietly copied every salary file. The presenter speaks slowly, and a dry line about Dev now refusing to open his own birthday cards lets the room breathe.
**Sources:** Kamath

## F253 · Knowledge Pyramid
**Also known as:** The Knowledge Pyramid (Kamath)
**Use when:** Pitching a radical idea that meets skepticism or bias.
**Avoid when:** The links are not real; a manufactured chain of progress weakens credibility.
**Steps:**
1. State the long-held conventional wisdom on solving the problem.
2. Show field evidence of where it failed in practice.
3. Cite an authoritative shift that sharpened the problem definition, while the problem persists.
4. Present your solution as the modest next step.
**Example:** A nurse-engineer pitching a hospital board on patient falls starts with the long-held view that more bed alarms prevent them. She then shows ward audits where units with the most alarms still had the most falls, because staff had learned to tune the noise out. She cites a national safety body that now names alarm fatigue as the core problem, while falls continue. Her proposal, a quiet sensor paired with hourly rounding on one ward, comes last as the modest next step.
**Sources:** Kamath

## F254 · Spine Plus Pattern Break
**Also known as:** Three-Pillar Spine and Pattern-Break Method, Lead with your strongest asset, Real-time risky demo (Kamath)
**Use when:** Pitching in crowded competitions or demo days where decks look alike.
**Avoid when:** The twist would replace problem, solution or path to market, or exist for shock without proof and smooth flow.
**Steps:**
1. Keep problem, solution and path to market intact.
2. Open with uncontentious, emotive detail to earn early trust.
3. Spot the predictable transition, especially solution to path to market, and jump from A to C so listeners ask how you get back to B.
4. Resolve with proof or a delayed reveal that supports your core value.
5. Options: open with the team or business model if that is your edge; run a live time-bound demo, or hold the key reveal until after tasting.
**Example:** A founder of a flood-sensor start-up at a city-tech demo day opens on a corner-shop owner sweeping water out the door after every storm, a picture no judge would dispute. She covers the problem and the sensor, then skips the expected rollout slide and says three cities already use the service without buying a single device. The judges want to know how, and she steps back to the path to market: an insurer pays for the sensors because each early warning cuts its claims, and she shows the signed pilot. The twist sharpens her core value instead of replacing problem, solution or path to market.
**Sources:** Kamath

## F255 · Story before argument
**Also known as:** Elephant and Rider (Maestas); Influence Story Pattern and Influence Stories (Callahan); Story-Wrapped Data Formula (Hall)
**Use when:** Changing habits, answering skeptics, countering cynical rumors, or delivering metrics to analytical audiences.
**Avoid when:** Emotion would stand alone; mindless emotion without a rational case cannot sustain action. If the rumor is true, first change behavior.
**Steps:**
1. Open with a relevance statement or name the objection (you might be thinking), or with a human story or humor aimed at emotion.
2. Tell a concrete story; to stop a bad idea, tell a negative story, then a positive one showing a working alternative.
3. For data, tell the opening story first, pause it for statistics and accolades, then return to the characters.
4. Give the argument, logic and steps once defenses are down.
5. End on the point, or for the data version, on the story's new normal so the last impression is emotional.
**Example:** A school superintendent faces a board that suspects the new reading program is another fad. She names that doubt, then tells of a seventh-grader who used to hide his book under the desk. She pauses the story for the numbers, a 14-point gain across the district, explains how the daily small-group sessions work, and returns to the boy now reading aloud to his little sister at night.
**Sources:** Maestas; Callahan; Hall

## F256 · Logos, ethos, pathos in interviews
**Also known as:** Aristotle's Persuasion Model in Job Interviews (Dolan)
**Use when:** Answering behavioural interview questions, especially with CV gaps or competitors holding the same qualifications.
**Avoid when:** You rely on logos alone by reciting CV facts; you will sound bland and not fit the culture.
**Steps:**
1. Logos: supply facts, credentials and the practical steps that show how you solved the problem.
2. Ethos: prove trustworthiness and values with specific true stories, not generic claims.
3. Pathos: share work and non-work stories that show personality, passion and fit.
**Example:** A candidate without a degree applies for a logistics analyst role. For logos she walks through how she rebuilt a depot schedule in Excel and cut late deliveries. For ethos she tells of staying overnight to fix a mislabeled pallet before a customer noticed. For pathos she mentions coaching her sister's football team, which shows how she treats people under pressure.
**Sources:** Dolan
