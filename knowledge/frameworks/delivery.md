# Frameworks — delivery

## F27 · Presentation phases from opening to close
**Also known as:** The five presentation phases (Parikh)
**Use when:** Planning the running order of any live or virtual presentation, keynote or client meeting.
**Avoid when:** The format is a short conversational exchange with no presenting role; use a single story pattern instead (F01).
**Steps:**
1. Opening, first 30 seconds: start with a hook, not thanks or logistics. Pick one of eight hook types: a provocative statement, a question, an authoritative quotation, a surprising statistic, a curiosity build-up (T071), a what-if scenario, an imagine scenario, or a short personal story (T048).
2. Speaker introduction: give credentials that are objective (evidence, not adjectives), tangible (years, numbers, named projects), relevant to this audience, and concise, or tell them as a brief career story. Skip this phase when the room already knows you, unless one past experience they do not know about is directly relevant (T049).
3. Topic introduction: say what the audience gets and which of their problems the session solves.
4. Main body: deliver the structure you chose (for example F10 or F12), with analogies, short stories, sensory detail and planned checkpoints where you confirm agreement (T072).
5. Close: end with one of three moves: a summary with a fresh angle, a single sentence the audience should keep, or a concrete call to action with dates and owners (T066).
**Common mistakes:** Spending the peak-attention seconds on thanks and housekeeping. Credentials that sound like boasting or are irrelevant to the room. Ending on an open request for questions instead of a deliberate final line.
**Example:** A consultant pitching a supply-chain review to a food distributor opens by asking how many trucks left half empty last week. She mentions that her team cut empty miles by a fifth for two regional grocers. She states that the session will show where the distributor loses money between depot and store, walks through three findings with a yes-check after each, and closes by asking the operations director to share route data by the fifteenth.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F28 · Oral delivery channels
**Also known as:** Oral Language Delivery Channels (Simmons)
**Use when:** Rehearsing how to tell a story aloud so that body, voice and timing carry its emotion as much as the words do.
**Avoid when:** You are already on stage; do not try to steer all channels consciously while speaking.
**Steps:**
1. Gesture: see the scene in your mind first, then use small hand movements to place or outline objects so listeners look at the scene, not your hands.
2. Face: recall the real feeling of the moment before you tell it, so your expression shows it without explanation (T051).
3. Body: change posture or centre of gravity to switch between characters instead of repeating who said what, and use left, centre and right positions to mark sequence (T052).
4. Senses: name or imitate one familiar sound, smell or taste that triggers a physical reaction in listeners (T101).
5. Telling detail: keep one specific, slightly off-topic fact that makes the memory feel real (T102).
6. Timing: hold a two-second silence at the emotional high point and before the punchline so listeners arrive there themselves (T053).
7. Tone: before speaking, clear any resentment, arrogance or neediness about the outcome, because tone overrides words and gestures (T054).
8. Rehearse one or two channels per session (T059). On the day, forget the list and attend to the listener (T061).
**Common mistakes:** Choreographed gestures that look rehearsed. Rushing through silences. Polishing the words while the tone still signals that you need the deal.
**Example:** A plant manager rehearses the story of the morning a forklift alarm saved a new hire. In one session he practises only the beep of the reversing truck and a two-second pause after it. In the next he practises shifting his weight to play the veteran driver. At the safety briefing he thinks only about the forty faces in front of him, and the pause lands on its own.
**Sources:** Simmons

## F29 · Battle plan for a high-stakes presentation
**Also known as:** Sun-tzu's Five Fundamental Elements for Presentations (Maxwell); Time-of-Day Pacing Adjustment (Maxwell)
**Use when:** Preparing an in-person pitch or talk to a skeptical board, conference or prospect where one meeting decides the outcome.
**Avoid when:** The exchange is informal and recurring; F31 and the techniques it cites cover everyday telling.
**Steps:**
1. Conviction: before you enter, confirm that you believe the message is true and care about it. If you cannot, fix the message, not the delivery.
2. Timing: check the hour of the meeting. Morning: bring dense, substantiating detail. Early afternoon (roughly two to four): keep segments under five minutes, hand off between speakers often, keep the lights up. End of day: cut the pitch to its core and close fast.
3. Terrain: arrive about fifteen minutes early, test sound, screen and chairs, and plan to speak away from the lectern. Step forward when talking about the future, back for the past, and stand still for the present (T055).
4. Presence: aim between timid and overbearing, with a calm voice and focused energy (T054, T060).
5. Game plan: rehearse the sequence aloud about five times, at least once to a live listener, without memorising wording (T058, T059). Cut any phrase you stumble on. End with one simple, doable call to action.
**Common mistakes:** Giving the same deck at 9 a.m. and 4 p.m. Discovering the projector problem in front of the client. Memorising a script that breaks at the first interruption. Ending without an ask.
**Example:** A startup gets a 4:30 p.m. slot with a hospital purchasing committee. The founder cuts her twenty slides to six, rehearses the order aloud five times, once in front of her accountant, and arrives early to move the screen away from the window. She closes by asking for a three-month pilot on one ward.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F30 · Emotional needs of a meeting audience
**Also known as:** Eight Basic Human Emotional Needs (Edwards)
**Use when:** Designing or running any meeting, pitch or presentation where you need agreement, and checking afterwards why one went well or badly.
**Avoid when:** Never skip it entirely; for a short email, cover at least alignment, ownership and safety.
**Steps:** Plan one concrete move for each need.
1. Alignment: start from where the audience is, including pressures they have not mentioned, and confirm agreement at each transition (T072).
2. Emotional connection: use everyday words (T104) and place a tension-resolution sentence at each turning point (T014).
3. Structure: show an agenda that marks finished and upcoming sections, colour-code sections, and hand out an agenda and team bios (T031).
4. Recognition: make the audience the hero (F21) and treat their domain knowledge as greater than yours.
5. Ownership: leave one part of the proposal deliberately open so they can shape it (T076).
6. Stimulus: vary pace, for example one long slide followed by several quick ones, break the template now and then, and change where you stand.
7. Safety: add explicit reassurance about risk for cautious listeners (T074).
8. Support: give implementation steps, dates and early wins that make them look right to their own bosses.
**Common mistakes:** Presenting a sealed, finished idea that leaves nothing to contribute. Treating the audience as students rather than experts. Forgetting the risk-averse person in the room.
**Example:** An agency presenting a loyalty programme to a pharmacy chain opens by acknowledging the chain's recent staff cuts. It shows a three-part agenda that ticks off as it goes, presents the mechanics with two reward tiers finished and a third left blank for the client's category managers to design, and states that the pilot can be switched off in any store within a day. It ends with a four-week launch calendar.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F31 · Storytelling habit and story lifecycle
**Also known as:** The Business Storytelling Habit Loop (Callahan); The Story Mastery Process / Four Disciplines of Story Mastery (Callahan); The Four Stages of Competence (Guptara)
**Use when:** Building storytelling into your everyday speech at work, or coaching someone who knows the theory but still argues in opinions.
**Avoid when:** You need a story for one specific event tomorrow; use F16 to find it and a structure such as F01 to shape it.
**Steps:**
1. Cue: notice the moment you are about to state an opinion or a list, such as starting with I think or there are three points.
2. Routine: tell a short story from your repertoire that shows the point instead.
3. Reward: watch the reaction in the room and listen for people retelling the story later.
4. Run the lifecycle continuously. Discover new stories from everyday moments and from others (F16, F17). Remember them with a tagged bank and spaced retelling (T019, T020). Share them in conversation with a relevance line first (T006). Refresh: retire a story when it bores you, when the same people have heard it, or when it starts to project an identity you do not want.
5. Expect a dip: after learning the theory you will feel less fluent for a while. That is the conscious-incompetence stage and a sign of progress (Guptara); the habit can take up to a year (Callahan).
**Common mistakes:** Working only on performance while never finding new stories or dropping stale ones. Quitting during the confidence dip. Telling one favourite story to the same team every quarter.
**Example:** A finance director notices he begins every budget meeting with three bullet points. He starts keeping a notes file of small moments, such as the intern who found a duplicate invoice by reading it aloud. At the next meeting he tells that story instead of a point about controls. When a colleague mentions it a week later, he logs it as a keeper; when he catches himself telling it a fourth time to the same group, he rests it.
**Sources:** Callahan; Guptara
