# Frameworks — review

## F45 · Stickiness and retelling checklist
**Also known as:** SUCCESs (Guptara, after Heath); Characteristics of a Retold Story (Callahan, after Berger)
**Use when:** Reviewing a draft that must be remembered and passed on: culture stories, safety lessons, strategy messages, campaigns.
**Avoid when:** The message is a one-off instruction to a single person; a plain request is enough.
**Steps:**
1. Simple: state the core in one sentence (T007). If you cannot, cut.
2. Unexpected: find the beat that breaks the listener's assumption (T012); add one if missing.
3. Concrete: replace abstractions with people, actions and objects (T101, T102).
4. Credible: check that a detail, source or witness makes it believable.
5. Emotional: name the emotion the listener should feel. For retelling, prefer high-arousal emotions (awe, amusement, anxiety, anger) or a moral line being crossed.
6. Social currency: ask whether telling it makes the teller look good or knowing.
7. Practical value: confirm it shows an action that solves a problem, linked to a broad life theme (family, health, career) so it spreads beyond specialists.
8. Triggers: tie it to something people see often (a ritual, an object, a place) (T095, T096).
9. Public: plan where it will be told so others can copy it (inductions, huddles, town halls).
10. Identity: check it reflects an identity the audience is proud of.
11. Confirm the story itself carries the other factors; if the draft is a list of facts, rebuild it as a story (F02).
**Common mistakes:** Narrow technical lessons with no broader theme; calm, low-arousal stories expected to spread; messages with no recurring trigger.
**Example:** A plant wants operators to report near misses. The draft is a policy summary. Reviewed against the list, it becomes the story of a forklift driver who stopped a line because a pallet looked off by a hand's width and found a cracked beam above it. The plant paints a hand outline on each loading bay, so the story is retold every shift.
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan

## F46 · Audience-action audit
**Also known as:** Do Think Feel; The Slide/Paragraph Where They... (Edwards)
**Use when:** Reviewing your own deck or document, or giving feedback on a colleague's or a multi-author draft.
**Avoid when:** There is no draft yet; write the audience outcome first instead (T022).
**Steps:**
1. Frame the review as a check against a model, not a judgement of the author.
2. For each slide or paragraph, complete the sentence "this is where they..." with what the audience should do, think or feel. Never accept "where we...".
3. If the sentence cannot be completed, cut the slide or paragraph or bring in someone to clarify its value.
4. Compare the content against the agreed sentence and let the author fix the mismatches.
5. Read the sentences in order; they should form the audience's journey toward the objective.
**Common mistakes:** Slides that only describe the seller; vague answers like "where they learn about us"; the reviewer rewriting instead of asking.
**Example:** A team audits a twelve-slide partnership proposal. Slide four becomes "where they see their returns rate is double the sector average"; slide seven only lists the team's awards and cannot be completed, so it goes. The author rewrites slide nine herself once its sentence reads "where they feel safe about data handling".
**Sources:** Edwards
