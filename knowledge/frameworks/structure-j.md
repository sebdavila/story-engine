# Frameworks — structure (F100–F104)

## F100 · Three-layer story blueprint
**Also known as:** Story blueprint / Pyramid hierarchy of information (Parikh); One-minute elevator scaling (Parikh); Key-argument executive summary (Parikh)
**Use when:** Structuring a business story after audience profiling and problem framing, before writing documents or slides.
**Steps:**
1. Layer 1: state the overall recommendation or call to action.
2. Layer 2, the key argument: a horizontal deductive chain of 3–5 premises linking the audience's starting reality to the recommendation. For status reports use an inductive set of KPI summary statements.
3. Layer 3: under each premise, attach a vertical inductive set of 3–5 supporting points from audience profiling, co-creation priorities and analysis.
4. Translate the same page by format. Elevator: narrate layers 1 and 2 only. Report: layer 2 becomes the executive summary, one paragraph per premise, ending with the recommendation; chapter headings come from layer 2 and sub-sections from layer 3. Presentation: layer 2 becomes agenda items and full-sentence slide titles, layer 3 the slide body.
**Common mistakes:** Keeping a deductive backbone when sections do not depend on each other. Parikh's case is a monthly status deck where each manager presents a KPI area and may leave early; use an inductive key argument there.
**Example:** A bakery owner proposes buying a second oven. Layer 1: buy it. Layer 2: demand outstrips capacity, the current oven caps output, renting costs more over a year, the new oven pays back in 14 months. Layer 3: order logs, bake-time data and quotes under each. In an elevator she says only layers 1 and 2.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F101 · Audience-first story creation
**Also known as:** Five-Step Process for Creating a Story (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** Building a story for a defined audience and goal. Non-linear: you can start from the problem, goal or character.
**Steps:**
1. Research the audience: who they are, stated and unstated wants, common ground, doubts, fears and misperceptions, baseline knowledge and jargon, mood and culture.
2. Define the goal: a concrete action such as buying or changing behaviour, or a softer one such as breaking the ice or building trust.
3. Uncover the challenges blocking that goal, often by researching or interviewing characters.
4. Choose a relatable character close to the audience's situation or sharing a universal struggle.
5. Deliver a satisfying resolution: goal achieved, goal re-evaluated with growth, or goal thwarted with a clear lesson.
**Common mistakes:** Open-ended art-house endings with no resolution lose general audiences. If the story has no resolution, pick another story.
**Example:** A city library wants teens to join a coding club. The writer asks teens what keeps them away (embarrassment at being a beginner), picks a sixteen-year-old who nearly skipped the first session, and ends with her fixing a broken game for a friend.
**Sources:** Biesenbach

## F102 · Spark-to-draft writing sequence
**Also known as:** The Write Right 11-Step Story-Writing Progression, A Better Recipe (Haven)
**Use when:** Taking any spark to a first draft. Beginners can phase in the advanced steps.
**Steps:**
1. Plant a seed: a situation or a what-if, not a plot.
2. Create the main character, the one who risks losing most, using the five layers (F103).
3. Define the story question (the goal as a yes/no question) and the human theme.
4. Plan structure: derive the ending from the goal, order obstacles, raise jeopardy.
5. Define supporting characters: helpers and a stronger antagonist.
6. Choose viewpoint and narrator.
7. Map scenes and sequels: one card per scene with purpose, setting, cast, conflict, resolution and summary; the sequel goes on the back.
8. Make it real and visual: say it aloud repeatedly until the characters seem like relatives and the settings like your own bedroom.
9. Trim openings that begin too soon and endings that run too long: scenes before the inciting incident and fluff after the climax, apart from the closing sequel.
10. Record the first draft by speaking it in two or three passionate passes, ignoring grammar.
11. Write the story: copy out your best pass one sentence at a time, strengthening verbs, details and dialogue as you go.
**Common mistakes:** Skipping the recording step increases revision work.
**Example:** Seed: what if a night-shift janitor finds a child's drawing that predicts tomorrow? Goal question: will he return it before the owner notices? Six scene cards later he speaks the story into his phone and transcribes the best take.
**Sources:** Haven

## F103 · Layered character profile
**Also known as:** Five Layers of a Character and The BIG Three (Haven)
**Use when:** Profiling leads and the antagonist before plotting, or fixing a flat character.
**Steps:**
1. Core layer, containing the BIG Three: first impression (species, age and gender, name, one or two traits, role, a one-word trait); goal; conflict and jeopardy (problems, flaws, risk, danger). Add a crucible (deadline, pride or loyalty that blocks retreat) and struggle (actions, reflexes).
2. Personality: fears, passions, loves, hates, humour, contradictions.
3. History: past events that explain flaws and motives.
4. Activity: work, pastimes, chores, belongings, habits.
5. Sensory image: sight, sound, smell, touch, taste, voice, dress, mannerisms.
**Common mistakes:** Building all five layers for every character. Do it for one or two leads and the antagonist; novices start with core and sensory layers.
**Example:** Marta, 58, a ferry deckhand with a limp, must retrieve her late husband's logbook from a sinking boat before the harbour closes. Pride keeps her from asking for help. She smells of diesel and peppermint and hums off-key when afraid.
**Sources:** Haven

## F104 · Epiphany and dramatic irony setups
**Also known as:** Epiphany and Dramatic Irony Three-Part Structures (Haven)
**Use when:** Building a twist ending or sharpening a reversal during revision.
**Steps:**
1. Setup: the character misjudges a person or thing, ideally the opposite of the truth, and acts in a way that worsens jeopardy.
2. Trigger (epiphany only): reveal concrete proof, optionally letting the reader spot it early for suspense.
3. Payoff: either the character realises the truth (epiphany), or the misguided act succeeds or fails opposite to intent with the character never realising (dramatic irony).
**Common mistakes:** A reveal that does not feel fully logical once the hidden facts come out.
**Example:** Priya trusts her new roommate Dev and tells him her savings hiding place. Epiphany: she finds her emptied envelope in his bag. Irony version: Dev, a thief, takes the envelope and unknowingly triggers a bank alert that saves her account.
**Sources:** Haven
