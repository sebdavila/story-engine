# Frameworks — structure

## F01 · Before–turn–after story arc
**Also known as:** Four Parts of a Good Story (Livesay); Steller Storytelling Framework: Normal, Explosion, New Normal (Hall); Story Spine (Edwards); Beginning, Middle, End with Bridge–Link–Pause (Dolan); 1-2-1 Story Formula (Guptara)
**Use when:** You need a short spoken or written story (10 seconds to 3 minutes) for a pitch opening, case study, meeting point or keynote moment.
**Avoid when:** The audience expects a recommendation deck or an analytical readout; use F04, F07 or F10 instead.
**Steps:**
1. Find the turn first: the event, decision or encounter where things changed. Draft it before anything else (T001).
2. Write the before: open on a time and place (T002), name one character, and give only the setting the listener needs to make sense of the turn. Too little context misleads; too much loses them.
3. Build the middle as a causal chain: each event happens because of the previous one, not merely after it. Split it into the problem and the actions taken if the story needs them.
4. Write the after so it answers the opening: show what is now different for the same character.
5. Add one unforgettable sensory detail or line of dialogue (T102).
6. End with a bridge sentence back to the business context, one inclusive takeaway sentence, and a silent pause (T008, T053). If time is tight, announce the point first (T006).
7. Delete any scaffold phrases you used to draft.
**Common mistakes:** Telling events in a list joined by "and then"; skipping the before so the turn has no weight; ending on a moral lecture instead of a link; overloading the before with background.
**Example:** A warehouse supervisor spent every Monday reconciling two inventory sheets by hand until midnight. One Monday a new picker asked why the sheets disagreed and nobody could answer. Because of that question, the team mapped the duplicate entries and merged the sheets into one. Now Mondays end at five, and the picker runs the weekly check. The point: the person newest to a process often sees its waste first.
**Sources:** Livesay; Hall; Edwards; Dolan; Guptara

## F02 · Story anatomy checklist
**Also known as:** Four Components of a Great Story (Hall); Story Spotting Framework (Callahan); Working Definition of Story in Business (Forman); Five-Element Story Model / PHAAT (Maxwell)
**Use when:** You must decide whether a draft is a story or a set of claims, or diagnose why a story feels flat.
**Avoid when:** The piece is a viral teaser that deliberately keeps one element (see F43).
**Steps:**
1. Character: confirm one identifiable person (not a company, value, product or crowd) does things in the draft.
2. Time and place: check for a marker that drops the listener into one moment (T003).
3. Connected events: confirm events are linked by cause or contrast, not just listed.
4. Emotion: find what the character felt; add it if missing (T100).
5. Surprise: identify the unexpected beat or realization; add one if none exists (T012).
6. Detail: look for at least one specific, audience-familiar detail (T103).
7. Opposition and change: name what stood in the way and what is different afterwards.
8. Point: state the business point the events illustrate for this audience.
**Common mistakes:** Treating an "Our story" page of claims as a story; assuming that meeting the minimum makes the story compelling; casting the brand as the character.
**Example:** A software firm's About page says it was founded in 2015 and values reliability. The checklist finds a date but no person, no events, no surprise. The rewrite follows a support engineer who drove to a client's server room during a storm, found the outage was a tripped breaker, and wrote the alert rule that now prevents it. Every box is now filled.
**Sources:** Hall; Callahan; Forman; Maxwell

## F03 · Hero's journey with the audience as hero
**Also known as:** Hero's Journey and Save the Cat (Edwards)
**Use when:** Crafting a customer-transformation campaign, a longer brand film or a keynote where the audience should picture themselves changing.
**Avoid when:** Telling an everyday oral anecdote in a meeting; the full plot feels artificial there (use F01 and S02).
**Steps:**
1. Show the hero (the customer or audience) in their ordinary world and add an early small act that makes them likeable.
2. Present the challenge and the hero's hesitation to take it on.
3. Introduce the mentor (you or your product) giving a tool or confidence for the first step (T024).
4. Escalate tests; let the hero doubt and drop an old belief for a new one.
5. Return the hero home with the reward improving their world.
**Common mistakes:** Casting your own company as the hero outside a mission story; skipping the likeable act so the audience never bonds; letting the mentor win the battle for the hero.
**Example:** A campaign for a bookkeeping app follows a bakery owner who stays late to pack leftover loaves for a shelter. Tax season hits and she resists switching tools. A neighbour shows her the app; she fails her first import, then learns to trust the automation. By spring she closes the books in an hour and spends the time teaching her apprentice.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F04 · Situation–Complication–Resolution
**Also known as:** SCQA; SCR; Persuasive Strategic Story; Nested Mini-SCR; Protagonist Lever Continuity; Conservative-First Sequencing; Presentation Epilogue (McKinsey); Evolution/change flow (Parikh)
**Use when:** Persuading decision-makers to approve a strategy, especially when they underestimate the problem; also for memo introductions.
**Avoid when:** The audience already feels the pain and needs hope (F05), or the mandate is to inform, not recommend (F07).
**Steps:**
1. Situation: state the uncontroversial current state and history. Name the core metric as protagonist and its drivers as supporting characters.
2. Complication: project how trends hit those same drivers, converging on the metric. Introduce no new characters.
3. In a memo, write or imply the question the complication raises.
4. Resolution: give a prioritized, non-overlapping set of actions. Order by impact; if the room fears risk, show standard actions first and prove them insufficient before radical ones.
5. Choose the order: lead with the resolution for an anxious audience (T009), with the complication for a complacent one.
6. When moving from industry findings to one client, embed a mini-SCR inside the resolution.
7. Add a labeled epilogue only for an urgent issue outside the main timeframe, paired with solutions.
**Common mistakes:** Bringing new drivers into Act II; listing actions without priority; jumping to radical changes before a cautious audience accepts the basics.
**Example:** A regional clinic chain shows patient visits flat and staff costs rising. The complication projects that wage inflation and two new competitors cut margin to zero within three years. The resolution starts with scheduling and supply savings, shows they close only half the gap, then proposes consolidating two sites.
**Sources:** McKinsey; Parikh

## F05 · Situation–Opportunity–Resolution
**Also known as:** SOR Framework; Opportunity-Capture Story (McKinsey); Opportunity flow (Parikh)
**Use when:** The audience already knows it has a problem, or is doing well and needs a reason to aim higher.
**Avoid when:** The audience denies the problem; build urgency with F04 instead.
**Steps:**
1. Situation: set the context and current challenges, neutral or negative in tone.
2. Optionally add a comment: one sentence interpreting what the situation means.
3. Opportunity: prove one avenue is real and lucrative, list the options, pick the best, and state what it requires (money, time, model changes).
4. Resolution: prescribe the actions to capture it; embed a mini-SCR if shifting to one organization (F04).
5. Close on an explicit call to action.
**Common mistakes:** Using it on a complacent audience; asserting the opportunity without sizing it; skipping the requirements so it sounds free.
**Example:** A print shop's owners know orders are falling. The deck shows that local event planners spend heavily on same-day signage and no competitor offers delivery. It sizes the segment, lists three ways to enter, picks a delivery bundle, and asks for one van and a part-time driver.
**Sources:** McKinsey; Parikh

## F06 · Pilot–Results–Scale
**Also known as:** PRS Framework; Lean Innovation / Project Status Story (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Reporting after a pilot or test, or turning a routine status update into a request for action.
**Avoid when:** Seeking approval to run the pilot; use F04 with untested hypotheses as the complication.
**Steps:**
1. Pilot: restate the business objective, the hypotheses the test was meant to check and the agreed success metrics.
2. Results: report plainly which hypotheses held, which failed and what surprised you.
3. Scale: ask for specific time, money and headcount, with a business case covering scope, timeframe, return and metrics.
**Common mistakes:** Hiding failed hypotheses; reporting activity instead of results; ending with no ask.
**Example:** A retailer tested self-checkout in four stores to see whether wait times and theft would fall. Waits dropped by a third; theft rose slightly; older shoppers used it more than expected. The team asks to roll out to twenty stores with one attendant per bank.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F07 · What / So what / Now what
**Also known as:** Approach–Findings–Implications / Informative Analytical Story (McKinsey); What? So What? Now What? meeting (Callahan)
**Use when:** Sharing research, a forecast or a field problem so a group can decide next steps, without arguing a contested recommendation.
**Avoid when:** You must win approval for a disputed course of action (F04).
**Steps:**
1. What: in an analytical deck, state the objective and method without leaking results; in a meeting, tell a specific negative story from the ground.
2. So what: lead with the headline finding, then break it down along one segmentation used throughout, with key assumptions and scenarios; in a meeting, tell a contrasting positive story where a better way already works.
3. Now what: list concrete changes, owners and leading indicators that signal which scenario is unfolding. Keep this part short and action-focused.
**Common mistakes:** Narrating the research chronologically with its dead ends; switching segmentation between slides; telling long stories in the action step.
**Example:** A hotel group studies late check-outs. The finding: three quarters of requests come from business guests on Fridays. The implication: offer paid late check-out on Fridays only, owned by front-desk leads, tracked by weekly upsell revenue.
**Sources:** McKinsey; Callahan

## F08 · Strategy story: past, pivot, present, future
**Also known as:** X Story / Four Ps (Guptara); Clarity Story; Strategy Stories; Costanza Manoeuvre (Callahan); change story (Edwards)
**Use when:** Explaining a new strategy, a reorganization or a hard decision such as layoffs, so people stop filling the gap with rumours.
**Avoid when:** The audience needs a multi-year roadmap of phases (F09) or a data case for approval (F04).
**Steps:**
1. Test each strategic choice by stating its opposite; drop choices whose opposite nobody would pick.
2. Draft in the order present, pivot, past, future: first what we are doing now, then what happened, then how things used to work, then what is at stake.
3. Tell it in chronological order: in the past; then something happened; so now; in the future.
4. Put a first-hand moment into the past, the pivot and the future parts. For the future, find a team already living it (S19) or signal an imagined scene.
5. Admit slow recognition and failed attempts, and name the internal advocates.
6. Have leaders co-create it rather than delegating it to a strategy team or agency.
**Common mistakes:** Moving outcomes to the front, which turns it back into an argument; keeping all four parts abstract; using platitudes as choices.
**Example:** In the past, a courier firm promised next-day delivery everywhere. Then a driver showed the CEO a week of half-empty vans on rural routes. So now rural parcels go out twice a week with tracking. In the future, one depot that already runs this way shows costs down and complaints flat; if it fails, rural service would be cut altogether.
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan; Edwards

## F09 · Strategy as a story in chapters
**Also known as:** Story in Chapters / Action Agenda; Story About the Future; Stepping-Stone Milestone Linking (Forman)
**Use when:** Leading a turnaround, merger integration or multi-year transformation that must hold together through setbacks.
**Avoid when:** The change is a single decision; F08 is enough.
**Steps:**
1. Name four to six chapters in sequence (for example stabilize, repair, turn around, build, break out).
2. Define the actions and metrics that complete each chapter.
3. Back the chapters with behaviors tied to appraisal and reward.
4. In every update, letter and earnings call, name which chapter a decision, metric or setback belongs to.
5. Declare a new chapter only when the numbers justify it.
**Common mistakes:** Announcing a later chapter early; chapters with no reward system behind them; updates that never reference the chapters.
**Example:** A family-owned furniture maker in debt names three chapters: stop the bleeding, fix quality, grow online. Each monthly letter opens with the current chapter. When a product recall hits, the CEO frames it as a hurdle inside chapter two, not a return to chapter one.
**Sources:** Forman

## F10 · Pyramid story blueprint
**Also known as:** Story blueprint / Pyramid hierarchy; One-minute elevator scaling; Key-argument executive summary; Deductive and inductive argument; Inductive status report; Blemishing effect (Parikh); Depth-First Tree Traversal; Mid-Act Recapitulation Slide (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Turning analysis into a recommendation that must work as a hallway update, a report and a deck.
**Avoid when:** The message is a single short anecdote (F01).
**Steps:**
1. Top: write the recommendation or call to action in one sentence.
2. Key line: write 3–5 premises. Use a deductive chain from an agreed fact to the conclusion when steps depend on each other; use independent reasons when they do not, as in status reports.
3. Test a deductive chain: is any relevant fact missing, and can every premise be proven? One rejected premise collapses it.
4. Under each premise add 3–5 independent supporting points matched to the audience's decision criteria. Arguing for something, put cons first; arguing against, put pros first.
5. Translate: top plus key line for a one-minute update; one paragraph per premise for an executive summary; key line as agenda and full-sentence slide titles (T029) for a deck.
6. Present depth-first: finish one branch, usually two levels down, before the next. After a deep branch, repeat the summary slide to reorient.
**Common mistakes:** Premises the audience can dispute; more than five points per level; breadth-first jumping between branches.
**Example:** A city IT team recommends renewing its permit system rather than replacing it. Premises: volumes will double; the current system scales with an upgrade; replacement takes two years; the upgrade takes four months. Each premise carries three facts beneath it.
**Sources:** Parikh; McKinsey

## F11 · Issue tree and problem framing
**Also known as:** Issue and Hypothesis Tree Workflow; One-Step-Too-Far Scoping; Top-Down Level Completion (McKinsey); Logic tree; Branch-level checkpoint; Visual self-screening prompt; Real-time tree markup (Parikh)
**Use when:** The brief is open-ended and guessing the audience's focus risks a wasted story; do it before analysis or drafting.
**Avoid when:** Scope and stakeholder expectations are already pinned down.
**Steps:**
1. Scope: keep asking what impact the problem has until it exceeds the decision-maker's remit, then step back one level.
2. List constraints that rule options out.
3. Break the question into complete, non-overlapping sub-questions, filling each level before going deeper.
4. Rewrite each sub-question as a testable statement.
5. Review the tree with stakeholders: main branches first, then ask them to scan each branch for what stands out; mark items keep, strike or maybe on the shared screen, then rank.
6. Prioritize by impact and ease; sketch full-sentence slide titles before analysis.
7. Test, look for disconfirming evidence, and update tree and outline.
**Common mistakes:** Overlapping branches; scoping beyond the decision-maker; reading every node aloud instead of letting stakeholders talk.
**Example:** Asked why a gym's members quit, an analyst splits causes into price, schedule, facilities and coaching. In a short call the manager strikes facilities (just renovated) and ranks schedule first, so the story focuses on class times.
**Sources:** McKinsey; Parikh

## F12 · Audience-first pitch arc (SUPERB)
**Also known as:** SUPERB Storytelling Model (Edwards)
**Use when:** Structuring a pitch, proposal, case study, award entry or persuasive email where the audience must stay at the center.
**Avoid when:** Delivering bad news (S15) or a neutral readout (F07).
**Steps:**
1. Shared experiences: open on something you both agree on, using questions whose answer is yes (T072).
2. Ultimate triumph: state the destination and its value to them up front.
3. Problem definition: name the obstacles between them and that goal.
4. Explore options and objections: compare options (F13) and voice the flaws in your own proposal with workarounds (T073).
5. Real: show the solution in one named person's day and list quick wins (T045).
6. Best of both worlds: close with both the gain and the safety (T074).
**Common mistakes:** Opening with your credentials; hiding weaknesses; ending only on upside for risk-averse buyers. In a short email you may drop the options but keep the objections.
**Example:** A cleaning contractor pitching a school opens by agreeing that sick days spike every February. It promises fewer closures, names the gaps in current cleaning, admits its night crew costs more, shows a janitor's new shift, and closes on healthier pupils and a guaranteed response time.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F13 · Option elimination
**Also known as:** Goldilocks Model (Edwards); Option-elimination flow (Parikh); Three-Level Solution Brainstorming (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Your recommendation must look discovered rather than asserted, or must quietly rule out competitors' approaches.
**Avoid when:** There is only one viable option and inventing straw options would insult the room.
**Steps:**
1. Generate options at three levels: standard, radical and do nothing.
2. Pick three to present, never more.
3. For the first two, state their appeal, then why each fails.
4. Present your choice as the one that keeps their upsides and avoids their downsides.
**Common mistakes:** Weak straw options; four or more options; skipping what is attractive about the rejected ones.
**Example:** A charity choosing a fundraising channel considers street fundraisers (fast sign-ups, high cancellations) and TV ads (wide reach, unaffordable). It recommends a referral drive among existing donors: cheaper than TV and more loyal than street sign-ups.
**Sources:** Edwards; Parikh; McKinsey

## F14 · Investor pitch deck
**Also known as:** 12-Slide Irresistible Investor Pitch Deck (Livesay)
**Use when:** Pitching investors or an executive buying committee that expects metrics inside a narrative.
**Avoid when:** In a standard sales presentation, drop or adapt the financials and funding slides.
**Steps:**
1. Title with a short tagline promising the big idea.
2. Summary: market size, traction, endorsements, one testimonial.
3. Three customer problems, one image each.
4. Three solutions in the same order.
5. Product: how it works better than alternatives.
6. Business model: customers, market size, revenue model, commitments.
7. Go-to-market: how you reach buyers and influencers.
8. Financials: simple three-year forecast and monthly burn.
9. Competition: a two-axis grid with you in the upper right.
10. Team: relevant experience.
11. Ask: amount, use of funds, milestones.
12. Close: contacts, testimonials, an invitation; keep backup slides for questions (T032).
**Common mistakes:** Problems and solutions in different orders; vague use of funds; no testimonial.
**Example:** A startup selling sensors for cold-chain trucks opens with "Never lose a load." It pairs spoiled cargo, late alerts and manual logs with live temperature, instant texts and automatic reports, and closes with a grocer's quote about saving a shipment.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F15 · Writing process from a blank page (AUTHOR)
**Also known as:** AUTHOR Writing Process (Edwards)
**Use when:** Starting any presentation, report or speech from nothing.
**Avoid when:** Writing legal, academic or strictly formal documents; skip the write-as-you-speak step there.
**Steps:**
1. Analogue: ideate with pen, paper and sticky notes before opening software.
2. Understand: keep asking what the audience needs to hear; research their knowledge and alternatives (F22).
3. Talk: write in your spoken words and read drafts aloud (T058).
4. Headlines: write all full-sentence headlines in sequence before any body text (T029).
5. One: one idea per slide or paragraph; the first sentence is the headline.
6. Rewrite: sleep on it, cut what does not move the story, read it to a colleague.
**Common mistakes:** Opening slides first; topic labels instead of sentences; shipping the first draft.
**Example:** A product manager preparing a quarterly review sticks twelve notes on a wall, rearranges them until the sequence reads as an argument, types the twelve headlines, and only then fills the slides. Next morning she cuts three slides that only described her team.
**Sources:** Edwards
