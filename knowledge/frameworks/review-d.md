# Frameworks — review (F372–F381)

## F372 · Human-first AI writing loop
**Also known as:** The AI Thought Leadership Loop (Lazer)
**Use when:** Developing an essay, book, speech or strategic narrative with AI as a sparring partner, not a ghostwriter.
**Avoid when:** The goal is a quick generic draft where original voice does not matter.
**Steps:**
1. Write the thesis and outline yourself first, then have a blunt AI persona (or a panel of several) attack the logic.
2. Feed the outline into a deep-research tool to surface papers, interviews and scene-level details; drill into key characters.
3. Draft every sentence yourself. Consult AI only briefly for alternatives when stuck on a cliché or transition.
4. Run your finished human draft through the AI critic with structured prompts tied to story elements and shapes.
**Common mistakes:** Letting AI draft the outline or the prose collapses thousands of small creative choices into one prompt and drags the text toward the average.
**Example:** A pastry chef drafting a memoir chapter outlines her apprenticeship alone, asks the AI to find holes in her timeline, writes the scenes by hand, and only then asks for a critique of where the tension sags.
**Sources:** Lazer

## F373 · Two-criteria detail filter
**Also known as:** Details Tick List Test, Two-Tick Minimum (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** A report, chart or interactive story feels overloaded with niche caveats, or lacks grounding context.
**Steps:**
1. Audit each descriptive passage, footnote, filter or caveat against six purposes: background context, further evidence for the main insight, depth on the broader context, something unique about the data, conflict or complication, data backstory.
2. Keep a detail only if it meets at least two purposes.
3. Cut single-purpose details or move them to linked material. Deep method notes for experts can sit behind secondary links.
**Example:** In a dashboard on bike-share use, a note that stations were added in 2021 passes (context plus backstory), and a filter by season passes (evidence plus depth). A note on the colour palette meets none and goes.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F374 · Positionality bias audit
**Also known as:** Standpoint Approach to Data Bias (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** Designing questions, collecting or coding data, interpreting results, or reviewing a data story before publication.
**Steps:**
1. List five stages in a worksheet: coming up with questions, data collection, data analysis, data storytelling, sharing.
2. At each stage ask what biases the team's demographics, assumptions or sampling boundaries could introduce (for example, surveying one neighbourhood only).
3. Write the standpoint fix for each: diversify respondents, narrow the claim to who was actually sampled, or bring affected communities into the design.
**Common mistakes:** Treating standpoint as relativism. Claims stay testable and evidence-based.
**Example:** A clinic reviewing wait-time data notices the survey ran only in English. The team adds translated forms and restates the finding as covering English-speaking patients until new responses arrive.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F375 · Separate authoring, drafting and editing phases
**Also known as:** Authoring-First Workflow (Emunds)
**Use when:** Taking a story from first spark to polished manuscript.
**Steps:**
1. Mind map: capture fragments as brief phrases inside the story-engine circles.
2. Contextual phase: push past the first ten obvious ideas and brainstorm ten options for each missing engine piece, fixing the climax first if possible.
3. Outline phase: map 50–60 scenes plus genre conventions, up to the point where objective structure takes over.
4. Creative writing phase: write all scenes quickly without policing craft.
5. Editing and rewriting phase: alternate analysis notes (editor hat) with rewrites (writer hat).
**Common mistakes:** Wearing editor and writer hats at once. Skimping on the authoring phase: Emunds suggests at least a third of project time on engine and outline to avoid rewriting whole acts.
**Example:** A novelist writing a lighthouse mystery spends the first month on the mind map and scene list, drafts all scenes in six weeks without rereading, then edits in alternating note and rewrite rounds.
**Sources:** Emunds

## F376 · Team alignment by story block
**Also known as:** Team-building: speed storytelling together (Cowie); Block-by-block feedback framing (Cowie)
**Use when:** Team members describe the business or project inconsistently, or one block (often how) feels unclear or contested.
**Avoid when:** A foundational block such as the name or timeline is still undecided by key insiders. Pause external use until it is resolved.
**Steps:**
1. Each person answers the six prompts (who, what, where, when, how, why) alone.
2. Compile responses and start with natural agreement to build a positive base.
3. Spot gaps, inconsistencies and blocks where confidence is low.
4. Treat disagreements as differences in a specific block, not as personal conflict.
5. Resolve disputed blocks in one workshop or in mini-meetings on one block each, until everyone endorses the story.
Cowie's framing line: open with the parts everyone agrees on, then name the single contested part.
**Example:** A logistics startup's staff give three different answers on how deliveries get routed. A short meeting on the how block alone settles a daily dispatch rhythm, and the other five blocks stay untouched.
**Sources:** Cowie

## F377 · Then-versus-now story review
**Also known as:** Then-versus-now momentum review (Cowie)
**Use when:** Enthusiasm drops, early markets or partners fail, or the team fixates on obstacles.
**Steps:**
1. Put the original six story blocks beside current reality in two columns.
2. Mark what stayed the same and what measurably grew.
3. Look at shifts in where and when caused by setbacks to spot unexpected traction.
4. Adjust connected blocks, such as narrowing how to specific segments, so the story still guides decisions.
**Common mistakes:** Treating it as a one-off. Cowie frames it as a living compass to revisit.
**Example:** A cooperative selling hand-dyed yarn lost its largest craft fair. Side by side, the why and how held, but the where shifted from fairs to online knitting circles, and the how narrowed to subscription boxes.
**Sources:** Cowie

## F378 · Reconstruction drill for writing craft
**Also known as:** The Ben Franklin Method for Improving Story Skills (Snow; Lazauskas)
**Use when:** Training craft alone, or studying how top storytellers pace scenes and choose words.
**Avoid when:** Only passive reading is planned. It does not give the same gains.
**Steps:**
1. Pick exemplary prose and jot a brief hint of the idea in each sentence.
2. Set the hints aside for a few days until the exact wording fades, then rewrite the piece from the hints.
3. Compare word for word with the original to find gaps in vocabulary and sentence variety.
4. Variant: turn the prose into verse, later back to prose, to widen word recall.
5. Variant: scramble the hints, wait weeks, rearrange into the strongest logical order, then draft.
**Example:** A junior reporter hints three paragraphs of a favourite feature writer, rewrites them the next week, and sees she always used flat verbs where the original used vivid ones.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## F379 · Word-cutting pass
**Also known as:** The Sludge Report (Snow; Lazauskas); Prose Liposuction (Gottschall); 11-Category Deadwood Purge (Haven); Mandatory 15% Word Cut (Haven)
**Use when:** Self-editing any draft, script or presentation before release.
**Avoid when:** A cut would remove context the reader needs. Fluency then collapses into confusion.
**Steps:**
1. Go sentence by sentence. Snow and Lazauskas aim to halve a paragraph.
2. Swap wordy connectors for single words (because for as a result of the fact that).
3. Strip hedges and weak qualifiers (somewhat, a bit, really, very).
4. Delete category nouns when the named examples make them obvious.
5. Tighten clauses into direct verbs and use pronouns where unambiguous.
6. Haven's deadwood list: abstractions, qualifiers, extra connectors, redundancies, clichés, circumlocution, hyperbole, euphemisms, jargon, double negatives. After editing, count words and cut a further 15%.
7. For action scenes (Gottschall): dictate aloud, draft fast, then break long sentences into short rhythmic pieces over many passes.
**Common mistakes:** Stopping after one pass. Snow and Lazauskas' own example took a paragraph from 46 words to 29, then 23.
**Example:** A product update opens with a long sentence about the fact that the team has been working on shipping speed. The editor cuts it to: Orders now ship in one day.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas; Gottschall; Haven

## F380 · Declutter a true story
**Also known as:** Seven Ways to Focus and Declutter Your Story (Biesenbach); Name the Known, Omit the Obscure (Biesenbach); Approximate Dates and Scale Numbers (Biesenbach); Witness-Fairness Litmus Test (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** Editing any story, especially a personal one where attachment to real-life detail clutters it.
**Avoid when:** The audience is insiders who value exact industry names and jargon. Keep those.
**Steps:**
1. Filter every word, fact and joke through the goal; cut what is not essential.
2. Keep one main character and at most one supporter.
3. Stay on the trunk; skip side-branch tangents and ad-libs.
4. Keep one turning point, not every committee and twist.
5. Trim details: keep brief time, place and sensory cues. Keep famous names, but turn obscure people and firms into roles (the bank manager). Drop exact dates unless pivotal; use half instead of 53 percent, several hundred instead of 278, one in five instead of a huge raw count.
6. Cut exposition and open in the middle of action.
7. Use essential lies: compress timelines or reorder minor facts without altering the truth.
8. Witness test: would someone present at the event find the account fair?
**Common mistakes:** Simplification that a witness would call dishonest.
**Example:** A nurse tells of the night a storm cut power. She drops the charge nurse's name and the exact date, merges two hallway conversations into one, and opens with the lights dying. A colleague who was there still finds it fair.
**Sources:** Biesenbach

## F381 · Protecting a story through approvals
**Also known as:** Defending Story Integrity, Approval Negotiation (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** An organizational story must pass HR, legal or committee reviewers.
**Steps:**
1. Set up front that final editorial authority rests with a small group of two.
2. Hold firm on character, conflict, stakes and emotion.
3. Accept small additions, like naming a department, that slow the story a little without breaking it.
4. Ask reviewers to explain their underlying concern and stay open to valid points.
5. Cite brain and memory evidence for emotional storytelling.
6. Steer talk toward what can be done, not what cannot.
**Common mistakes:** Demanding perfection instead of accepting small compromises.
**Example:** A hospital foundation's donor story is reviewed by five people. The writer gets sign-off narrowed to two, accepts adding the clinic's full name, and refuses to cut the patient's fear from the opening scene.
**Sources:** Biesenbach
