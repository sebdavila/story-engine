# Frameworks — brand (F332–F338)

## F332 · Sketch development from observation to script
**Also known as:** Sketch Comedy Development Workflow (Cody)
**Use when:** You launch a differentiated PR or marketing campaign, pitch a competitive account, or defuse anxiety around a milestone or change by turning an unspoken truth into a surprising piece.
**Avoid when:** You skipped audience empathy; that leads to tone-deaf backlash.
**Steps:**
1. Observation and thesis: notice what others miss and commit to one clear point of view.
2. Form: frame the thesis as parody (spoof a familiar format), inversion (a character acts opposite to expectation) or exaggeration (push familiar reality to extremes).
3. Visual brainstorming: produce many answers to the Five Ws, hold back criticism, and welcome and extend wild ideas.
4. Improv exploration: put the thesis, characters and setting on their feet in long-form improv to find the premise (clear in 5 to 10 seconds), the complication (an outside obstacle or character flaw), the escalation and the payoff.
5. Script and revise: capture the best live moments in a tight script, plan the performance and test the tone against audience sensibilities before launch.
**Common mistakes:** Launching without checking tone against the audience.
**Example:** A small accounting firm pitches a bakery chain. Observation: owners dread tax season like a dentist visit. Form: parody of a dental commercial. In improv, the premise is a "tax hygienist" arriving to scold a baker's receipts, the complication is a shoebox of crumpled paper, and the payoff is the baker cheering at a clean ledger. They script it and test it on a friendly owner first.
**Sources:** Cody

## F333 · Leader-targeted satire for honest inclusion talks
**Also known as:** DEI Comic-Magazine Workflow, modified "Dozens" (Cody)
**Use when:** Diversity, equity and inclusion sessions feel performative or box-checking, or unsafe for marginalized staff to voice real concerns.
**Avoid when:** The humour would target protected identities; self-deprecation may aim only at leadership or yourself.
**Steps:**
1. Invite team members to submit anonymously candid, even exaggerated assessments of the company's inclusion climate from their viewpoint.
2. Filter out unusable submissions, then combine the nameless critiques with photos of your own exaggerated faces inside a comic-layout app.
3. Perform the comic magazine at the next session, using shared laughter at leadership's expense to move into serious problem-solving.
**Common mistakes:** Asking minority employees to educate the room instead of putting the leader in the hot seat; mocking anyone other than leadership.
**Example:** A regional logistics director collects anonymous notes such as "Meetings are scheduled when parents can't attend." She pairs each with a photo of herself looking aghast in a comic layout and reads the issue aloud at the quarterly session. The room laughs, then the team sets new meeting hours the same afternoon.
**Sources:** Cody

## F334 · Create, connect, optimize flywheel
**Also known as:** The CCO Pattern / The Flywheel (Snow; Lazauskas)
**Use when:** You run an ongoing publication, brand media hub or content marketing program and want an audience that compounds.
**Avoid when:** The team will stop iterating; the loop fails when algorithms or audience habits shift and nobody adapts.
**Steps:**
1. Create: make stories around genuine audience curiosity, not shiny-platform hype.
2. Connect: deliver them where your target audience already gathers and draw people into owned channels.
3. Optimize: check which pieces actually drove engagement and conversion, improve both the craft and the distribution, and cycle through the loop faster than rivals.
**Common mistakes:** Treating a platform's current feed rules as permanent.
**Example:** A bike-commuter blog from a helmet maker publishes a story about winter riding. It tests three headlines on a small group in a cycling forum, sends the best performer to its newsletter, and then tracks which version led to signups. The next story reuses the winning angle.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## F335 · Concentric channel map
**Also known as:** The Storytelling Bull's-Eye (Snow; Lazauskas)
**Use when:** You select publishing channels and design calls to action for a brand or publication, pulling audiences from rented feeds into owned relationships.
**Avoid when:** You treat the listed outer platforms as fixed; they change fast, and only the pull toward owned email and web stays constant.
**Steps:**
1. Classify the organization by audience (B2B or B2C) and main objective (branding or conversion).
2. Put your website in the centre ring (full control of brand and conversion) and email in the second ring (direct inbox reach).
3. Map outer-ring platforms to your quadrant. For B2B, brand work suits LinkedIn, Facebook, YouTube and podcasts, while conversion suits Google Search, SlideShare and industry email lists. For B2C, brand work suits Facebook, YouTube, Instagram and Reddit, while conversion suits Pinterest and Instagram.
4. Shape each story to fit the outer channel it appears on, and add a route that draws readers at least one ring closer in (social to email to website).
**Common mistakes:** Posting on outer platforms with no path inward.
**Example:** A B2B payroll-software firm (brand quadrant) posts a founder interview on a professional network and boosts it, ending with a link to sign up for its monthly letter. Letter readers are invited to a webinar page on the site, where demo requests arrive.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## F336 · Funnel stage by timing matrix
**Also known as:** The Story Funnel-Matrix (Snow; Lazauskas)
**Use when:** You brainstorm story topics across the buyer journey or audit gaps in an editorial calendar.
**Avoid when:** You would push product stories at cold awareness audiences; that breaks rapport, like proposing on a first date.
**Steps:**
1. Set three stages of the relationship: awareness (common interests and values, leaving your product out), consideration (stories of people around you, such as customers, employees, innovations) and acquisition (stories on your products or services).
2. Cross those with three timings: timely (tied to news and current events), seasonal (holidays and calendar moments that recur) and evergreen (useful at any time).
3. Populate all nine cells, drawing on search and social data so stories for the top, middle and bottom of the funnel stay in balance.
**Common mistakes:** A calendar heavy on acquisition and empty at awareness.
**Example:** A tent maker fills awareness with an evergreen camp-cooking guide and a timely wildfire-season safety explainer; consideration with a seasonal story about a family's first backcountry trip; acquisition with an evergreen repair-service page showing a mended seam.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## F337 · Company origin story
**Also known as:** Company Origin Story Pattern (Biesenbach); Company Origin Story, Ch. 11 (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** You write an About Us page, a recruiting narrative or heritage communications and want to humanize the company and anchor its promise.
**Avoid when:** The audience is investors or franchisees and only wants a financial chronology; for consumers such timelines alienate.
**Steps:**
1. Present the founder or founders starting from a humble or relatable place.
2. Show the annoying problem they fixed, or the promise to run things differently.
3. Explain how that insight became a product or a company.
4. Follow its growth through a single steady brand theme, for instance innovation or value.
5. Link the founding story to the values guiding the company today.
**Common mistakes:** Stacking too many themes (quality, service, cost and location together) and diluting focus; a purely financial timeline.
**Example:** A mother in a small town bought a second-hand sewing machine to patch her kids' backpacks, which kept splitting at the straps. Neighbours asked for her reinforced stitch. She built a workshop around one theme, durability, and today the company still repairs any strap for free.
**Sources:** Biesenbach

## F338 · Career story in five beats
**Also known as:** Five-Part Personal Narrative Structure (Biesenbach); Personal Career Narrative, Ch. 13 (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** You answer "tell me about yourself" in interviews, networking or client introductions, replacing a chronological résumé recital and linking scattered career stops into one thread.
**Avoid when:** Your career shifted gradually; adapt emphasis to the audience rather than invent one cinematic moment. Early in your career, use personal life milestones.
**Steps:**
1. Beginning: lay out your starting point, meaning your everyday situation or career emphasis.
2. Inciting incident: identify the event or realization that knocked you off that path.
3. Turning point: say what new direction or mission you took up as a result.
4. Conflict: describe the obstacles or internal struggles you hit along the way.
5. Resolution: show how the conflict resolved into your current purpose, bringing the arc full circle.
**Common mistakes:** Reciting jobs in date order; forcing a fake dramatic moment.
**Example:** A hospital IT analyst started in retail software chasing bonuses. A nurse friend lost a day's charts to a crash, which shook his view of the work. He moved to clinical systems, struggled with doctors who distrusted new tools, and now builds software that clinicians help design. At an interview he ends with, "I make sure the charts are there when the nurse needs them."
**Sources:** Biesenbach
