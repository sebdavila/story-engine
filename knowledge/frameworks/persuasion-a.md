# Frameworks — persuasion (F224–F234)

## F224 · Audience-first business story model (SUPERB)
**Also known as:** The SUPERB Storytelling Model (Edwards)
**Use when:** Structuring a pitch, presentation, data deck, email, case study or meeting where the audience must agree with you.
**Avoid when:** The message is a brief email; there you may skip comparing options, but still voice doubts you suspect the reader holds.
**Steps:**
1. Open on shared ground: a moment, a common business challenge, or a question the audience will silently answer yes to.
2. State the destination and the main benefit to them before any background.
3. Define the specific obstacles between them and that goal; for an internal audience, hold diagnostic conversations first.
4. Compare several options before revealing yours (see F225), and name the flaws and risks of your own choice with workarounds.
5. Make the solution real by showing its effect on one person's working day, with quick wins.
6. Close with wording for both kinds of listener: future gain for the reward-seekers, safety and reliability for the risk-averse.
**Common mistakes:** Giving background before the destination; hiding the weaknesses of your own recommendation so they fester in listeners' heads; closing with gain language only.
**Example:** A freight broker pitches a regional bakery chain. She starts from the owner's own worry about late flour deliveries, then promises a 99% on-time week. She maps what causes delays, compares two routing setups, admits her pick needs a Monday cut-off, walks through one baker's 4 a.m. shift, and ends with growth plus a refund guarantee.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F225 · Three options, recommend the middle
**Also known as:** The Goldilocks Model (Edwards); Behavioral Economics / Choice Architecture (Lupton)
**Use when:** Explaining why your recommendation or method beats alternatives, in a pitch, strategy paper or the options step of F224; or laying out tiers in pricing and sign-up flows.
**Avoid when:** You would need more than three options, or when the design would use defaults or framing to trap people.
**Steps:**
1. Present a first plausible approach, say why it appeals, then say why it fails or is too risky.
2. Present a contrasting second approach that fails in the opposite direction (outdated, unworkable or too costly).
3. Present yours as the middle path that keeps the upsides and avoids both pitfalls.
4. In product design, set ethical defaults, offer three tiers so the middle gets chosen most, and tune size, colour and description without deceiving.
**Common mistakes:** Listing four or more options; straw-man extremes; pre-checked add-ons, disguised clickbait or hard-to-cancel flows, which erode trust.
**Example:** A gym owner chooses a software vendor. The cheap spreadsheet tool saves money but loses members' booking history. The enterprise suite handles everything but takes months to set up. The mid-size app imports history in a day, and she picks it.
**Sources:** Edwards; Lupton

## F226 · Eight emotional needs of an audience
**Also known as:** Eight Basic Human Emotional Needs (Edwards)
**Use when:** Designing or rescuing a meeting or presentation and you want the audience to leave feeling it went well.
**Steps:**
1. Alignment: start where the audience emotionally is and re-confirm agreement at each transition with a short question.
2. Connection: use plain speech without jargon, and put tension and its resolution into one sentence at turning points.
3. Structure: show an agenda that marks done and coming sections, with progress cues.
4. Recognition: cast the audience as the hero and defer to their expertise.
5. Ownership: leave part of the proposal open so they can adjust and co-create.
6. Stimulus: vary slide pace, break template rhythm, move around the room.
7. Safety: say explicitly what is reliable and protected for risk-averse listeners.
8. Support: lay out implementation steps, dated commitments and quick wins that make them look good to their bosses.
**Common mistakes:** Delivering a sealed, finished idea that gives the audience no role; one monotone pace; forgetting reassurance.
**Example:** A consultant presenting a new rota to hospital managers leaves the weekend cover section blank on purpose and asks the head nurse to fill it. She also ends with a two-week pilot plan the managers can show their director.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F227 · Visibility ladder with a prospect
**Also known as:** Invisible to Irresistible Ladder (Livesay)
**Use when:** Planning outreach, judging where you stand with a prospect, or tracking progress across a long sales cycle.
**Steps:**
1. Diagnose the rung: invisible (they do not know you), insignificant (they see you as noise), interesting (they pause and ask for more), intriguing (they lean in to see how it works for them), irresistible (you are the only logical choice).
2. Invisible to insignificant: get seen where they already look, for example through a podcast that invites them as guests.
3. Insignificant to interesting: use empathy, relevant industry insight and rapport.
4. Interesting to intriguing: use what-if scenarios and differentiated value.
5. Intriguing to irresistible: deliver return on wisdom (F236) with a tailored vision.
6. Move only one rung at a time.
**Common mistakes:** Pushing for the close from a low rung, which loses ground.
**Example:** A small landscaping firm wants a hotel group as a client. It sends the facilities director a seasonal planting guide for her three sites (interesting), then sketches how her courtyard could save water (intriguing), and asks for a trial only after she replies.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F228 · Trust, like, then fit
**Also known as:** Three Unspoken Buyer Questions: Trust, Like, and Know / Gut, Heart, Head (Livesay)
**Use when:** A first meeting or the arc of a sales conversation.
**Steps:**
1. Gut, do I trust you: show calm presence, a warm greeting and plain integrity.
2. Heart, do I like you: show real empathy and share a short human origin story.
3. Head, will this work for me: tell a conflict-and-resolution story about a similar client so they can picture their own success (see F238).
**Common mistakes:** Using the know-like-trust order; stopping after trust and liking, since the buyer still says no if they cannot see it working for them.
**Example:** A tax adviser arrives at a busy dental clinic and tells the harried receptionist the schedule looks like a juggling act and she should call when ready. Later, with the owner, he mentions growing up in his father's repair shop, then tells of another clinic that stopped losing evenings to paperwork.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F229 · Typing the buyer to tailor the pitch
**Also known as:** Three Personality Types of Buyers (Livesay); Believer, Builder, Bean Counter (Livesay)
**Use when:** Discovery calls, walking into a buyer's office, or pitching several stakeholders.
**Steps:**
1. Read the personality from cues. Feeling: speaks of comfort, shows family photos; use rapport, self-deprecating humor, interest in family. Information: punctual, tidy, wants the numbers behind numbers; state the time frame, be organized, bring proprietary data. Big picture: bored by detail, quotes visionaries; talk about future trends and link them to a grand vision.
2. Read the emotional driver. Believer: wants the vision and team; paint the future world together. Builder: wants to co-create; invite their input. Bean counter: wants return; check every analytic box and contrast return with the cost of doing nothing.
3. Combine the two readings, since one buyer can be a bean counter with a big-picture style.
**Common mistakes:** Asking an information buyer if it feels good; giving a big-picture buyer dry demographics.
**Example:** A solar installer meets a farm co-op board. The chair is a builder, so she asks him to help choose panel placement. The treasurer is a bean counter, so she brings a ten-year savings table next to the cost of staying on the grid.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F230 · Sell yourself, company, then product
**Also known as:** Three-Part Pitch Structure: Yourself, Company, Product (Livesay)
**Use when:** Structuring a sales presentation or new-business pitch.
**Steps:**
1. Yourself: a short origin story showing identity, compassion and why you love this work.
2. Company: its story, mission and integrity, tied to values the buyer shares.
3. Product or service: dramatic client case studies, each bridged with a statement of what it means for this buyer.
**Common mistakes:** Jumping straight to features, which puts you in a commodity contest.
**Example:** A veterinary-software seller opens with how a rescued greyhound led her into the field, then tells how her company was founded by two vets tired of lost records, and only then shows one clinic's before and after.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F231 · Elements of a winning business presentation
**Also known as:** Five Principles of Getting to Yes (Livesay)
**Use when:** Designing a formal new-business presentation.
**Steps:**
1. Vision: your differentiating mission beside the client's vision and their gain.
2. Team: why each person chose the company and how their background helps the client.
3. Culture: link your values and passion to theirs.
4. Traction: show product and company momentum, and build relationship momentum up the ladder in F227 with useful ideas.
5. Landing the plane: sit beside the buyer like a co-pilot so the move to yes feels like a planned descent.
**Example:** A branding studio pitching a food bank states its mission to cut waste, introduces the designer who once volunteered there, shows the local campaign that doubled donors, and ends by asking which launch date the board prefers.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F232 · Calm objection handling
**Also known as:** The Dance: Five Tools for Overcoming Objections (Livesay)
**Use when:** A buyer or investor raises an objection or a tough question.
**Steps:**
1. Pause after the objection, and restate the concern to put yourself in slow motion.
2. Slow your pace and lower your tone so the buyer's state follows yours.
3. Talk less: no defensive monologue; keep the earlier back-and-forth rhythm.
4. Clarify with a question that reflects what you heard, answer, then ask whether it fully answered.
5. Ask whether it makes sense and where they think you should go next.
**Common mistakes:** Blurting a quick answer; speeding up; talking for minutes.
**Example:** A buyer at a school district says the pilot sounds too long. The vendor pauses, says she hears worry about teacher workload, asks if that is right, offers a shorter first month, and checks it addresses the worry.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F233 · Feel, felt, found
**Also known as:** Feel, Felt, Found (Livesay)
**Use when:** Common objections such as price, needing time, or missing features.
**Steps:**
1. Feel: acknowledge the emotion without agreeing the objection is valid.
2. Felt: say other clients felt the same at first.
3. Found: tell what those clients found, with a specific result.
**Example:** A prospect says a bike-fleet service costs too much. The rep says she understands, that a bakery owner felt the same, and that after six months the bakery found repairs fell by half and deliveries stopped slipping.
**Sources:** Livesay

## F234 · Client win-back process
**Also known as:** Repair and Rebuild (R&R) Client Win-Back Process (Livesay)
**Use when:** A valuable former client left over a rival, a missed deadline, quality or a rift.
**Avoid when:** The client was unprofitable, abusive or a slow payer and you are relieved they left.
**Steps:**
1. Manage your own self-talk; choose winning them back over being right.
2. Listen like a doctor with a notepad: let them vent, write every grievance, read it back, find the wound under the complaint.
3. At the first meeting skip decks, take full responsibility even if a predecessor erred, and describe the new systems.
4. While they decide, treat them as family with congratulations, referrals and small gestures.
5. Ask for the business, hold the silence, and once they return, agree the past is settled.
**Common mistakes:** Arguing the facts; bringing market data to the apology meeting.
**Example:** A print shop lost a theatre company after botching a poster run. The owner listens for an hour, owns the error, shows a new proof-approval checklist, sends opening-night flowers during the cooling period, then asks for the next season's work.
**Sources:** Livesay
