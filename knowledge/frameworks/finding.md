# Frameworks — finding

## F16 · Story mining
**Also known as:** Story Collecting and Story Choosing (Hall); Work-Related and Non-Work-Related Story Tables; Everyday trivial-to-triumph mining (Dolan); Seven Techniques for Finding Stories (Simmons); Three Story Source Buckets: our stories, other people's stories, stories from other sources (Callahan); Relational Fragment Pairing (Guptara); Machine-Free Photo Prompting (Forman)
**Use when:** Someone says they have no stories, you are building a first story bank, or you need fresh material for a talk, pitch or leadership message.
**Avoid when:** You already hold a well-tagged bank and only need to choose; go straight to selection (F19, F20, F21).
**Steps:**
1. Separate collecting from choosing. List fragments without judging whether they are good or shareable.
2. Prompt with concrete nouns: people, places, objects, events. Add firsts, every job since your first casual one, mistakes, turning points, frustrations, customer objections, moments you saw your message working, and a favorite film, book or folktale you could retell from your angle.
3. Run the lenses: recurring patterns, efforts with notable consequences, painful lessons, stories that changed you, moments of vulnerability, imagined futures fleshed out as scenes with real people.
4. Work two tables: one row per job and one row per life experience, with columns for triumph, tragedy, tension and transition (F20). Tick at most two columns per fragment.
5. Count the columns and mine again where a category is thin.
6. Widen the sources: your own experiences, other people's stories (ask permission and credit them), and published stories.
7. Pair two fragments that show a before and after in your relationship to a person or issue.
8. For technical teams, show photos of people with no products in them and ask which image brings back a memory.
9. Only now choose: define the audience and what they should think, feel, know or do (T022), then pick the fragment that serves it.
10. Protect new fragments: say what works in them before critiquing.
**Common mistakes:** Judging fragments while collecting; choosing the most moving story rather than the one that drives the needed action; borrowing a colleague's story without credit; mining only work triumphs, which sounds boastful.
**Example:** An operations lead claims she has nothing to tell. Listing objects, she writes down the brass key to her first shop's back door; listing jobs, a night shift at a bakery. The key reminds her of the day she locked herself out before a delivery and the driver helped her climb through a window. Paired with her current rollout of shared access codes, it becomes a story about designing processes that survive a bad morning.
**Sources:** Hall; Dolan; Simmons; Callahan; Guptara; Forman

## F17 · Story listening
**Also known as:** Six Conditions for Stories to Be Told; Pretend-Insider Prompt; Post-It Project Timeline; Triangulating Meaning (Callahan); Storylistening / Verbal Download; Pre-decision story round (Simmons); Blank Yellow Pad Client Opening (Forman); Good-Cop-Only Listening (Maxwell)
**Use when:** You need stories from colleagues, clients or opponents: discovery meetings, interviews, retrospectives, culture diagnosis, or a resistant or angry group before you present your own view.
**Avoid when:** You plan to use what you hear as a trap to prove someone wrong; the method fails and burns trust.
**Steps:**
1. Set the six conditions: show real curiosity, clear unhurried time, establish common ground, tell a short story of your own first, meet near artefacts (maps, whiteboards, photos, objects), and make it safe by promising not to misuse what you hear.
2. If the teller summarizes because they assume you lack the background, ask them to talk as if you were an experienced insider.
3. Ask when and where questions rather than why or how (T017): when did this last happen, where were you, who was there, what happened next.
4. With an angry or resistant teller, let them vent without interrupting. Then steer gently from their conclusion back to the raw event, and hold silence so they can hear their own story and correct it.
5. For a project review, put a long paper timeline on the wall, have the team stick notes on key moments, and ask what happened at each.
6. Before a contested vote, invite each stakeholder to tell the history behind their misgivings.
7. With a new client, arrive with only a blank pad, write down their concerns, then tell the story that fits them.
8. With an opponent, offer to hear their side and listen calmly for small contradictions.
9. Collect several stories from different vantage points before you draw a conclusion.
**Common mistakes:** Rigid agendas that leave no room; why-questions that produce opinions; analytical interruptions that stop the story; drawing conclusions from one anecdote.
**Example:** A new finance director hears that the sales team distrusts the expense system. Instead of presenting fixes, she sits with three reps at their desks, tells how she once lost a receipt on a client trip, and asks when the system last let them down. One describes resubmitting a claim four times from a hotel lobby. Hearing similar accounts from a manager and an assistant, she learns the real problem is the mobile upload, not the policy.
**Sources:** Callahan; Simmons; Forman; Maxwell

## F18 · Collecting customer stories
**Also known as:** Curating Customer Stories: Two Rules; Leave Scratches in the Record (Hall); Post-Meeting Phone Photo-and-Note Capture (Forman)
**Use when:** You need authentic testimonials for a website, ads, social media, sales decks or a customer success story (S09).
**Avoid when:** You intend to script or polish the result into marketing copy; it will read as fake.
**Steps:**
1. Ask. Send a personal request signed by a named person, not a system address, a few days after the customer has actually used the product. Optionally offer a small thank-you for a video.
2. Ask questions that follow the arc of F01: what they used before and why they switched, what trying it was like, how things are now, and where they were when they first used it.
3. In B2B, capture right after each customer meeting: take a photo and note the customer's problem and outcome while it is fresh.
4. Store every story in a shared, searchable repository tagged by industry, problem and outcome (T019).
5. Edit lightly. Keep small verbal slips, laughs and quirky specifics; cut only for length and clarity.
6. Get permission to publish and keep the customer's own words.
**Common mistakes:** Asking for a star rating instead of a story; asking before the customer has used the product; generic prompts that produce generic praise; over-editing until the voice disappears.
**Example:** A bicycle repair subscription emails riders a week after their first service, signed by the mechanic who did the work. It asks what they rode before, what the first service was like, how riding feels now, and where they took the bike first. One reply describes riding to a daughter's graduation with the chain finally silent, typo included; it goes on the homepage as written.
**Sources:** Hall; Forman
