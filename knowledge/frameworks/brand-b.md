# Frameworks — brand (F299–F309)

## F299 · Visceral, behavioral, reflective layers
**Also known as:** Don Norman's Three Layers of User Experience (Lupton)
**Use when:** Reviewing a product or experience from first impression through use to afterward.
**Steps:**
1. Visceral (beginning): shape the immediate reaction to form, color, texture and material.
2. Behavioral (middle): make the actions (clicking, pushing, reading, leaving) clear and satisfying.
3. Reflective (end): shape the memories, meanings and personal associations that remain afterward.
**Common mistakes:** Treating the layers as separate; they overlap and influence each other.
**Example:** A bike-share kiosk gets a bright, tactile handlebar tag (visceral), a one-tap unlock and a clear return dock (behavioral), and a trip summary with the route and calories saved, sent after the ride (reflective).
**Sources:** Lupton

## F300 · Staging an experience
**Also known as:** Experience Economy (Lupton)
**Use when:** Differentiating in a crowded market or turning a transactional service into a participatory brand story.
**Avoid when:** Corporate theater would contradict real business practices; customers are not passive audiences and social media punishes the gap (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. Climb the value ladder: commodity (bulk), product (standardized), service (customized), experience (personal, revealed over time).
2. Turn the noun into an activity: food becomes dining, cars become driving.
3. Stage the interaction as theater: actors, script, special vocabulary, sensory atmosphere, and guest participation both ways.
**Common mistakes:** Staging from the top down without letting guests take part.
**Example:** A flour mill sells sacks (product). It adds baking classes (service), then a Saturday "bake-along" where staff wear aprons with guests' names, use mill slang, and everyone shapes loaves together under warm light (experience).
**Sources:** Lupton

## F301 · Multisensory design
**Also known as:** Multisensory Design (Lupton)
**Use when:** Designing packaging, food and drink brands, physical spaces, type, or products for blind or neurodivergent users.
**Avoid when:** Stimulation is piled on; strong scent, heat or volume can overwhelm users with autism or dementia. Taste and smell vocabularies also differ by region and culture (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. List every nonvisual touchpoint: weight, temperature, surface, grip, sound, scent, mouth-feel.
2. Pair color, shape and type with sensory expectations, such as rounded forms for sweet, angular or rough type for sour or salty.
3. Make forms and caps distinguishable without sight, and cap the intensity.
**Common mistakes:** Designing for the eye only.
**Example:** A tea brand gives its sleeping blend a soft matte tin and rounded lettering, and a citrus blend a ridged lid. The two tins feel different in a dark cupboard, and the scent is mild.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F302 · Storytelling Maturity Ladder
**Also known as:** Story Design Thinking (Sommer Paulsen); The Seven Levels of Evolution (Terenzzo); The 7 Evolutionary Narrative Types (Terenzzo)
**Use when:** Diagnosing how deeply story shapes an organisation and choosing the next capability or multi-year roadmap.
**Avoid when:** You need beat-level scene or dialogue craft; the ladder does not give it (Sommer Paulsen). Levels 5 and up need cross-functional alignment, contracts and about three months of lead time for live theater (Terenzzo).
**Steps:**
1. Place the organisation on Sommer Paulsen's design scale: none; styling (story added late as decoration); innovation process (multidisciplinary circular loops); creative strategy (story drives positioning); organisation (audience touchpoints and backstage unified around story).
2. Or place it on Terenzzo's narrative scale: fragments (tools, mascots); anecdotes; full dramatised plot where characters enact lessons; non-linear with branching and replay; transmedia (one story, media tailored per audience); managed spiral with monthly feedback across internal and external telling; ownable entertainment franchise.
3. For level 7, build in seven phases: foundation (character flaws, backstories), interlacing (villains, allies, artifacts, brand role), transmedia bible, storybook, narrative cuts per audience, telling with suspense, results and new cycles.
4. Work on whatever the next rung needs.
**Common mistakes:** Jumping to transmedia while story is still cosmetic.
**Example:** A pasta maker sits at rung 2: a mascot and a few founder anecdotes. Next it writes one dramatised short play about two rival grandmothers at the factory, in which each lesson is enacted. It stays at that rung until the play works, before planning a branching video game.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen; Terenzzo

## F303 · Narrative Scale Layers
**Also known as:** The Seven Layers of Narrative Scale / Story OmniVerse (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** Planning multi-platform expansion, sequencing short hooks into long-form worlds, or sorting canon from spin-offs.
**Avoid when:** The core is unproven; building outer layers first leads to narrative paralysis (Sommer Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. MicroVerse: instant sensory or short-form hooks.
2. StoryWorld: one unified fictional reality, such as a film or venue.
3. StoryVerse: sequels, prequels and spin-offs sharing fixed canon.
4. MultiVerse: parallel realities and alternate timelines from shared elements.
5. MegaVerse: crossovers and festivals uniting properties without strict continuity.
6. Interface: a persistent digital, XR or metaverse bridge that stores user progress and user-created content.
7. OmniVerse: everything that exists around the story, including official works, contradictions, mistakes and fan creations.
**Common mistakes:** Over-engineering outer layers before the core is validated.
**Example:** A sock company posts 15-second clips of a sock thief (MicroVerse), opens a pop-up laundromat where the thief's story unfolds (StoryWorld), and keeps visitors' collected clues in an app (Interface). Sequels wait until the first venue works.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen

## F304 · Brand Mythology System
**Also known as:** Brand Mythology System (Vincent)
**Use when:** Diagnosing or designing the foundation of a meaning-driven, high-involvement brand.
**Avoid when:** The brand sells on impulse, low involvement or pure function; buyers seek no deeper meaning (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Worldview: state the non-negotiable beliefs that go beyond specs and unite insiders against nonbelievers.
2. Brand agent: tie those beliefs to a person, place or thing that people can touch, buy or experience.
3. Brand narrative: bind worldview to agent, weighting logic with emotion and prescribing aspirational behavior.
4. Culture: foster tribes, shared rituals that produce liminality (suspended status, unity) and visible symbols.
**Common mistakes:** Applying it to a commodity.
**Example:** A small climbing-gear maker believes risk is best taken with a partner. Its founder, a guide, is the agent; a story casts the brand as the rope between two climbers. Members hold knot-tying nights and wear a rope-loop pin.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F305 · Brand in the Consumer's Self-Story
**Also known as:** Legendary Brands and Personal Narrative (Vincent)
**Use when:** Positioning a brand around real rituals, life stages or aspirational peer groups.
**Avoid when:** You expect a tidy single identity; people juggle competing narratives and mix brands unpredictably (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Before: match the brand's theme to the consumer's own identity theme.
2. Plot: give entry and exit bookends (liminality) and ritual routines that sequence the experience.
3. Character: act as a visible prop or costume, or as an unseen mentor or ally.
4. Aesthetics: deliver consistent cues for all five senses that feel real to the wearer and to onlookers.
5. After: reinforce the chosen identity through peer feedback and imitation.
**Common mistakes:** Ignoring that identity work is nonlinear.
**Example:** A running-club apparel line matches the theme "late bloomer." Its Sunday long-run ritual begins with a start-line bell and ends with a shared breakfast. The jacket is the costume, nods from other runners reinforce the identity.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F306 · Brand Bible
**Also known as:** Brand Bible Crafting and Four Elements of Brand Narrative (Vincent); The Box (Vincent); Four-Slide Consumer Biography (Vincent); Brand Soundtrack and Image Library (Vincent); Annotated Narrative History (Vincent)
**Use when:** Setting or overhauling brand strategy before ads, launches or agency briefs.
**Avoid when:** Top-down builds can feel sterile; bottom-up builds can lose focus without disciplined synthesis (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Plot: choose a genre (heist, romantic comedy, epic), a three-act trajectory, inciting incident, climax, the gap between expectation and outcome, and the elixir.
2. Character: three layers, the brand archetype, three-dimensional consumer biographies, and concrete brand agents.
3. Theme: one governing theme pairing a core value with its cause, with subordinate themes.
4. Aesthetics: sensory style across sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, held in an image library and soundtrack.
5. Pick a path: top-down (theme, then archetype, plot, aesthetics) or bottom-up (collages and biographies leading to a theme).
6. Compile with a glossary, annotated history and a Box diagram.
7. Tools: sort traits inside or outside the brand world in the Box; turn research into four-slide named portraits with contradictions, goals and rituals; map real past breakthroughs and crises against cultural events before drafting new arcs.
**Common mistakes:** Demographic bullets instead of portraits.
**Example:** A craft-chocolate brand picks the genre "caper." Its Box excludes corporate gloss. Four-slide portraits include Mira, a night-shift baker who hides squares in her apron. The playlist is jazz brushes, and the history marks the 2008 recession crisis.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F307 · Growing Brand Ritual and Culture
**Also known as:** Semiotics, Slow Burn and Consumer Ritual Hierarchy (Vincent)
**Use when:** Managing a surge in adoption, symbol use or community rituals without a backlash.
**Avoid when:** The plan is to command culture; heavy-handed control destroys authenticity (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Semiotics: put marks where the story actually happens, or give them to believers to display.
2. Slow burn: pace distribution, reward early adopters, protect scarcity, keep core rituals, deepen narrative layers.
3. Ritual (Rook): design or support artifacts, scripts, performance roles and audience feedback.
4. Move up the artifact ladder: start with props anyone in the category uses, then tools particular to the category, and finish with objects that only this brand makes indispensable.
**Common mistakes:** Loud corporate proclamation instead of communal gathering.
**Example:** A cold-plunge studio hands loyal members a free brass bell. Members ring it after their first plunge and a regular crowd cheers. The studio limits new memberships each month so the ritual survives.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F308 · Partnership Narrative Fit
**Also known as:** Partnership Trinity, Partnership Brand Audit, and Planning the Partnership Narrative (Vincent)
**Use when:** Judging co-branding, sponsorships, naming rights, licensing and retail alliances.
**Avoid when:** The motive is exposure, reducing ad clutter or quick cash; such deals turn into signage and invite ambush marketing (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Overlap the two brands' beliefs in a Venn diagram to confirm shared sacred beliefs and a joint story.
2. Draw the trinity: consumer at the apex above Partner A and Partner B.
3. Audit each side on benefits, value, delivery and boundaries.
4. Check systemic value across narrative, culture, distribution, communications and goals.
5. Assign a governing team of one to three people.
**Common mistakes:** Signing without narrative fit.
**Example:** A trail-running event and an outdoor-socks brand both believe in "slow miles." They plan a joint finish-line ritual of a sock swap, audit what each gains, and name two owners to govern it.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F309 · Brand Agent Strength Matrix
**Also known as:** Strength of Brand Agents Matrix (Vincent)
**Use when:** Auditing spokespeople, charismatic founders, flagship venues or hero products.
**Avoid when:** Renting agents indefinitely without owning a narrative locks you into permanent talent spend (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Plot the brand on a 2x2: narrative strength against agent strength.
2. Strong narrative, strong agent: create new arcs, avoid oversaturation, diversify agents, prepare an exit plan.
3. Weak narrative, strong agent: find why people love the agent, uncover the worldview behind it, then diversify.
4. Strong narrative but a feeble messenger: enlist a CEO as sponsor, pass the narrative research to product and operations teams, or borrow unexpected outside voices.
5. Weak narrative, weak agent: build the worldview first, renting short-term agents to borrow attention.
**Common mistakes:** Loving the product while lacking a worldview, which leaves it exposed when rivals match the hardware.
**Example:** A sneaker brand's one star athlete drives sales but no story exists (weak narrative, strong agent). The team interviews buyers, finds a worldview of "quiet comeback," then adds two non-athlete agents: a coach and a flagship store.
**Sources:** Vincent
