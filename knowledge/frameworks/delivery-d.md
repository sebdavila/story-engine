# Frameworks — delivery (F214–F223)

## F214 · Oral-first drafting
**Also known as:** Talking Out Loud: The Middle Path (Maguire); Oral Tape-Recorded First Draft (Haven)
**Use when:** Turning a raw memory or storyboard into spoken language for the first time, before any prose exists.
**Avoid when:** You need a polished script in one pass; this method only produces raw spoken material.
**Steps:**
1. Pick a private space where nobody will interrupt.
2. Think silently through the key scenes before speaking.
3. Speak the story aloud for at least 20 minutes, or record two or three passionate passes (Haven). Maguire offers four modes: storyteller, conversational, interviewer, think-aloud. Pause freely and let the tale continue on its own.
4. Ignore grammar. Stop at a definite ending and hold all written notes until you finish.
5. Only now write or transcribe. Haven transcribes the best pass sentence by sentence.
**Common mistakes:** Waiting passively for the story to arrive, or reading from a rigid written script (Maguire).
**Example:** Dev, a bakery owner, walks the garage alone and talks through the night his oven died before a wedding order. He records three passes on his phone, never stopping to fix wording. The second pass contains the line about burning his sleeve, which he types out afterward.
**Sources:** Maguire; Haven

## F215 · Storyboard of scene shifts
**Also known as:** Storyboarding (Maguire)
**Use when:** Locking structure after oral drafting without writing full paragraphs; you want a modular map you can recall mid-telling.
**Avoid when:** You would fill the panels with prose; that defeats the visual memory advantage.
**Steps:**
1. Draw one comic-style panel for every change of scene, viewpoint or subject.
2. Use simple realistic sketches, stick figures or abstract memory symbols, plus a few trigger words.
3. Annotate the panels to test additions, cuts and reorderings.
**Common mistakes:** Overwriting text inside panels (Maguire).
**Example:** A nurse prepares a story about her first night shift. She draws six panels: the empty parking lot, the charge nurse's nod, the alarm, the wrong chart, the apology, the dawn coffee. Testing a cut, she crosses out the parking lot and finds the story starts faster.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F216 · One story adapted to many rooms
**Also known as:** Knapsack Tales (Maguire)
**Use when:** You need one dependable story for different audiences, such as family dinner, a team meeting and a civic event.
**Avoid when:** The workplace version would turn preachy, long-winded or overly confessional.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a lived incident that carries universal human tension.
2. Adjust framing or intro, emphasized details, tone and length for the room: brief, simple and pointed at work; personal at home; inclusive for a community.
3. Cut subplots that do not serve that room, and keep the emotional core intact.
**Common mistakes:** Letting the work version drift into sermon or confession (Maguire).
**Example:** Lena once got lost hiking and was guided out by a stranger. For her team she tells it in ninety seconds about asking for directions early. For her sister she keeps the fear and the shared laughter. At the town fundraiser she stresses the stranger's kindness.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F217 · Charisma tactics for speakers
**Also known as:** Charisma-Building Playbook, Antonakis study techniques (Cody)
**Use when:** Building or rehearsing a keynote, pitch or all-hands talk to hold a distracted room.
**Avoid when:** A tactic clashes with your natural personality; it will read as fake.
**Steps:**
1. Use metaphors to capture imagination.
2. Tell stories and anecdotes from your own journey.
3. State moral conviction to signal shared values.
4. Voice shared feelings so listeners feel understood.
5. Expect a lot of the group, which signals that you believe in them.
6. Use contrasts to separate yourself from the competition or the norm.
7. Use lists; research finds them effective, though the authors cannot say why.
8. Ask rhetorical questions to draw listeners into your reasoning.
9. Animate the words with body language, facial expression and vocal tone.
10. Keep the combination that fits you; performers find theirs by repeated trial.
**Common mistakes:** Adopting every tactic at once instead of selecting those that suit your temperament (Cody).
**Example:** A plant manager opens a safety all-hands by comparing the line to a river with hidden rocks, then names the fear everyone shares of a call home. She sets the bar: zero injuries this quarter. Afterward she keeps the metaphor and drops the rhetorical questions that felt stagey.
**Sources:** Cody

## F218 · Stand-up comedy workshop for leaders
**Also known as:** Three-Hour Stand-Up Comedy Training Module (Cody)
**Use when:** Breaking status barriers on a team or coaching leaders to command a room authentically.
**Avoid when:** Material would breach the organization's cultural or inclusion boundaries; adapt delivery for introverts.
**Steps:**
1. Open with a short lecture and clean business-safe examples that show stand-up is about truth, not raunch.
2. Lead a guided writing brainstorm in which each person lands on at least one personal irritation or story premise, with coaches moving around the room.
3. Pause for 5-10 minutes to let anticipation grow.
4. The most senior person takes the mic first for 1-2 minutes and gets immediate encouraging feedback on clarity, timing, body language and vulnerability.
5. Volunteers follow one by one with warm peer applause.
6. Debrief how to carry the room's trust into next-day work.
**Common mistakes:** Letting juniors go first; the leader's early risk sets the tone (Cody).
**Example:** At a logistics firm's retreat, the regional director goes first with a true tale about locking herself out of her own warehouse on day one. Coaches praise her pause before the punch line. Twelve staff follow, and the debrief produces a rule that managers share one failure each month.
**Sources:** Cody

## F219 · Roll: stacking punch lines on one premise
**Also known as:** Roll Structure (Cody)
**Use when:** The audience already laughed at or agreed with your setup and is ready to ride the premise further.
**Avoid when:** Listeners reject the opening premise; also stop before the bit wears out.
**Steps:**
1. State one clear setup naming the subject and your attitude toward it.
2. Land a short, surprising punch line that wins buy-in on the premise.
3. Keep squeezing the same premise with three to five escalating variations before moving on.
**Common mistakes:** Resetting to a new topic after every laugh; running the roll past the room's energy (Cody).
**Example:** A finance lead tells the staff that the new expense portal has the personality of a locked door. She follows with the receipt upload that rejects every photo, the approval that arrives after retirement, and the password hint that says only no. The room laughs three times, and she moves on to the budget.
**Sources:** Cody

## F220 · Improv workshop for listening and building
**Also known as:** Three-Hour Improv Comedy Training Module (Cody)
**Use when:** Teams talk over each other, kill wild ideas in brainstorms, or freeze when a client meeting goes off script.
**Avoid when:** Facilitators cannot promise physical, theatrical games stay within comfort zones; conservative teams will be put off.
**Steps:**
1. Teach the core rules: acceptance, building, teamwork, active listening, first-best idea, positivity, bold choices, statements not questions. Show how yes-but kills a scene.
2. Play short-form games in small groups, such as Yes And, One-Word Story, Excuses, Cocktail Party and First Letter Last Letter, and coach the behaviours instead of performing.
3. After a break, play unscripted long-form scenes around one core idea, each with an opening, a complication and a resolution.
4. Debrief on how those mechanics settle particular workplace frictions that came up during the exercises.
**Common mistakes:** Games that make people crawl or make noises (Cody).
**Example:** A software support team that interrupts each other in escalations plays One-Word Story, then a long scene about a failing food truck. In the debrief an agent notices she always answers a colleague's idea with but. The team agrees to open replies with and for the next incident review.
**Sources:** Cody

## F221 · Five presentation phases
**Also known as:** The five presentation phases (Parikh)
**Use when:** Planning the flow of any live or virtual presentation or keynote.
**Avoid when:** The audience already knows you well; skip the credibility phase unless a little-known experience matters.
**Steps:**
1. Open in the first 30 seconds with a hook instead of thanks or self-introduction: a bold statement, question, quotation, surprising statistic, curiosity build-up, what-if, imagine scenario or personal story.
2. Establish credibility with credentials that are objective, tangible, relevant and concise, or as a short career story.
3. State the value: what is in it for the audience and which problem the session solves.
4. Deliver the main body: the story blueprint's sections, enriched with metaphors, anecdotes, sensory detail, characters and planned interaction checkpoints.
5. Close with a deliberate impression: a summary with a fresh angle, one emphatic takeaway, or a call to action with exact next steps.
**Common mistakes:** Starting with thanks and self-introduction (Parikh).
**Example:** Priya pitches a route-planning tool to a bus operator. She opens with a stranded commuter's wait time, gives two named city rollouts and their results, ties it to the operator's missed-schedule penalties, walks the demo with two question stops, and ends by asking for a pilot on one line by June 1.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F222 · Stage zones for presence
**Also known as:** The six stage zones (Parikh)
**Use when:** Presenting in person in a room with a screen.
**Avoid when:** Presenting online; fill the camera frame instead.
**Steps:**
1. Treat front centre as the power zone and stand there for your opening and closing words.
2. During the body, move calmly and unhurriedly among the three front zones (left, centre, right) to engage different parts of the room.
3. Treat the three rear zones as dead zones. Enter one only briefly to point at the screen, then return to the front.
**Common mistakes:** Lingering at the back, which weakens presence through distance (Parikh).
**Example:** In a ten-seat meeting room, Omar opens and closes at front centre, spends the middle section at front left and right, and steps back to the screen just once to tap a chart before returning.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F223 · Tribute built on one trait and one story
**Also known as:** Toast, Tribute, and Eulogy Guide (Biesenbach); Toast, Tribute, or Eulogy Story (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** Preparing a wedding toast, retirement tribute, farewell or eulogy.
**Avoid when:** Treating it as a rigid template; at weddings avoid inside jokes and roast humour, and allow raw grief time to settle before speaking.
**Steps:**
1. Speak from memories that truly matter to you.
2. Narrow the honoree to two to four specific traits, ideally three, not generic superlatives.
3. Choose one trait as the theme.
4. Tell one focused story that shows it, with sensory detail and clutter trimmed.
5. Close with the lesson or how the person changed your life.
6. Optionally frame it with a personally meaningful quote, within the time limit.
7. Deliver from physical notes, pause and breathe if choked up, apologize minimally, keep humour kind and sober, and use selective eye contact.
8. For a complicated relationship, find one admirable trait or report others' stories neutrally rather than faking praise.
**Common mistakes:** Reciting a long list of adjectives (Biesenbach).
**Example:** For his retiring bus driver colleague Tomas, Carlos picks three traits: punctual, patient, quietly funny. He tells only the snowstorm morning Tomas waited twenty minutes so a child could find a lost glove. He ends with how that patience changed how Carlos treats passengers.
**Sources:** Biesenbach
