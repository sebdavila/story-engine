# Frameworks — structure (F001–F011)

## F001 · Five-act dramatic pyramid
**Also known as:** Freytag's Pyramid, five-act form (Quesenberry); Narrative Arc (Lupton); Freytag's Pyramid (James); Dykes's data storytelling arc (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** You need one master template that scales from a single ad, chart or onboarding flow to a campaign, speech or report, and the audience must stay engaged to a peak and a release.
**Avoid when:** The piece is a very short format with room for one moment only (spread the five beats over a short series instead), or the audience is peer researchers who want the method first (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Exposition: set the baseline, characters and stakes; end on the inciting moment or call to action.
2. Rising action: stack obstacles and effort; nest small arcs inside the rise (Lupton).
3. Climax: place the peak of tension, effort or insight. It need not sit in the exact middle (James).
4. Falling action: show consequences moving toward the outcome.
5. Resolution: release tension and show the new equilibrium; add poetic justice if the story allows (James).
6. For data, cast the people in the data as characters, make the key insight the climax and the recommendations the resolution (Feigenbaum).
**Common mistakes:** Opening with how the analysis was done; slow exposition online; no stakes the audience cares about; no obstacles at all, which leaves no arc.
**Example:** A bakery chain reports why its loyalty app is failing. Exposition: a night-shift nurse who wants breakfast after work. Rising action: three failed log-ins and a lost reward. Climax: the finding that 60% of sign-ups die at the phone-verification screen. Resolution: drop that screen, with a date and an owner.
**Sources:** Quesenberry; Lupton; James; Feigenbaum

## F002 · Unified causal plot: beginning, middle, end
**Also known as:** Aristotle's Theory of Drama (Quesenberry); Whole Plot and Magnitude (Aristotle); Complication and Resolution, desis and lusis (Aristotle)
**Use when:** You must test whether a narrative is one tight action or a bag of scenes, or fix where the knot starts and unties.
**Avoid when:** You are deliberately telling a serial or flashback structure and only need causal clarity, not strict chronology (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Choose one complete action to follow, not a whole life (Aristotle).
2. Beginning: an event that needs nothing before it but leads onward. Middle: follows and causes. End: follows and needs nothing after.
3. Size it: long enough for fortune to turn, short enough to hold in mind at once.
4. Split it into complication (everything up to the turn, including relevant backstory) and resolution (from the turn to the end).
5. Make the resolution grow from the complication, not from an outside fix.
6. Delete or move any scene whose removal breaks nothing.
7. Check characters are worthy of respect, fitting, believable and consistent (Quesenberry).
**Common mistakes:** Strong complication, weak untying; a rescue that arrives from outside the story; biography padding.
**Example:** A repair-shop owner's story covers only the day a customer's wedding-dress zipper failed. Her late-night fix comes from a skill planted earlier, sewing sail covers as a teen. Her trip to Lisbon that summer is cut because nothing depends on it.
**Sources:** Aristotle; Quesenberry

## F003 · Engineered reversal and recognition
**Also known as:** Clash Expectation with Reality (Rubin); Complex Plot Mechanics: reversal, recognition, suffering (Aristotle)
**Use when:** Building a twist or turning point that should surprise yet feel inevitable on review.
**Avoid when:** The twist would break character logic or merely withhold information to trick the audience (Rubin); a simple linear shift of fortune is enough (Aristotle).
**Steps:**
1. Show the expectation clearly through concrete action.
2. Make it fully reasonable for what the character knows.
3. Make the audience care about the expected outcome.
4. Misdirect so the audience relaxes.
5. Deliver the clash: surprising, believable in hindsight, revealing of character, fitting the genre, and opening a new question (Rubin).
6. Make the reversal (an intention turning into its opposite) and the recognition (ignorance to knowledge) flow from earlier events, ideally in one event (Aristotle).
7. Show the cost (suffering) when the turn needs weight.
**Common mistakes:** A reveal with no earlier cause; a twist that changes nothing about who the character is.
**Example:** A clinic director expects the audit to praise her new triage rule. We watch a flawless week of numbers. The auditor shows that the rule sent night-shift patients away, and she realizes her pride in the metric hid it. Fortune and understanding flip together.
**Sources:** Rubin; Aristotle

## F004 · Therefore / but causal linking
**Also known as:** Therefore / But / Meanwhile chain (Rubin); Kenn Adams's Story Spine (Edwards); Principle of But and Therefore (Dicks); Parker and Stone Rule (Simson); Plot, Three Cs (Sandler); connective words (Miller)
**Use when:** Plotting, auditing a step outline, editing transitions, or keeping a group or oral story moving.
**Avoid when:** The piece is intentionally non-linear or surreal; do not label every link in a rough outline (Rubin; Simson).
**Steps:**
1. Write the main event of each beat in order (Simson).
2. Test each link: therefore (A forces B) or but (A causes B, yet C blocks it). Rewrite, reorder or cut any link that is only and then.
3. For parallel storylines, use meanwhile and hold the cut together with a shared motif or action (Rubin).
4. State things in the negative to imply an alternative, and end a run of buts with one short positive line if useful (Dicks).
5. To align a team, use the fairy-tale spine (see F022), then remove the scaffold words before delivery (Edwards).
6. Live, drop connectives such as meanwhile, but and later to keep a shared plot moving (Miller).
**Common mistakes:** Flat chronology; saying the scaffold phrases aloud in a business talk; negative phrasing in plain factual answers (Dicks).
**Example:** A trainer's story: the scheduling tool froze at 8 a.m., therefore Ana phoned each driver by hand, but half were already on the highway, therefore she texted the depot, but the depot had no signal. Each beat forces the next.
**Sources:** Rubin; Edwards; Dicks; Simson; Sandler; Miller

## F005 · Hero's journey
**Also known as:** Vogler's Hero's Journey and Save the Cat (Edwards); Campbell and Harmon's Story Circle (Quesenberry); Hero's Journey (Lupton; Barker & Brown; Sandler; Snow & Lazauskas)
**Use when:** Telling a transformation: customer case, founder origin, brand anthem, long client story, or a journey through a space or service.
**Avoid when:** A rigid single path would feel forced (Lupton), or the story is a multi-generational collective one (Barker & Brown). Never cast your company as the hero except in a mission story (Edwards).
**Steps:**
1. Ordinary world; add an early moment that makes the hero likeable (Edwards).
2. Call to adventure; allow a refusal (Snow & Lazauskas).
3. Mentor gives the tool or nerve to start; the hero crosses a no-return threshold.
4. Tests, allies and enemies, then approach to the inmost cave.
5. Supreme ordeal: near defeat, doubt, an old belief dropped.
6. Reward, road back, resurrection.
7. Return with the elixir that improves the home world.
8. Check the cast for archetypes (hero, shadow, herald, mentor, shapeshifter, trickster, allies) so each serves the spine (Barker & Brown).
9. Compressed option: Harmon's circle (you, need, go, search, find, take, return, change) maps onto five acts (Quesenberry).
**Common mistakes:** Skipping struggle and stakes and praising the product; making the brand the hero; following stages mechanically.
**Example:** A cycling-shop customer, Ravi, is stuck commuting by car. A neighbor mentors him onto a used bike. He nearly quits after a flat tire in the rain, then finishes his first week and returns to organize the street's bike pool.
**Sources:** Edwards; Quesenberry; Lupton; Barker & Brown; Sandler; Snow & Lazauskas

## F006 · Character, goal, obstacle, struggle, outcome map
**Also known as:** Four Parts of a Good Story (Livesay); Story Grammar and Story Map (Quesenberry); Character, Goal, Challenge (Biesenbach); C, C, S and G (Haven)
**Use when:** Outlining before scripting, or testing whether an anecdote is a story or just an incident.
**Avoid when:** You need visual or tonal craft; this is a diagnostic outline only (Quesenberry). It is not for essays or reports (Haven).
**Steps:**
1. Character and setting: a specific person, time, place, mood, in a normal state; enough detail to picture the scene, no more (Livesay).
2. Initiating event and internal response: the problem that triggers a feeling and a goal (Quesenberry).
3. Obstacles: problems plus the character's own flaws, with risk (odds of failing) and danger (cost of failing) (Haven).
4. Struggle: concrete attempts and stress reactions (Haven; Quesenberry).
5. Outcome and reaction: the result, the new normal and what it means; for selling, tie it to the buyer's situation (Livesay; Biesenbach).
6. If any link is missing, fix it before drafting.
**Common mistakes:** Too little exposition misleads, too much bores (Livesay); a goal with no jeopardy.
**Example:** Marta, a florist, must deliver 200 centerpieces by noon (goal). Her van dies and she is known for never asking help (flaw). She tries three fixes, then calls her rival. The wedding opens on time, and she now shares a delivery rota.
**Sources:** Livesay; Quesenberry; Biesenbach; Haven

## F007 · Dramatic question architecture
**Also known as:** Dramatic Questions Architecture (Rubin)
**Use when:** Outlining an arc, designing a B-story, or giving a single scene its own pull.
**Avoid when:** You expect the central question to be obvious live; internal or retrospective questions may only crystallize at the end (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Setup: fix the protagonist's baseline emotional state.
2. Ask: a disruption (see F008) poses the central question, will the protagonist get what they want. Choose external, internal, shifting or retrospective form.
3. Build: add secondary questions through subplots that light the core idea from another side, and a small question inside every scene.
4. Answer: the hero gets it, fails, or gets part of it at a transforming cost.
**Common mistakes:** Scenes with no question of their own; never answering the central one.
**Example:** A food-truck pitch: will Dev win the stadium contract (central)? Will his partner stay (secondary)? Each scene asks smaller things, such as will the generator start before the tasting. He wins half the contract at the price of the partner's dream menu.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F008 · Drop the hammer opening
**Also known as:** Drop the Hammer (Rubin)
**Use when:** Designing an opening that must lock the story onto its track.
**Avoid when:** A simple setup needs only a quick hammer; some stories use an early hammer mainly to set up a bigger one (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Show, do not state, what the hero feels now and values most.
2. Drop a surprising event, good or bad, that radically changes their fortune.
3. Build empathy for this person at this moment and urgency to act.
4. Set tone and genre, define a concrete object of desire, raise the central question (F007) and hint at possible endings.
**Common mistakes:** Hitting a hero we have not yet met; an event that changes nothing the hero cares about.
**Example:** A retired bus driver, Lena, proudly keeps a spotless route log. A city letter announces her route is cancelled in two weeks. Now we know what she loves, the clock is running, and the question is whether she saves it.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F009 · Launchpad and landing pad
**Also known as:** Launchpad and Landing Pad (Bowles)
**Use when:** Polishing the first lines and last beat of a spoken story.
**Avoid when:** Never memorize the middle; only the first and last lines (Bowles).
**Steps:**
1. Launchpad: begin inside a scene, a specific place and time, the key people or the heart of the stakes. No meta-preamble about what you will tell.
2. Plant early the need or conflict that the ending resolves.
3. Landing pad: either end on a final vivid moment that embodies the change, or on a brief wrap-up of your own change, with a call to action when you are advocating.
4. Memorize exactly the first and last lines.
**Common mistakes:** Announcing the story; an ending that resolves something never set up; rambling at the exit.
**Example:** A nurse opens with her hand on a locked supply-room door at 3 a.m., key missing. She plants the fear of asking for help. She ends on the sticky note she now keeps on that door: who has the key.
**Sources:** Bowles

## F010 · Two-step climax
**Also known as:** Two-Step Climax: Critical Decision and Confirmation (Rubin)
**Use when:** Structuring the final third, from roughly two-thirds to the last line.
**Avoid when:** The hero's choices would be accidents; both beats must be conscious (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Critical decision: after alternatives run out, the hero consciously picks a point-of-no-return action, knowing the cost.
2. Final pressure test: a last plea or blow offers one chance to back out.
3. Confirm or abandon the choice in a contrasting setting and tone, in a surprising way, answering the central question (F007).
4. Wrap up fast: tie loose ends and finish on one resonant image.
**Common mistakes:** Repeating the same setting and tone in both beats; a long epilogue.
**Example:** At the end of a quarterly review, Priya decides in the cold server room to shut down her own failing product. At her mother's birthday dinner, a colleague offers a rescue deal. She declines, and the story ends on her switching off the office light.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F011 · Risk escalation ladder
**Also known as:** Risk Escalation Ladder (Rubin)
**Use when:** Ordering obstacles and building tension toward the climax.
**Avoid when:** Do not read risk only literally; an emotional risk can outweigh a physical one (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Inventory the hero's possible losses, from minutes and reputation to livelihood, savings, bonds and survival, and rank the ones they fear most.
2. Brainstorm at least ten concrete risks they could take toward the goal.
3. Order them strictly from smallest to largest; never follow a peak with a smaller risk.
4. Stack later risks so one choice threatens physical, social and emotional stakes together.
**Common mistakes:** A flat list of equal risks; a big risk placed mid-story that makes later ones feel minor.
**Example:** A shy engineer wants to expose a safety flaw. Risks climb: ask a colleague, email her manager, interrupt the review, present to the client, and finally refuse to sign the release in front of her mentor, risking job, friendship and reputation at once.
**Sources:** Rubin
