# Frameworks — selection (F142–F150)

## F142 · One-sentence story filter
**Also known as:** Your One Sentence (Bowles)
**Use when:** You have a story seed and its stakes and need a filter for pruning drafts or cutting a two-minute pitch.
**Avoid when:** You are tempted to open the telling by announcing the sentence; saying it aloud as a premise spoils discovery.
**Steps:**
1. Pick one angle: you against yourself (inner struggle), you and another person (a relationship that shifts), or you against the world around you.
2. Write a single sentence holding the catalyst (what set the plot moving) and how you changed because of it.
3. Keep that sentence private. Never present it as a thesis.
4. Go through the draft and cut every scene, date, name or joke that does not serve the sentence.
**Common mistakes:** Stating the sentence out loud at the start; keeping a vivid detail that serves a different story.
**Example:** A bakery owner, Dana, wants to tell the night her oven failed before a wedding order. Her sentence: it took a dead oven for me to learn that my night cook was the real heart of the shop. She keeps the scene of the cook quietly rebuilding the batch by hand and drops the supplier dispute and the oven's model number. The audience never hears the sentence, but every remaining scene points to it.
**Sources:** Bowles

## F143 · Scar-not-wound readiness test
**Also known as:** Scars vs. Wounds Readiness Check (Bowles); Steps to Taking Life's Mishaps & Making Them into Story Magic (Leitman); The Crisis / Mishap Story (Leitman)
**Use when:** You are deciding whether to tell a story about grief, trauma, a breakup, illness, ongoing conflict, a career failure or an embarrassing mishap.
**Avoid when:** The trauma is unprocessed enough to need professional or specialized support; telling it is not a substitute (Bowles). Experienced performers may break the waiting rule, beginners should not (Leitman).
**Steps:**
1. Check for signs that the wound is still raw: deadlines slipping, plans changed at the last minute, losing your bearings while describing vivid scenes, dread of crying without end.
2. Ask whether the situation is truly over. Deaths and breakups can take years before the real arc shows.
3. Test whether you can retell it calmly, with no trembling, pounding pulse, angry tone or tears, and whether any humour is available in it.
4. Ask whether you can step back and name what the event was really about, the larger realization or pivot it triggered.
5. Draft it and speak it aloud. If your stomach knots and you lose command of the narration, answer not yet and wait weeks, months or years.
6. Tell it only when you control the material and the audience will leave uplifted rather than worried about you. Let the painful event be context, not the whole story.
**Common mistakes:** Telling the story while still knee-deep in the problem; ending without closure so listeners fear for you; making the trauma the entire story.
**Example:** Priya wants to tell about losing her restaurant to a flood. Six weeks on, she cracks when she reaches the water-in-the-kitchen scene, so she waits. A year later she can tell it, laughing at the soggy menus, and she sees the story is really about learning she could cook anywhere. She tells it as a mishap with a clear ending.
**Sources:** Bowles; Leitman

## F144 · Story selection criteria
**Also known as:** Evaluating a Story, Ten Selection Criteria (Haven)
**Use when:** You are choosing a traditional or personal story to add to your repertoire, especially when starting out.
**Avoid when:** The story is an authored literary tale where exact wording is the point; criterion 7 does not apply.
**Steps:**
1. Personal pull: did it hook you on first hearing and does it linger?
2. Character economy: prefer few characters, so there is less voice and emotion to track.
3. Brevity: start with short tales, even two-minute ones, that still carry strong intent, obstacles and detail.
4. Scene breaks: prefer sharp, distinct scenes to one continuous run.
5. Visible core: you can point to the character, intent, conflict, plot and key details.
6. Understanding: you understand what the characters feel, want and are driven by well enough to play them with ease.
7. Wording freedom: you can tell it in your own natural vocabulary.
8. Familiarity: for young children, a tale they already know lets them join in when invited.
9. Style fit: match the story's energy, quiet drama or raucous comedy, to your temperament.
10. Audience fit: characters, goals and obstacles suit the listeners' ages and interests.
**Common mistakes:** Choosing a story with a crowd of characters; choosing one that needs memorized phrasing; choosing a style you cannot deliver naturally.
**Example:** Tomas, a library volunteer, has two candidates for a school visit: a six-character quest and a short tale of a boy who keeps losing his umbrella. He scores both. The umbrella tale wins on length, scene breaks and character count, and its goofy tone suits him, so he learns that one.
**Sources:** Haven

## F145 · Three distinctions of imitation
**Also known as:** Three Distinctions of Imitation: Media, Objects, Manner (Aristotle)
**Use when:** You are starting a project and must fix form, point of view and the register of the characters.
**Avoid when:** The only change is surface polish; verse alone on a scientific subject does not make a work storytelling.
**Steps:**
1. Media, how: select the means of imitation, whether rhythm, spoken language (prose or verse), melody, or a blend.
2. Objects, who: settle whether the people in the action are superior to us, inferior to us, or average like us.
3. Manner, narration: tell it in a single voice, move between character voices, or have characters act it out directly.
4. Record the three choices and hold the draft to them.
**Common mistakes:** Leaving the three choices implicit so form and point of view drift mid-draft.
**Example:** A museum team plans a short piece about a night guard. They choose spoken prose with light music, an ordinary guard (like us) rather than a hero, and a single narrator voice that stays close to him. Those choices settle the script's register before drafting begins.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## F146 · Creative brief before production
**Also known as:** Creative Strategy & High Concept Testing Framework (Sommer Paulsen); The One-Page Creative Brief (Maestas); End-in-Sight Reverse Engineering (Creek); Storytelling Mission Design Template (Rodriguez)
**Use when:** Before drafting any video, report, deck or campaign, so the team cannot drift and every later choice can be checked against the brief.
**Avoid when:** The brief would be treated as fixed; it is a living guide. If insiders are blinded by expertise, add outside input. A brief alone gives only the spine; craft and design must still make it engaging (Rodriguez).
**Steps:**
1. Name the exact response wanted from the viewer: like, comment, share or buy (Creek).
2. Write a short overview of who you are and what you do, without a list of achievements (Maestas).
3. List the exact deliverables and scope.
4. Identify a core bullseye audience, plus a secondary outer-ring audience whose opinions sway the core.
5. State the change the story must cause as a concrete, measurable outcome, such as 100 applicants by December.
6. Compress the message into one key idea; allow a second only if it reinforces the first.
7. List honest, checkable reasons to believe: tenure, awards, statistics, benefits. Inventory proof assets already in hand, such as testimonials and case studies (Rodriguez).
8. Choose the tone and the exact feeling to evoke; record brand positioning (personality, tone, voice, slogan) and a first wish list of formats (Rodriguez).
9. For larger projects, answer eight core questions (purpose, value, people, theme, genre, character, connection, impact), add accessibility specs, and distill a high concept of 25 words or fewer (Sommer Paulsen).
10. Test the concept early with at least two, ideally three, neutral facilitators who are not the presenter, using present-tense walkthroughs and unbiased prompts (Sommer Paulsen).
**Common mistakes:** Defending your concept in testing; leading respondents; skipping accessibility; a second message that distracts from the first.
**Example:** A cycling-repair cooperative briefs a 90-second film. Response wanted: share. Bullseye: commuters who own a bike but never service it; outer ring: their partners. Outcome: 200 workshop sign-ups by March. One message: ten minutes of care saves a month of walking. Proof: 4,000 repairs, a regional award. Tone: warm and wry. Every storyboard frame is checked against this page.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen; Maestas; Creek; Rodriguez

## F147 · Ride the Cultural Moment
**Also known as:** Trendjacking (Vaynerchuk); Leverage unofficial National Days (Vaynerchuk); The Five Zeitgeist Triggers (Creek); Delayed Trend Publishing (Creek)
**Use when:** You want timely content, you can find a genuine link between a live conversation and your brand, and you can publish while it is alive.
**Avoid when:** The link is forced; a golf brand riding soccer hype alienates people. Invented brand hashtags rarely take off.
**Steps:**
1. Scan national, regional and global trends, viral memes and search trends, not just top-ten lists (Vaynerchuk).
2. Pick from five triggers: pop culture, news and events (including gatherings with a shared immediate desire), seasons and holidays, trending topics, music (Creek).
3. Find an unexpected but authentic bridge between the moment and your brand or point of view.
4. Publish in your own brand voice, or promote a post on a relevant trending tag, and answer replies while the trend is live (Vaynerchuk).
5. For daily calendars, tie light visual content to quirky observances such as unofficial national days (Vaynerchuk).
6. For business buyers, record early, then publish when decision makers start searching for what the trend means, not when it first appears (Creek).
7. Check every post against your rules of engagement.
**Common mistakes:** Publishing too early to an unprimed audience or too late; chasing trends that decay within hours with no link to the brand.
**Example:** A payroll software firm sees news of a new overtime rule. Its team records a two-minute explainer the same day, holds it until managers begin searching for the rule's meaning, then posts it in the firm's plain voice and answers comments that afternoon.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk; Creek

## F148 · Excerpting the Narrative for Media
**Also known as:** Distilling Narrative into Communications & Communications Planning Process (Vincent); Communications Narrative Forms (Vincent)
**Use when:** You are planning integrated campaigns, creative briefs or entertainment partnerships and have a full brand story to draw from.
**Avoid when:** The brand would be pasted in; product shots dropped into a script feel intrusive unless the brand belongs in that story world.
**Steps:**
1. Set behavioural and emotional objectives before choosing formats.
2. Select one excerpt of the brand story that fits; do not recap the whole myth.
3. Choose one of four forms: abstraction (mood, palette, sensory style), episode (a self-contained three-act micro-story), fragment (deep focus on one turning point), extension (conflict-free slice-of-life portrait).
4. Match the form to the medium: fluid broadcast, static print or outdoor, or placement inside entertainment.
5. Plan two-way responses to audience replies.
**Common mistakes:** Retelling the entire brand story in one asset; forcing the brand into entertainment where it does not belong.
**Example:** A mountain-hut network chooses one excerpt from its story, the night a storm trapped strangers together. For a poster it uses a fragment, the moment someone hands over a spare blanket. For video it uses a full episode. It also schedules a staffer to reply to guests who post their own storm nights.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F149 · Story Before Telling
**Also known as:** Storytelling = story > telling (Terenzzo)
**Use when:** You are structuring a narrative or diagnosing why an expensive film, deck or campaign feels flat.
**Avoid when:** Withholding has gone so far that the audience cannot follow; key causal clues must stay in the chosen scenes.
**Steps:**
1. Build the story first: everything that happened, character biographies, emotional stakes.
2. Choose the telling second: the few scenes, their order, and the medium.
3. Show less than you know, so the audience infers, imagines and co-creates meaning.
4. Check that enough causal clues remain on the surface for the scenes to make sense.
**Common mistakes:** Starting with scenes and medium before the underlying story exists; hiding so much that cause and effect vanish.
**Example:** A founder-led shoe repair chain drafts the full history of the founder's first shop, her apprentice and the debt that nearly closed it. The ad shows only the apprentice resoling a single boot at closing time. Viewers infer the loyalty behind it, and one visible clue, a ledger on the counter, keeps the stakes clear.
**Sources:** Terenzzo

## F150 · Peaks-Only Company History
**Also known as:** Topography of Interest (Terenzzo)
**Use when:** You are building institutional memory, an anniversary video, merger onboarding or an origin book.
**Avoid when:** Time is short for mapping a full history; mine origin stories through Aha moments instead.
**Steps:**
1. List all notable company events along a timeline (horizontal axis).
2. Score each vertically by how much irreversible change it caused.
3. Draw a cut-off line at the chosen height of transformation and discard everything below it.
4. Link only the peaks into scenes.
**Common mistakes:** Keeping events because they were pleasant or recent rather than because they changed the company for good.
**Example:** A family-run ferry company lists forty events. Winning a city contract and buying a second boat score low; the 1998 flood that forced it to move to a new harbour and the decision to hire its first non-family captain score high. The anniversary film links those two peaks and drops the rest.
**Sources:** Terenzzo
