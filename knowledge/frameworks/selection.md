# Frameworks — selection

## F19 · Stories that answer the unspoken questions
**Also known as:** Three Unspoken Buyer Questions: Trust, Like, Know / Gut, Heart, Head; Three-Part Pitch Structure: Yourself, Company, Product; Five Principles of Getting to Yes (Livesay); Your 4 Ws: Who, What (story bait), Why, Way (Guptara); Six Stories You Need to Know How to Tell (Simmons); Three Ways to Identify Stories for Sales (Dolan)
**Use when:** Preparing for a first sales meeting, a pitch, a new leadership role, networking, or any situation where you must earn trust before asking for anything.
**Avoid when:** The audience already knows and trusts you and only needs a decision structure (F04, F12).
**Steps:**
1. List the questions the audience is silently asking: who are you, why are you here, are you relevant, will this work for me, and what are they thinking but not saying.
2. Prepare one story per question:
   - Who: a personal story showing character, a formative struggle or a flaw (S07).
   - Why: your stakes stated openly, or a sacrifice that proves your motive (S08).
   - What: a one-line hook in the form "many [customers] want [desire] but [struggle]; after [credential], I help them [result]".
   - Way: how your process works, through contrast with the usual approach or an analogy.
   - Will it work for me: a story about a similar client who solved a similar problem (S09).
   - Unspoken objection: a story about someone who held that exact concern and got a good result (S11).
   - Add as needed a vision story (S19), a teaching story (S12) and a values-in-action story (S18).
3. Order them: earn trust first (calm presence, warm opening), then liking (a human origin story), then relevance (the client story). In a pitch, sell yourself, then the company and its culture, then the product through client stories with a plain statement of what each means for them.
4. In a formal pitch, also cover vision, team, culture and traction, and plan the final steps to the decision together with the buyer.
5. Tell these before you ask for the order; the set, not one great story, builds trust.
**Common mistakes:** Opening with product features, which makes you a commodity; small talk stories with no business purpose that misfire; stories that do not match how you behave offstage.
**Example:** A consultant meets a hospital purchasing board. She opens with the night her father waited six hours in an emergency room (who), says plainly she is paid per project and why she took this niche (why), names their likely doubt that consultants leave binders and vanish, and answers it with a clinic whose waiting times stayed down two years after her team left (objection and will it work).
**Sources:** Livesay; Guptara; Simmons; Dolan

## F20 · Story Wheel: a balanced personal repertoire
**Also known as:** Story Wheel; Triumph, Tragedy, Tension and Transition stories; Work-related vs. non-work-related stories (Dolan)
**Use when:** Auditing your personal story bank and choosing which personal story fits a moment: building credibility, rapport, values or a change message.
**Avoid when:** The story must be about a customer or the company rather than the teller (F19, F21).
**Steps:**
1. Sort every personal story into one or two of four types:
   - Triumph: achievement or courage, told with humility and light self-deprecation (T090).
   - Tragedy: a regret, something that happened to you, or a mistake you made, focused on the lesson.
   - Tension: torn between values, loyalties or obligations, including times you got it wrong.
   - Transition: a significant forced, chosen or self-started turning point, focused on fear and excitement rather than logistics.
2. Tag each as work or non-work. Work stories prove capability; non-work stories reveal personality and values.
3. Check two criteria for each: fully true and your own, and linked to a business purpose for this audience (T107).
4. Count the mix. Too many triumphs sound boastful; too many tensions sound indecisive or stressed. Mine more where thin (F16).
5. Choose by purpose: tragedy to build rapport and humility, tension to show values, transition to guide people through change, triumph to show capability.
6. Decide yourself whether a tragedy is too raw for this setting.
**Common mistakes:** Inventing or borrowing experiences; using trivial events as transitions; triumphs told as boasts.
**Example:** A new regional manager needs a story for her first team meeting during a restructure. Her bank holds three triumphs and one transition: leaving teaching to join the company, unsure she would fit. She picks the transition, telling how she cried in the car park after her first week, because the team is facing the same uncertainty.
**Sources:** Dolan

## F21 · Choosing the hero
**Also known as:** Four Crucial Functions of a Corporate Hero; Highest-Common-Denominator Hero Selection (Maxwell); Protagonist Shift Reframing (Hall)
**Use when:** Picking the protagonist of a case study, campaign or pitch, choosing a spokesperson, or fixing a story that moves people toward the wrong action.
**Avoid when:** The story is a personal one where you are necessarily the protagonist (F20); still apply the flaw and common-good checks.
**Steps:**
1. List candidate protagonists from the same events: customer, employee, beneficiary, partner, you.
2. Score each on four tests: shares the audience's values and stakes; commands real expertise or territory; has human flaws that make the ending believable; visibly serves the common good and the customer.
3. Pick the highest scorer whose eyes the audience will see through; keep the company and product in support (T024).
4. Run the story past the objective: what will the audience do after hearing it? If the action is wrong, keep the event and switch the protagonist.
5. Avoid cynical antiheroes unless the opponent is so dangerous that the audience forgives them.
**Common mistakes:** The company as hero; a flawless hero nobody believes; a moving story told from a viewpoint that triggers sympathy but not the needed action.
**Example:** A food bank's story about a volunteer packing boxes earns applause but few donations. The team retells the same Saturday through a nine-year-old who brings his own bag and asks for cereal his sister likes. Donations rise because donors now picture who receives their gift.
**Sources:** Maxwell; Hall

## F22 · Audience profiling
**Also known as:** Three preparation activities to profile an audience (Parikh)
**Use when:** Before framing or drafting any presentation, pitch or report for a client or internal unit.
**Avoid when:** Never skip entirely; with only minutes, do the basic step.
**Steps:**
1. Basic preparation, about one hour: industry, geography, financials, key executives, units, products, customer segments, competitors, recent news, existing relationship and known issues, from public sources, CRM and colleagues.
2. Detailed preparation, half a day or more for high stakes: stated strategy, history and acquisitions, industry trends, channels, positioning, operations and multi-year financials.
3. Profile every person who can help or block: background, role-driven interests, likely concerns and political objections.
4. Record it in one template and use it to pick stories (F19, F21) and frame the problem (F11).
**Common mistakes:** Profiling only the most senior person; generic research with no implication for the story; skipping likely objections.
**Example:** Before pitching a logistics firm, a software vendor notes the firm's push to cut fuel costs, then profiles the operations head (worried about driver adoption) and the finance chief (wants payback within a year). The pitch leads with a driver's story and closes on a twelve-month payback.
**Sources:** Parikh
