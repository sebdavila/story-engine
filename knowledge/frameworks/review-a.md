# Frameworks — review (F339–F349)

## F339 · Is-it-a-story minimum checklist
**Also known as:** The Five Essential Elements of Stories (Haven); The 5 Fundamentals (Terenzzo); Five Minimum Story Elements Audit (Creek); Story Spotting Framework (Callahan); Four Components of a Great Story (Hall); Working Definition of Story in Business (Forman); Components of a Good Story (Apps); Bullet-Proofing Your Structure (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** Screening any draft, even a 30-second spot or a short post, to learn whether it is a story or a report, sound bite or copy wearing a story label. Also useful for deciding what can be cut safely (Haven) and for auditing other people's text (Callahan).
**Avoid when:** Drafting. Apps says to ignore the list in the first draft and apply it in revision. Passing the list does not make a story good (Forman).
**Steps:**
1. Name a specific character, a person or an animal, not a company, a value or a crowd. Terenzzo allows humanized objects only if they get a face, flaws and desires.
2. State what that character wants and why it matters. Haven calls this goal plus motive; it gives every event a purpose.
3. Find the conflict: something that blocks the goal and puts something at risk. Biesenbach asks whether early attempts fail and whether the stakes force action rather than mild inconvenience. Terenzzo rejects corporate goals such as "we must sell more" as tension.
4. Check the sequence: events linked by cause and effect ("so", "but"), not just "and then". Callahan wants a time or place marker and people doing observable things; Forman wants sequenced events, a narrator's point of view and a real audience.
5. Check for concrete detail: a few sensory specifics (Haven), details the audience recognizes (Hall), one specific moment rather than a broad summary (Hall).
6. Check the ending: a resolution and a lesson or business point (Creek; Callahan), plus authentic emotion (Hall; Biesenbach).
7. Discard or rework anything missing a piece. In a very short format Hall allows dropping one component, usually the moment or one detail.
**Common mistakes:** Opening with a time marker and then listing assertions (Callahan). Treating a timeline of company milestones as a story (Hall). Giving the lesson as a résumé of events instead of through lived scenes (Terenzzo).
**Example:** A bakery owner pastes a draft: "Founded in 2009, we use local flour and care about community." It has a date and no person, no event, no obstacle. The reworked version follows Ines, who runs out of flour at 4 a.m. before a wedding order, drives to a farmer she barely knows, and gets milled flour still warm from the stone. It now has a character, a goal, a blocker, a sequence and a detail the audience can smell.
**Sources:** Haven; Terenzzo; Creek; Callahan; Hall; Forman; Apps; Biesenbach

## F340 · Storyboard and ingredients check
**Also known as:** Storyboard & Ingredients of a Story (Lupton)
**Use when:** Pitching an idea, testing a user workflow, or planning an animation, video or multi-screen product, and you want to see the whole arc before building it.
**Steps:**
1. Compress the action into a fixed small number of frames, for example six. Mark point of view (near or far, first or third person), camera moves and transitions.
2. Show the trigger, the obstacles in the middle and an active resolution in the frames.
3. Check five ingredients: arc (beginning, middle, end), change (the action transforms a character or situation), theme (a larger meaning), coherence (concrete, relevant details), plausibility (believable inside its own rules).
4. Reject any ending that rescues the character with a magic shortcut or leaves them passively defeated.
**Common mistakes:** Resolving the problem with an implausible rescue, or ending in a passive loss. Both fail the check; the satisfying ending is the one where the character acts in a way that fits the theme.
**Example:** A transit startup storyboards a commuter, Dev, whose bike tire goes flat before a meeting. Frame 3: the bus passes him. Frame 5: he scans a code at a repair kiosk and fixes the tire in two minutes. Frame 6: he arrives with grease on his cuff. A teammate notices frame 5 is the only action, and that the resolution depends on his choice, not luck, so it passes.
**Sources:** Lupton

## F341 · Criteria for a strong ending
**Also known as:** Six Criteria for Inspired Endings (Rubin)
**Use when:** Choosing among brainstormed endings during outlining, or repairing a finale that feels unsatisfying.
**Steps:**
1. List candidate endings, including the obvious ones, and set them against six tests.
2. Meaningful: causal events lead to a clear takeaway.
3. Inevitable: loose ends close, prepared by quiet foreshadowing, not coincidence.
4. Surprising: it breaks the obvious outcome or surprises in how it arrives, without cheap tricks.
5. Just: it honors the genre's and the world's promise and explains why things happen rather than indulging random cruelty.
6. Emotional: it lands a strong jolt at the last moment.
7. Authentic: it fits the writer's real worldview and the characters' psychology.
8. Drop endings that depend on coincidence or tricks; keep the one that passes most.
**Common mistakes:** Faking an outlook you do not believe; authenticity cannot be forced (Rubin).
**Example:** A short film about a lighthouse keeper has three endings: the ship is saved by luck, the keeper dies unseen, or the keeper, who ignored early radio warnings, saves the ship by lighting the old lamp by hand. The third closes the radio setup, surprises in how, and fits the film's idea about trust in old tools.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F342 · Earned transformation timeline
**Also known as:** Timeline Method for Earned Transformations (Rubin)
**Use when:** Mapping a character arc, or diagnosing a change in behavior that feels unmotivated.
**Steps:**
1. Write the start and end snapshots on a timeline, each in one crisp line.
2. Set the amount of detail by distance travelled and genre. A small emotional shift needs very fine beats.
3. Add resistance, denial and backsliding, because change comes hard.
4. Place the necessary milestones first, then fill causal steps around them, using psychological mechanisms such as repression, regression, sublimation or projection.
5. Cross-examine the chain as prosecutor and as defense until each step holds logically and emotionally.
**Common mistakes:** A chain that works as arithmetic but not as feeling; intuition has the last word (Rubin).
**Example:** A customer-story draft says a skeptical clinic manager, Omar, goes from refusing new software to championing it. The timeline adds: a missed prescription refill, a night spent reconciling paper logs, a first small trial he abandons after a glitch, a nurse's thank-you that pulls him back. Each step has a reason, and the glitch gives the backsliding that makes the final conversion believable.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F343 · Four qualities of character
**Also known as:** Four Qualities of Character (Aristotle)
**Use when:** Designing characters and writing their decisions and dialogue.
**Steps:**
1. Goodness: show positive moral choices in words and actions; even flawed figures stay basically decent.
2. Appropriateness: give traits that fit the character's social role and type.
3. Relatability: keep the character recognizably human and like the audience.
4. Consistency: keep behavior uniform; an erratic character must be erratic in a consistent way.
**Common mistakes:** Behavior that contradicts earlier scenes, such as a terrified person who later volunteers without explanation. Aristotle's examples of appropriateness assume ancient Greek views of gender and status; apply the test to role, not to stereotype.
**Example:** In a safety-training story, a foreman named Rosa is shown early double-checking every harness. Later she shrugs off a missing clip. The audience stops believing her. Either give her a visible reason (a deadline that has bent her habits) or keep her careful throughout.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## F344 · Theme engine: define, amplify, attack
**Also known as:** Three-Part Theme Engine: Define, Amplify, Attack (Rubin)
**Use when:** You are reworking a draft's meaning and want to check that its core idea survives scrutiny.
**Avoid when:** The work is feeling-driven or surreal and is meant to go beyond rational argument (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Define: state your take on the subject, not a one-word topic, in the shortest sentence the ending's action proves.
2. Amplify: repeat and vary the theme through motifs such as phrases, props, background signs, weather and sound.
3. Attack: give the smartest, most compassionate counterargument to a credible character, or to a pointed silence, so the thesis survives its hardest test.
**Common mistakes:** Letting the theme be preached by narration rather than proven by the ending and varied through motifs.
**Example:** A documentary short claims "small repairs build neighborhoods." A shopkeeper's chalk sign, "fixed today," recurs in four scenes. Midway, a landlord argues repairs only raise rents. The film lets that stand, then ends with the landlord's tenants fixing his stair themselves.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F345 · Six parts of drama by priority
**Also known as:** Six Parts of Tragedy (Aristotle)
**Use when:** Planning or revising a drama and judging where to put your craft effort.
**Steps:**
1. Rank elements: plot first, then character, reasoning, diction, music and spectacle last.
2. Plot: organize incidents into one unified action.
3. Character: reveal moral quality through deliberate choices in speech and action.
4. Reasoning: what characters argue, prove, refute or state as general views.
5. Diction: word choice that conveys meaning.
6. Music, then spectacle: pleasing accompaniment and staged visuals; spectacle is the producer's concern more than the writer's.
7. Spend revision effort in that order; a play with strong plot and weak speeches beats polished speeches with no plot.
**Common mistakes:** Polishing style or visuals while the plot is loose. Epic shares the first four elements but has no music or spectacle (Aristotle).
**Example:** A team polishes the animation of a product-launch video while the plot is "features, features, features." Re-ranking, they first rebuild the sequence of events around one customer's problem, then her choices, and only then the narration and motion effects.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## F346 · Critique defense matrix
**Also known as:** Critique Defense Matrix, Five Objections and Twelve Solutions (Aristotle)
**Use when:** Reviewing a draft, or answering a critic who says a passage is wrong.
**Avoid when:** The flaw is irrational or depraved and the plot did not require it; never excuse it where a logical alternative existed (Aristotle).
**Steps:**
1. Classify the objection: impossible, irrational, morally harmful, contradictory, or against artistic standards.
2. Separate errors of craft from minor factual slips outside the art.
3. If something is impossible, ask whether it makes the work more wondrous and could not be achieved logically.
4. Ask which the passage shows: things as they are or were, things as people believe them, or things as they should be.
5. For morality, ask who did the deed, to whom, at what moment, and whether it served a larger good or prevented a harm. For wording, check loan words, metaphor, accent, punctuation, ambiguity and custom.
**Common mistakes:** Judging a detail by the critic's assumption instead of the tradition the work draws on.
**Example:** A reviewer objects that a founder story shows a customer paying cash in a 1990s shop, which the reviewer thinks is implausible. The writer checks: this is a depiction of how people in that town actually paid, so it is a factual matter, not a craft error, and the scene stays.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## F347 · Audience-centred writing process
**Also known as:** The AUTHOR Writing Process (Edwards)
**Use when:** Writing any presentation, document, report or speech.
**Avoid when:** The text has a prescribed formal register, such as legal contracts or academic papers; the write-as-you-speak step does not apply (Edwards).
**Steps:**
1. Analogue: ideate on paper with pen and sticky notes, not in Word or PowerPoint.
2. Understand: keep researching the audience's needs, knowledge and competitors while you write; ask what they need to hear, not what you want to say.
3. Talk: write in your everyday plain language and read drafts aloud.
4. Headlines: write all full-sentence headlines in order before any body text or slides.
5. One: one idea per slide and per paragraph; the first sentence is the headline, the rest explain, prove or illustrate.
6. Rewrite: treat the first draft as raw material, sleep on it, cut darlings that do not advance the story, then read aloud to a colleague.
**Common mistakes:** Starting in the slide tool, which hides early thinking and discourages a real second draft.
**Example:** A logistics analyst, Priya, covers a wall with sticky notes about warehouse delays, writes ten headline sentences that tell the story from "Customers wait four days" to "Fund the second dock", and only then builds slides. Her colleague, hearing it aloud, spots one headline carrying two ideas and splits it.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F348 · Audience-action audit of each slide
**Also known as:** Do Think Feel & The Slide/Paragraph Where They... (Edwards)
**Use when:** Reviewing your own data-heavy deck or document, or giving editorial feedback to a colleague or multi-author team.
**Steps:**
1. Present the review as a joint sense-check against a model, not a personal critique.
2. Per slide or paragraph, have the author complete the line "this is where they..." (an audience action or realization), never "this is where we...". Alternatively ask what the audience should do, think or feel here.
3. If the blank cannot be filled clearly, ask whether to cut it or bring in a colleague to find its audience value.
4. Compare the actual content with the agreed sentence so the author finds and fixes the mismatches.
**Common mistakes:** Slides that only describe the presenter; cut those.
**Example:** A pharmacy chain's pitch has five slides. The team defines: where they see how long customers wait at pickup; where they understand the app removes one step; where they feel reassured about data privacy. The slide on the chain's founding year fits no sentence about the audience and is cut.
**Sources:** Edwards

## F349 · SCAMPER for vulnerable angles
**Also known as:** SCAMPER for Vulnerable Storytelling (Rodriguez)
**Use when:** A brand story feels sanitized, corporate or emotionally flat.
**Avoid when:** Stay within the vulnerability limits agreed with PR and leadership about topics that are off-limits (Rodriguez).
**Steps:**
1. Substitute: swap a character, a twist such as a brand failure, or the conclusion.
2. Combine: blend structures or pair written and visual formats.
3. Adapt: reframe the story for another purpose or secondary business objective.
4. Modify: change visuals, typography, colour or presentation, with input from ambassadors.
5. Put to another use: retrieve unused ideas from earlier brainstorms.
6. Eliminate: remove a character, a visual or the conclusion so what remains stands out.
7. Reverse: invert roles or sequence, for example employees as heroes and customers in support.
**Common mistakes:** Crossing the agreed boundaries on what may be disclosed.
**Example:** A furniture maker's polished page about a new chair is run through SCAMPER. Eliminate the triumphant ending; reverse the cast so the warehouse packer, not the designer, tells the story; substitute the failed first prototype for the hero shot. The rougher version about the chair that collapsed in testing draws far more replies.
**Sources:** Rodriguez
