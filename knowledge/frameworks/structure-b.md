# Frameworks — structure (F012–F022)

## F012 · Concentric rings of conflict
**Also known as:** Concentric Rings of Conflict (Rubin)
**Use when:** Building a character profile, enriching a flat scene, or choosing a medium whose length can carry the conflict.
**Avoid when:** The piece is short; a short farce needs far fewer rings than a long drama (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Centre: conflict with self (shame, illness, anxiety, mortality).
2. Close relationships: friction with family, lovers, friends, neighbors, coworkers.
3. Society: religion, government, corporations, media, cultural rules.
4. Surroundings: weather, terrain, decay, machines, sensory environment.
5. Activate as many rings as the length and genre can carry, and let them press at once.
**Common mistakes:** Maxing every ring in a short piece; a flat scene with friction from only one ring.
**Example:** A small-town pharmacist must tell customers a drug is recalled. She doubts her own judgment (self), her brother runs the rival store (personal), the chain's policy forbids comment (society), and a blizzard cuts the road (surroundings). The scene is short, so she keeps two rings pressing hardest and lets the others hum in the background.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F013 · Progressive character reveal
**Also known as:** Peel the Onion (Rubin)
**Use when:** Designing a character and pacing what the audience learns across beginning, middle and climax.
**Avoid when:** Treating the layers as rigid compartments; in drafts they overlap (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Surface: appearance, clothes, posture, basic bio, first impressions.
2. Mental: how they think, plan and solve problems, including blind spots.
3. Spiritual: ethics and how they judge right and wrong under stress.
4. Core: identity proven only by a high-stakes action when everything is on the line.
5. Place each layer later in the story than the one before.
**Common mistakes:** Stating the core in the first scene; layers treated as rigid compartments.
**Example:** A new warehouse manager shows up in a crisp jacket (surface), obsesses over route spreadsheets (mental), quietly pays a driver's fine from his own pocket (spiritual), and at the flood night stays to carry pallets with the crew. That last act proves who he is, and it comes after the other three layers.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F014 · Character mask management
**Also known as:** Five-Factor Character Masking (Rubin)
**Use when:** A character hides a secret, wound, scheme or dual identity.
**Avoid when:** In drama, avoid obvious masks; horror and comedy tolerate broad ones (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. Decide why the mask is worn: noble duty, protecting vulnerability, malice, public persona or unconscious repression.
2. Sell it early with believable outward cues and social shortcuts.
3. Plant small slips, fitting the genre, that do not give the secret away.
4. Show how wearer and onlookers react when the mask shifts.
5. Time the unmasking, or a permanent re-masking, to serve genre and theme.
**Common mistakes:** No slips, so the reveal feels arbitrary; unmasking with no thematic payoff.
**Example:** A cheerful sales director, Omar, jokes through every meeting. Slips: he never goes to the Friday lunch, and he flinches at a competitor's name. The reveal that he left that firm after being scapegoated recasts his cheerfulness as armor.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F015 · Dialogue as tactics with subtext
**Also known as:** Actionable Dialogue and Subtext Pass (Rubin)
**Use when:** Drafting or revising conversations and monologues.
**Avoid when:** Do not force hidden meaning into every line; keep most scenes to two active speakers (Rubin).
**Steps:**
1. For each speaker note background and temperament, mood coming in, view of the relationship, what they need from the listener, and the response they expect.
2. Give every line an action verb to play (flatter, guilt, bargain, threaten) so talk works like combat.
3. First draft on the nose: characters say exactly what they mean.
4. Rewrite backward so fear, shame or pride hides beneath a surface topic.
**Common mistakes:** Exposition dumped in speech; every character using the same tactic.
**Example:** A daughter needs her father to sell the shop. On the nose: I think you should retire. Rewrite: she offers to help him inventory the back room (bargain), then lifts a dusty box and sighs (guilt), while they discuss only the weather.
**Sources:** Rubin

## F016 · Scenes, summaries and reflections
**Also known as:** Narrative Stepping Stones (Bowles)
**Use when:** Outlining between the opening hook (F009) and the closing line.
**Avoid when:** Never narrate mundane literal steps; jump cut to the next meaningful beat (Bowles).
**Steps:**
1. List every essential plot turn, piece of backstory and inner realization up to the main event and the resolution.
2. Render pivotal moments and the climax as scenes: slow down and recount sensory action from inside.
3. Bridge time gaps with brief summaries and jump cuts.
4. Add reflections on what you felt, learned or accepted, so action and interiority alternate.
**Common mistakes:** Narrating mundane literal steps instead of jump cutting; action with no reflection to alternate with.
**Example:** A volunteer coordinator tells of her first food drive. Scene: a donor truck arrives empty. Summary: two weeks of phone calls. Scene: the church basement fills with strangers. Reflection: she learned to ask for specific help.
**Sources:** Bowles

## F017 · Personal story sequencing options
**Also known as:** Moth Story Structures (Bowles)
**Use when:** Choosing the order of scenes for a spoken story before rehearsing.
**Avoid when:** A cliffhanger feels forced, or a tragic outcome would create dread; then reveal it early (Bowles).
**Steps:**
1. Chronological: when mystery, surprise or gradual escalation carries tension.
2. Two chronologies in tandem: cut between two simultaneous lines whose tensions deepen each other and resolve together.
3. Flashback or several flashbacks: pause at a tense beat for motivating backstory.
4. Cliffhanger: open on a few lines of a late crisis, pause at the brink, rewind, then finish that scene as the climax.
5. Smaller lens: frame a huge event through one relationship, object or age.
6. Signal every time jump clearly; listeners cannot rewind.
**Common mistakes:** Unmarked time jumps; a forced cliffhanger; withholding a tragic outcome that creates dread.
**Example:** A teacher tells of the morning she locked herself out of the exam hall. She opens with two lines: her hand on the cold handle, 300 students behind the door. She pauses, rewinds to the alarm that never rang, then returns to the handle as the climax and the dean's key.
**Sources:** Bowles

## F018 · Plot-first drafting
**Also known as:** Plot-First Drafting Workflow (Aristotle)
**Use when:** Drafting an original story or adapting a traditional one, to catch staging and logic flaws early.
**Steps:**
1. Picture each scene as if watching it live, spotting contradictions.
2. Act out gestures and feel the character's emotion while writing.
3. Write the bare causal skeleton with no names or local detail.
4. Add names and expand into episodes, brief for drama, longer for epic.
**Common mistakes:** Naming and decorating before the causal chain works.
**Example:** Skeleton for a team story: a person is blamed for a missed deadline, hides the real cause, a colleague finds the proof, and the blame reverses. Only then do we add names, the supplier delay and the Thursday meeting. Writing the skeleton first shows the blame reversal works before any decoration.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## F019 · Short business story formula (1-2-1)
**Also known as:** The 1-2-1 Story Formula (Guptara, after Brinkmann)
**Use when:** Making a point in 30 to 90 seconds in a meeting, conversation or pitch.
**Avoid when:** The setting calls for a longer stage performance (Guptara).
**Steps:**
1. One point: announce the lesson and when it was learned, and build the story around only that.
2. Two halves: Before and After, split by a watershed moment of change.
3. One unforgettable detail: a concrete sensory image, action or line of dialogue.
**Common mistakes:** Two lessons; no moment of change; a detail that is generic.
**Example:** I learned to price for value, not hours, at a wedding fair in 2019. Before: as a freelance photographer I quoted by the hour and kept losing to cheaper rivals. Then a bride pointed at my portfolio and asked which of these would make her grandmother cry. After: I sold albums built around that question and doubled my fee within a year.
**Sources:** Guptara

## F020 · Rule of three
**Also known as:** Rule of Threes (Lupton); Rule of Three (Sandler); Triadic Life-Story Opening (Salmon)
**Use when:** Grouping points, steps, choices or stories for recall; structuring onboarding, tiers or three-panel ads.
**Avoid when:** Cramming too many tasks into three steps hides what is required; more than four steps signals heavy commitment (Lupton).
**Steps:**
1. Compress the process or message into three steps or choices.
2. Put the strongest item last (Sandler), or use items one and two to set a pattern and the third to twist it (Lupton).
3. Chunk dense strings of data in threes or fours.
4. For a talk, announce three short personal stories and close on a motto (Salmon).
**Common mistakes:** Cramming too many tasks into three steps; a weak final item.
**Example:** A gym's flyer reads Book, Sweat, Repeat. A speaker opens a toast with three tiny stories: how they met, how they argued, how they stayed. The strongest one, how they stayed, goes last, and a one-line motto closes it.
**Sources:** Lupton; Sandler; Salmon

## F021 · Three-act change arc
**Also known as:** Three-Act Structure (Sandler; Barker & Brown); Freytag's Pyramid (Sandler; Creek); Steller framework (Hall); Beginning, Middle, End with Bridge, Link, Pause (Dolan); Three-part with future-back vision (Buster); social three-act (Creek); therefore/but check (Kamath)
**Use when:** You need the simplest arc for a business, case-study, social or speech story; a short story must end on one takeaway.
**Avoid when:** Slow exposition would lose an online viewer (Creek); the work is deliberately non-linear (Barker & Brown).
**Steps:**
1. Normal: the identifiable character, their emotions and familiar details in the status quo.
2. Explosion: the event, realization or decision that breaks it. Draft this first, then build Normal backward (Hall).
3. Link beats by consequence or reversal (see F004); add stasis, point of no return, midpoint reversal and either All Is Lost or False Victory when the piece is long (Barker & Brown).
4. Climax, then new normal that answers the opening.
5. End: for spoken business stories, a Bridge sentence, a Link takeaway using we, then a one-to-two second Pause (Dolan); or speak from a lived future and exit at once (Buster).
6. Reduce the lesson to one a child could repeat (Creek).
7. Diagnose weak catharsis by checking which Freytag stage is missing (Sandler).
**Common mistakes:** Drafting in order; long setup; a complex moral; a bridge skipped on a non-work story.
**Example:** Normal: a support lead answers the same refund question forty times a day. Explosion: a customer thanks her for the forty-first answer, which she realizes is wrong. New normal: she writes a one-page guide; the team halves its calls. Takeaway: fix the answer once.
**Sources:** Sandler; Hall; Dolan; Buster; Barker & Brown; Creek; Kamath

## F022 · Story spine prompts
**Also known as:** Once Upon a Time magic words (Terenzzo); Pixar's Story Structure (Sandler)
**Use when:** You need a full causal arc fast, before slides or scenes, or to rescue a disorganized pitch or frame a design problem.
**Avoid when:** The campaign is multi-channel and non-linear (Terenzzo); never say the prompt words aloud to a business audience (see F004).
**Steps:**
1. Complete the starters in order: once upon a time there was (character and world); every day (routine); one day (disruption).
2. Because of that (repeat for each consequence).
3. Terenzzo's version: add an unexpected obstacle, a turning insight, then a transformed new normal.
4. Sandler's version: until finally (climax, resolution, new equilibrium).
5. Build the slides or scenes from the filled sentences.
**Common mistakes:** Treating the starters as the script; no true disruption.
**Example:** Once there was a dispatcher named Joy. Every day she routed trucks by memory. One day the new GPS rerouted everyone onto a closed bridge. Because of that, she built a manual check. Until finally the depot adopted it.
**Sources:** Terenzzo; Sandler
