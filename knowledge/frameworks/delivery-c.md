# Frameworks — delivery (F203–F213)

## F203 · Three-voice palette
**Also known as:** Three-Voice Delivery Palette (Miller)
**Use when:** You tell any improvised story aloud and want to steer mood, pacing and wonder without ornate vocabulary.
**Avoid when:** Listeners are very young; hold back the sinister voice and heavy jeopardy until around seven and older.
**Steps:**
1. Use a bright, bold, strong voice for energy, action and vitality.
2. Drop to a sinister voice for a touch of fear, mystery or dread at moments of jeopardy.
3. Use a quiet, gentle voice to lull listeners into a dreamlike calm and draw out wonder.
4. Notice how your tone changes the room and shift deliberately between the three.
**Example:** On a lantern tour of an old harbor, a guide tells adult visitors about a smuggler's escape. She uses the bright voice for the rowing chase, drops to the sinister voice as the customs boat's lamp sweeps the water, and ends in a near whisper as the fog swallows the skiff. She watches the group lean in and slows the whisper further.
**Sources:** Miller

## F204 · Directing through playable actions
**Also known as:** Actioning the Lines (Simson); Playable Direction (Simson); Work-Plan Actioning Sequence (Simson); Anti-Jargon Take Cues (Simson)
**Use when:** You prepare dialogue for performers or adjust a flat performance in rehearsal or between takes.
**Avoid when:** Telling a performer what emotion to show; that makes them monitor themselves instead of listening. Offer verbs as options, since they are personal to the actor.
**Steps:**
1. Give each line a transitive active verb for what the speaker does to the listener (to bully, to persuade, to confront).
2. Start a new beat where the verb shifts in response to the other character. Optionally add subtext, the unspoken thought under the words.
3. To adjust a take, offer one of: a new active verb, a fact about the circumstances, an image or personal memory, an event that just shifted, or a physical task such as packing a bag.
4. Prepare each scene with ten character questions, beats and turning point, a verb per line, the scene's place in the larger story, a floor plan and links to other scenes.
5. Use calm take cues such as Let's start and Okay, stop in place of Action and Cut to lower self-consciousness.
**Example:** In a hospital drama, a nurse says, The doctor will be here soon. The director actions it as to stall, with the subtext I cannot tell you yet. When the take reads flat, she reminds the actress that the scan results arrived ten minutes ago, then hands her a clipboard to straighten while she speaks.
**Sources:** Simson

## F205 · Status and space
**Also known as:** Status and Social Space (Simson); Axis Alignment (Simson)
**Use when:** You block actors, arrange furniture or design a turning point on a stage or set.
**Avoid when:** The setting is crowded, such as a packed beach, where territorial spacing compresses.
**Steps:**
1. Decide each character's relative status at the scene's start; keep gaps subtle for authenticity.
2. Choreograph distance, posture and eye contact; breaking eye contact signals high status, and servants avoid dominating space near a master.
3. Lay out furniture along one axis: a desk set deep in the room makes a visitor cross a long stretch of floor. Gather key characters, props and furniture along a single line of depth ahead of the main camera, so the relationships register in three dimensions.
4. Stage the turning point as a visible reversal of status or territory.
**Example:** In a sports drama, a coach stands at center court while her star player hovers at the gym door, eyes down. When the player says she is quitting, she walks onto the court, then turns away from the coach mid-sentence to look at the empty hoop. The coach backs up and sits on the lowest bleacher, now beneath her, still staring at her face.
**Sources:** Simson

## F206 · Keep a mental movie running
**Also known as:** Cinema of the Mind (Dicks)
**Use when:** You draft openings, character descriptions, backstory, and technical or historical context, and want listeners to stay in a scene.
**Avoid when:** Over-describing irrelevant room dimensions; naming the setting lets listeners fill in the rest.
**Steps:**
1. Attach a physical location to every moment, so a film director would know where to point the camera.
2. Place character descriptions and long backstory inside a physical scene rather than stopping for voice-over.
3. Partway through long backstory, remind listeners where you are physically.
4. Deliver needed facts in a brief flashback, for instance a classroom lesson, rather than pausing the story to explain.
**Example:** An aircraft maintenance trainer has to teach new hires why a torque spec cannot be eyeballed. She sets the story in a hangar at 2 a.m., where she lies on a creeper under a wing with a flashlight in her teeth. Halfway through the history of the bolt, she reminds them she is still on her back under that wing. The rule itself comes as a quick flashback to her first instructor tapping a wrench against her helmet.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F207 · Strategic placement of humor
**Also known as:** Strategic Humor: Milk Cans & Babies and Blenders (Dicks)
**Use when:** You add laughs to a serious or light story without becoming stand-up, even if you are not naturally funny.
**Avoid when:** Humor is optional; stakes and heart are not. Nonstop laughter prevents the emotional range that makes stories memorable.
**Steps:**
1. Place laughs at four moments: in the first thirty seconds, right before a painful event, right after intense horror, and near a tearful ending to reset the room.
2. Setup and punchline: stack expectations step by step and put the most surprising, oddly specific word last, after a brief pause.
3. Pair two unrelated ideas, or use a mismatched list of three, or test exaggerations until the funniest numbers stick.
4. End the last lines on heart, vulnerability or meaning, never a joke or pun.
**Example:** A fire captain telling recruits about her worst warehouse fire gets a laugh in the first thirty seconds by comparing the station's old engine to a toaster with a siren. Just before the roof gives way, she mentions the rookie beside her had been on the job nine hours, a number she tested against six and twelve until nine got the biggest laugh. Her last line is not a joke: it names that rookie, who now runs the station.
**Sources:** Dicks

## F208 · Multi-sense scene description
**Also known as:** VAKOG (Karia)
**Use when:** You draft or revise scenes so settings, actions and characters feel visceral and stick in memory.
**Avoid when:** Breadth of senses is used as an excuse for slow, long description; keep it short.
**Steps:**
1. Visual: say what can be seen.
2. Auditory: name distinct sounds or sound analogies.
3. Kinesthetic: convey touch, pressure, movement or bodily and emotional feeling.
4. Olfactory: evoke the smells of the setting.
5. Gustatory: evoke taste where it fits.
**Example:** A trainer opening a talk on ward rounds says: fluorescent hum, a cart wheel squeaking, the sting of hand gel on cracked knuckles, the burnt-toast smell of the staff kitchen. Four short cues, no paragraph.
**Sources:** Karia

## F209 · Stories placed through a talk
**Also known as:** Presentation Story Architecture and Delivery (Biesenbach); Story vs. Anecdote & Serial Recall Pairing (James)
**Use when:** You build and deliver a speech or business presentation around stories.
**Avoid when:** Using robotic formulas or overloading: five two-to-three-minute stories in a fifteen-minute talk make ideas seem thin (Biesenbach). Ending on an anecdote forces a tacked-on explanation (James).
**Steps:**
1. Open with a short story or anecdote tied to the audience's problem, not agendas or bios.
2. Place short stories between dense, data-heavy sections, about one per major section.
3. Close with a full story or a callback twist extending the opening (Biesenbach). An anecdote leans on a brief comment after it; a story carries its meaning in the change and needs no explanation (James).
4. Pairing: anecdote to open, full story to close, because listeners recall beginnings and endings best (James).
5. Rehearse repeatedly to trim, and visualize scenes and faces so repeated tellings stay fresh and slightly larger than life (Biesenbach).
**Example:** A cybersecurity lead opens with a one-minute anecdote about a lost badge at a hotel, places a two-line story between the incident statistics, and closes with the badge returning, found by a cleaner who had read the sign.
**Sources:** Biesenbach; James

## F210 · Delivery equation and five delivery shifts
**Also known as:** Delivery Equation & Five Secrets of Delivery (James)
**Use when:** You rehearse or perform any oral story and want live connection rather than recitation.
**Avoid when:** Word-for-word delivery is warranted only for narrow legal, regulated or corporate sound-bite lines inside a speech.
**Steps:**
1. Read delivery as the sum of story, listeners, context and teller; adjust for each.
2. Recite less, respond more: know the story's landscape, not its exact phrasing.
3. Concentrate less, relax more: drill mechanics in practice, then stay present on stage.
4. Pretend less, believe more: picture the scene as real so your body reacts honestly.
5. Explain less, evoke more: show transformation through action, not moral.
6. Imitate less, embody more: tell it through your own personality.
**Example:** A union organizer telling a strike story at shift change sees the night crew yawning, so she drops her planned timeline and starts at the moment the gates locked. She tells it in her own blunt cadence instead of the rousing tone she had borrowed from a famous speech. She ends on the foreman handing back the keys rather than explaining what solidarity means.
**Sources:** James

## F211 · Channels of live delivery
**Also known as:** Eight Instruments of Orchestration (James); Voice of Your Being & Delivery Protocol (Maguire); Oral Language Delivery Channels (Simmons)
**Use when:** You refine live telling and want technique to disappear into the story.
**Avoid when:** Controlling every channel consciously during delivery overloads you; train them separately beforehand, then focus on the listener (Simmons). Theatrical acting and forced vocal tricks break trust (Maguire).
**Steps:**
1. Voice: vary pace and pitch, finish sentences, and speak from a natural lower register, not thin or monotone (James; Maguire).
2. Face: conjure the true feeling first and let it reach the face (Simmons; James).
3. Eyes: look at listeners during narration and toward imaginary characters during dialogue (James).
4. Posture and body: stand tall, shift slightly to differentiate characters, step left, center and right to anchor sequence (James; Simmons).
5. Gestures: gesture from shoulder or elbow, commit, and keep imaginary objects fixed in space (James).
6. Pauses: hold two full seconds at emotional peaks (Simmons).
7. Tone: clear resentment or neediness first, since tone overrides words (Simmons). Add an unrelated specific detail for authenticity.
8. Set up the occasion: control setting, ask permission to tell, and give an unmistakable ending signal (Maguire).
**Example:** Dev practices only pauses on Monday, only gestures on Tuesday. When he tells the story of the broken crane to his crew, he thinks of the crane and the crew only.
**Sources:** James; Maguire; Simmons

## F212 · Five elements of presentation readiness
**Also known as:** Sun-tzu's Five Fundamental Elements for Presentations (Maxwell)
**Use when:** You prepare a high-stakes presentation or pitch to a skeptical audience in person.
**Steps:**
1. Conviction: confirm your message is true and that you care about it.
2. Timing: adapt to the slot. Morning suits sharp substantiating detail; during the 2 to 4 p.m. dip keep segments under five minutes, share speaking roles and leave lights on; late in the day strip the pitch down for a fast close.
3. Terrain: arrive fifteen minutes early, test equipment and squeaky chairs, stand outside the podium, step forward for the future, back for the past, and stay still for the present.
4. Command: balance timidity and overconfidence with calm voice and focused intensity.
5. Plan: rehearse aloud five times, once or twice before a live listener, without memorizing word for word; cut phrases that trip the tongue; end with a simple, doable call to action.
**Example:** Before a 3 p.m. budget pitch, Amara arrives early, finds a loose-wheeled lectern, moves to the open floor, splits her talk into three four-minute parts with a colleague, and finishes with one ask: approve the pilot for March.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## F213 · Sender, sign, message, receiver chain
**Also known as:** The Semiotic Sign System (Schwabauer & Sweet)
**Use when:** You clarify authorial intent, narrator reliability and how an audience interprets a story.
**Avoid when:** Expecting it to show whether the audience is humble enough to receive the message; it explains transmission only.
**Steps:**
1. Sender: name the originator behind the curtain shaping choices and purpose.
2. Sign: name the vehicle, such as a letter, parable or character voice.
3. Message: state what the sender intends to communicate through that sign.
4. Receiver: name who interprets it. When no external sender exists, the self becomes all four and meaning shrinks to personal preference.
**Example:** A town archivist writes a letter in the voice of a long-dead ferryman (sender: archivist; sign: the letter; message: the river is worth protecting; receiver: council). Mapping this shows the ferryman's voice is a vehicle, not the sender.
**Sources:** Schwabauer & Sweet
