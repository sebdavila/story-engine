# Frameworks — brand (F288–F298)

## F288 · Customer as hero, brand as guide
**Also known as:** Classic hero formula (Mastropierro); Hero story (Mastropierro); The Hero's Journey (Creek); Make the Viewer the Hero (Creek); StoryBrand (Maestas); The Robin to Batman Effect (Rodriguez)
**Use when:** Writing About pages, blog openers, sales copy, brand films, fundraising or mission statements, or auditing a pitch that talks mostly about the company.
**Avoid when:** Operations contradict the promise (the story backfires), or leaders will not give up the lead role (Rodriguez; Maestas).
**Steps:**
1. Name the hero: the customer, beneficiary or community. A founder can also be the hero in a history piece (Mastropierro).
2. Name the problem and its visible symptoms; add a villain or opposing force if the piece is long (Creek).
3. Cast the brand as guide: give a clear plan and a call to action (Maestas).
4. Show what the hero avoids (failure) and what they gain (success) (Maestas).
5. Resolve the struggle and end on one lesson (Creek).
6. For an audit, read your mission statement, find where the customer appears, and rewrite until their win is central; then check every department supports it (Rodriguez).
**Common mistakes:** The brand grabs credit; operations or support contradict the story; over-building backstory when a minimal sketch suffices (Mastropierro).
**Example:** A bike-repair co-op writes its homepage about Dana, a courier whose chain snaps before every shift. The co-op appears only as the guide: a three-step maintenance plan, a button to book a slot, a line on missed deliveries avoided and a line on her calm Monday. Staff are briefed so the counter experience matches.
**Sources:** Mastropierro; Creek; Maestas; Rodriguez

## F289 · Design-thinking loop for brand stories
**Also known as:** Storytelling with Design Thinking Principles (Rodriguez); The Story Design Playbook (Rodriguez)
**Use when:** Turning a technical offering into a human story, entering a new market, or testing a sensitive message cheaply before production.
**Avoid when:** You need a one-pass, permanent narrative; the loop never finishes and restarts as audiences shift (Rodriguez).
**Steps:**
1. Empathize: listen through social monitoring, short surveys or observation; choose one universal truth, an inclusive feeling.
2. Define: set mission, characters and conflict with a fill-in-the-blank sentence naming the brand, the goal the story showcases, whose perspective tells it and which customer need it meets; pick a structure.
3. Ideate: hold at least two rule-bound sessions (mind map, sketch, SWOT), tying each idea to mission and universal truth.
4. Prototype: reduce each concept to a napkin pitch with four quadrants: theme, stakeholder benefits, execution, business rationale; watch reactions.
5. Test: soft-launch with listening tools and sentiment tracking; iterate or drop.
6. Workshop aids: stack emotion (core emotion, body reaction, named feeling); practice three levels of empathy (cognitive, emotional, compassionate); fill a definition matrix; map assets in an Ikigai-style diagram (Rodriguez).
**Common mistakes:** Treating the first prototype as final or the loop as finished (Rodriguez).
**Example:** A tax-software team targets freelancers. After listening to forum threads, the universal truth is relief. Three napkin pitches are shown to a small freelancer panel while the team watches faces. The pitch with the loudest laughter is the shoebox-of-receipts tale, so the team sharpens it and soft-launches it, tracking comments for a week before spending on production.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## F290 · Integrated story across channels
**Also known as:** IMC Reimagined (Rodriguez); Kotler's Four Ps, Schultz's IMC and Dietrich's PESO Model (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Aligning teams, agencies and channels around one narrative; planning campaigns and media budgets.
**Avoid when:** Storytelling is only an outside marketing campaign with no cross-functional sponsor (Rodriguez); budget is spent on reach before target roles and media habits are known (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Anchor the message in a big idea (a universal truth) and launch it inside first through a shared hub: story deck, guidelines, techniques, training (Rodriguez).
2. Audit the four Ps, positioning and sales objectives (Quesenberry).
3. Profile the real target, including every buying-center role in B2B: initiators, users, influencers, deciders, approvers, buyers, gatekeepers (Quesenberry).
4. Set goals about target emotions and share them across departments (Rodriguez).
5. Use psychographic insight, saturate internal channels, then write tailored mini-stories per outside cohort (Rodriguez).
6. Deliver one story through paid, earned, shared and owned channels (Quesenberry).
7. Schedule flights (pulsing, flighting, continuous) and measure against a pre-story baseline with early feedback loops (Quesenberry; Rodriguez).
**Common mistakes:** Disconnected one-off campaigns; buying reach before knowing who decides.
**Example:** A regional clinic chain tells one story of "Mondays without dread." Staff see it first in the intranet hub. Patients meet it through a local radio spot (paid), a newspaper feature (earned), patient photo posts (shared) and a weekly email (owned), each with the same goal and the same baseline survey.
**Sources:** Rodriguez; Quesenberry

## F291 · Designing a story ambassador persona
**Also known as:** Designing the Story Persona (Rodriguez)
**Use when:** Choosing, briefing or governing employee advocates and outside influencers who carry the story.
**Avoid when:** Candidates are paid-only voices with no real fondness for the brand, which reads as transactional (Rodriguez).
**Steps:**
1. Test whether each candidate belongs naturally in the story.
2. List audience motivations; if cohorts differ, plan several storytellers who draw audiences on their own.
3. Define four sets of cues: cognitive (catchphrase), physical (props, calls to action, visuals), sensory (tone, presence), functional (traits that move the mission).
4. Match their natural style to the emotional job: never pair a sarcastic voice with a gentle brand.
5. Document personality traits as a flexible guide, not a script, and revisit it periodically.
**Common mistakes:** Over-scripting people, which kills authenticity.
**Example:** A plant-nursery brand wants to calm anxious first-time gardeners. It picks a retired nurse on staff who already talks soothingly, gives her a signature "one pot at a time" cue and a trowel prop, and a one-page trait sheet she may bend. A teenage seller handles the TikTok cohort separately.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## F292 · Internal story training
**Also known as:** Internal Stakeholder Training Protocol (Rodriguez)
**Use when:** Rolling out a brand story inside, onboarding teams, or retraining after the story changes.
**Avoid when:** The plan is a webinar or a pile of self-serve resources; people forget most of it within a day without practice (Rodriguez).
**Steps:**
1. Run a one-hour hands-on workshop: hand out printed company decks, teach design thinking and empathy, give five minutes to define an audience and spot characters, let volunteers ideate a personal story, prototype delivery with hub assets, test it live in the room.
2. Walk through the hub and split non-negotiables (company deck, mission and universal truth, story elements) from negotiables (delivery techniques, brainstorming tools).
3. Repeat the training whenever the story evolves.
**Common mistakes:** Teaching only the story and not the method.
**Example:** A logistics firm gathers drivers and dispatchers for an hour. Pairs pick one customer, sketch the problem, and tell a 60-second story using the shared deck. The deck stays fixed; they choose their own delivery. When the company reframes its mission, the session is rerun.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## F293 · Brand identity matched to buyer identity
**Also known as:** Brand/Buyer Social Identity Model, Unconscious Codes and Archetypes (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Shaping brand personality, choosing an archetype, or diagnosing why a sound ad feels off.
**Avoid when:** The plan contradicts the category's unconscious meaning; buyers disbelieve it (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Decode the category's unconscious code (Rapaille), such as indulgent guilt for pint ice cream.
2. Pick one of the twelve archetypes: Hero, Ruler, Sage, Explorer, Innocent, Outlaw, Magician, Lover, Jester, Caregiver, Creator or Everyperson.
3. Map the brand's vision, beliefs, qualities, personality, look, expression and offers against the buyer's vision and values, beliefs, qualities, personality, look, expression, needs and tension.
4. Lead buyers through categorization (this group is for me), identification (I adopt its norms), comparison (I favor it), ending in loyalty.
**Common mistakes:** Pitching against the category's code, which makes buyers disbelieve the message.
**Example:** A sparkling-water startup finds that soda signals reward and water signals duty. It picks the Jester archetype, aims at office workers tense about nagging health habits, and shows drinkers joining a playful "fizz club" instead of lecturing on hydration.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F294 · Relevance, reach, resonance
**Also known as:** The Three Rs of Influence (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Judging whether creative merely fits the category or can trigger fan advocacy.
**Steps:**
1. Relevance: cover topics pertinent to the category; this is only the price of entry.
2. Reach: deliver the message through chosen media so the target segment is exposed.
3. Resonance: align brand identity with buyer identity so core fans echo and amplify the story.
**Common mistakes:** Stopping at relevance and reach, which yields paid impressions with almost no organic sharing.
**Example:** A city transit agency wants more riders under 30. Its posts on route changes (relevance) and its bus-shelter posters (reach) are seen but never passed on. Interviews show its loyal young riders see themselves as people who chose not to own a car, so the agency launches a "car-free since" year badge riders can add to their own profiles and a monthly page of their routes to work. Riders share the badge to say who they are, and friends ask how to start (resonance).
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F295 · Product–story link
**Also known as:** Glaser and Reisinger's Product–Story Link Model (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Placing a brand inside a dramatic narrative in video, audio or print.
**Avoid when:** The plan is a logo flashed at the end of unrelated entertainment; viewers recall the joke and nothing about the brand (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Connect the product's situation to a life situation of the target through an active plot link.
2. Choose one mechanism: a character uses it, it solves the problem, it is essential, it changes behavior, it serves as metaphor, or it drives the plot.
3. Aim for comprehension and narrative transportation so attitude toward brand and product shifts.
**Common mistakes:** A product that can be removed while the plot survives, like a logo shown after an unrelated clip.
**Example:** A short film follows a night nurse stranded after her shift when her car dies. A portable jump-starter in the trunk solves the crisis and gets her to her daughter's recital; the story fails without it.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F296 · Flip one layout expectation
**Also known as:** Helmut Krone's Theory of New Page, "Umgekehrt" (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Designing print, outdoor or visual social work that suffers template fatigue.
**Avoid when:** The flip makes the ad unreadable or unscannable; fluency collapses (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Keep the classic elements inside the page frame: image, headline, subhead, body, call to action, logo.
2. Turn exactly one expectation upside down slightly to create surprise.
**Common mistakes:** Flipping typography or hierarchy so far that the page cannot be read.
**Example:** A language school's poster keeps image, headline, subhead, body, button and logo in their usual places. The one change: the photo shows a traveler seen from behind, staring at a menu she cannot read, instead of a smiling face turned to the camera. Everything else stays familiar, so the surprise reads at a glance.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F297 · Useful content over hype (Youtility)
**Also known as:** Jay Baer's Youtility (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Building content marketing, email flows, search hubs and lead magnets.
**Avoid when:** The resource has no brand or story link; it becomes an anonymous commodity tool (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Find recurring buyer problems close to the purchase: recipes, calculators, maintenance, how-to guides.
2. Give away a resource so useful people would pay for it.
3. Add light brand story and a natural next step, not a hard pitch.
**Common mistakes:** Utility with no attribution.
**Example:** A paint brand publishes a free room-coverage calculator and a short story from a renter who repainted over a weekend. A closing line offers sample pots; nothing else is pushed.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F298 · Truthful PR rules
**Also known as:** Gini Dietrich's Spin Sucks PR Rules (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Pitching journalists, handling a crisis, planning stunts or influencer deals.
**Avoid when:** The plan involves fabricated outrage or paid placements disguised as organic news (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. State only what can be verified; do not stretch claims.
2. Study good fiction and feature journalism to lift unpaid storytelling.
3. Publish fresh, authentic content on owned hubs; turn ambassadors into defenders and engage critics constructively.
4. Run PR together with paid, earned, shared and owned channels rather than as isolated releases.
**Common mistakes:** Defensive denial in a crisis.
**Example:** A rooftop-solar installer wants local press for a new leasing plan. Instead of promising halved bills, it pitches a reporter the measured savings from 40 homes, shaped as a feature about one retired couple's first winter on the plan. It posts the full meter data on its own blog, answers a skeptical engineer's forum thread point by point, and backs the article with a small paid post and its customer newsletter.
**Sources:** Quesenberry
