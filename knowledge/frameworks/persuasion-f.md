# Frameworks — persuasion (F279–F287)

## F279 · Changing a mind in small steps
**Also known as:** Psychological Baby Steps for Changing Minds (Simmons)
**Use when:** Influencing someone entrenched in a rigid assumption, such as people are sheep or guards are apathetic.
**Avoid when:** You are tempted to rush the listener to your conclusion or attack the belief head-on; that strengthens it.
**Steps:**
1. Connect before you convince: start with a story about an experience where you both feel the same emotion.
2. Narrate, in order, a series of particular stories about real people that follow the experiences that won you over.
3. Stop short of preaching so the listener reaches the new conclusion alone.
**Common mistakes:** Pushing the whole turn in one sitting.
**Example:** A shop steward wants a veteran foreman to accept job rotation. He begins with a shared memory of a bad night shift, then tells of two machinists who learned a second station and saved a line. He stops there and lets the foreman say it first.
**Sources:** Simmons

## F280 · Stories matched to protective emotions
**Also known as:** Selling Justice: Two-Step Process for Resistant Audiences (Simmons)
**Use when:** People seem unwilling, unconcerned or unmotivated and protect themselves within a worldview of past injustice.
**Avoid when:** The person has no conscience at all; stories will not reach a rare sociopath, and force or control is needed.
**Steps:**
1. Acknowledge their honorable intentions and real past inequities, then place the small grievance inside a larger story of shared stakes and justice.
2. Pick the story for the defence they hold:
   - Cynical: a verifiable story of walking the talk, with exact event sequence, not promises.
   - Resentful: a story that makes taking the first peacemaking step heroic.
   - Jealous: a fable showing turf wars hand the prize to outsiders.
   - Hopeless: restore your own hope first, then show one small unplanned choice sparking large change.
   - Apathetic: look beneath the not-my-problem wall for disillusioned over-concern and show collaboration feels better than isolation.
   - Greedy: a haunting story about what wealth cannot buy, love and belonging.
**Example:** Two nursing teams on a hospital ward barely speak after a rota dispute, and each waits for the other to apologize. The ward head, speaking to the resentful night team, tells of a nurse who walked over at dawn with coffee and a swap offer before anyone asked. The story makes going first look like the brave move rather than a defeat.
**Sources:** Simmons

## F281 · Story listening to drain resistance
**Also known as:** Storylistening / Verbal Download (Simmons)
**Use when:** A listener is furious, resistant or reciting a rehearsed list of excuses.
**Avoid when:** You would use scene questions as a trap to prove them wrong, or interrupt with analytical questions.
**Steps:**
1. Give warm, curious attention without interrupting or waiting for your turn, so negative emotion bleeds off.
2. Ask non-adversarial scene questions to take them back to the original event: when did it last happen, where, who was there, what happened next.
3. Hold silence so they can hear themselves; speakers often unravel their own assumptions and invite your view.
**Common mistakes:** Asking for an example in order to rebut it.
**Example:** A customer storms into a bike shop furious about a repair. The owner listens without a word, then asks when she picked the bike up and who was at the counter. By the end she says the real trouble was a missed call, and asks what he can do.
**Sources:** Simmons

## F282 · Parasocial contact with out-group characters
**Also known as:** The Media Equation and Parasocial Contact (Gottschall)
**Use when:** Reducing prejudice toward unfamiliar or stigmatized groups, or modeling cooperative norms at scale.
**Avoid when:** The portrayal would be flat, disgusting or threatening; it transfers hostility to real people the same way.
**Steps:**
1. Craft rounded, relatable characters from the out-group without heavy messaging.
2. Keep audiences exposed repeatedly so they form parasocial friendships.
3. Let the warmth carry over to real members of the group.
**Common mistakes:** A single token appearance; a lecture in place of a character.
**Example:** A children's series follows a refugee family running a corner bakery over two seasons, with a funny grandmother and a teen who is bad at football. Viewers write in about the grandmother, and parents report kids greeting real new neighbours.
**Sources:** Gottschall

## F283 · Diagnosing the storyverse behind a reaction
**Also known as:** The Heider–Simmel Effect and Storyverse Diagnostics (Gottschall)
**Use when:** Reactions to the same facts are polarized, or you want to audit your own reading before writing or acting.
**Avoid when:** Expecting that knowing the reflex switches it off; overriding it takes a deliberate pause and trusted empirical checks.
**Steps:**
1. Observe that unclear events are immediately read as driven by human motives and cast as hero, victim and villain.
2. Identify the governing storyverse, the inherited master narrative that filters which facts are accepted or dropped.
3. Replace automatic certainty with testing and exposure to competing views.
**Common mistakes:** Assuming your own reading is just the facts.
**Example:** After a factory announces layoffs, workers read a betrayal story, shareholders a rescue story. A union organizer writes out each side's plot, then asks both for the one fact their plot cannot explain, and tests it against payroll data.
**Sources:** Gottschall

## F284 · Seven-step business story method
**Also known as:** Storytelling methodology (Parikh)
**Use when:** Preparing any business-critical message: pitch, change initiative, consulting recommendation or executive briefing.
**Steps:**
1. Define the vision: the realistic, immediate outcome after the story is delivered.
2. Understand the audience: profile the organization and individual stakeholders.
3. Frame the problem: construct a hypothesis logic tree and test it with stakeholders in a co-creation workshop.
4. Construct the framework: combine F285 and F286 into a three-layer, one-page blueprint.
5. Prepare the delivery: structure five presentation phases, with linguistic devices, emotional triggers, detail, characters and planned interactions.
6. Visualize data: pick chart types and pre-attentive attributes that reach memory in seven to ten seconds without overloading working memory.
7. Deliver: lead with vocal modulation, presence, structured question handling and composure under resistance.
**Example:** A museum director must persuade trustees to close the main wing for a year of repairs. She defines the vote she needs that evening and profiles each trustee's worries. She tests her hypothesis tree in a workshop with curators and the finance lead, builds a one-page blueprint, rehearses her five phases and shows two clean charts on visitor safety and lost revenue.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F285 · Deductive argument chain
**Also known as:** Deductive argument (Parikh)
**Use when:** Building the main backbone of a business story where each premise can be substantiated.
**Avoid when:** Premises are subjective or easily disputed; if one premise is rejected the whole chain falls.
**Steps:**
1. Start from a provable fact or a point of firm agreement.
2. Chain three to five premises, each moving closer, such as situation, comment, opportunity, solution.
3. State the conclusion or call to action that must hold if all premises do.
4. Test that all relevant information is included, with no omitted counter-facts, and that every premise can be proved.
**Common mistakes:** Leaving out a relevant counter-fact; including a premise that cannot be proved.
**Example:** To justify a third delivery van: orders grew 40 percent; two vans are at capacity; routes cannot be shortened; renting costs more than owning; so buy a third van. Each premise is backed by a log or invoice.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F286 · Inductive argument group
**Also known as:** Inductive argument (Parikh)
**Use when:** Backing a single claim, dealing with subjective evidence, comparing pros and cons, or organizing a status report that spans independent workstreams.
**Avoid when:** You need an air-tight conclusion; this gives a probable one, though losing a reason weakens rather than sinks it.
**Steps:**
1. State the claim at the top.
2. Group three to five independent reasons ordered by priority; to support a positive claim put cons before pros, for a negative claim pros before cons.
3. Check there are three to five reasons (regroup if more), nothing relevant is missing and each reason fits the audience's decision criteria.
**Common mistakes:** Listing more than five reasons without regrouping them.
**Example:** A principal argues for a later school start, a positive claim, so cons come first: bus contracts must be renegotiated, after-school sports end later and some parents start work early. Then three weightier pros follow in priority order: attendance rises, first-period grades improve and nurse visits for exhaustion drop. Each reason is checked against what the school board weighs most.
**Sources:** Parikh

## F287 · Six keys to a moving story
**Also known as:** Six Keys to Powerful Stories (Biesenbach)
**Use when:** Evaluating or shaping any business, brand or leadership story so it provokes empathy and action instead of reading like a fact sheet.
**Steps:**
1. Tap emotion, which unlocks decisions and memory.
2. Put a human face on the issue, not a process or program.
3. Connect through universal experience rather than industry mechanics.
4. Humanize the teller by revealing values and personality.
5. Raise the stakes to values people hold dear, such as family, health or safety.
6. Show through specific action, not generic labels.
**Common mistakes:** Reporting metrics where a person's moment would do.
**Example:** A university provost preparing a speech to alumni donors on expanding student counseling starts with a five-week wait time. She rebuilds it around one first-year student who walked to the counseling office three times and left each time, a feeling of being lost that any former student recognizes. She admits she did the same in her own first year, which shows who is asking. The stakes rise from a wait-time figure to a young person's health, shown through one act: the student emailing her parents that she was coming home.
**Sources:** Biesenbach
