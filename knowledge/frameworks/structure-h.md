# Frameworks — structure (F078–F088)

## F078 · Worldview break drives catharsis
**Also known as:** Cognitive Frame Break and Controlling Idea (Coyne)
**Use when:** You design a protagonist's arc from early competence through failure to transformation, in any genre.
**Avoid when:** You need a scene-level plotting recipe; this is a unifying lens, not a beat sheet.
**Steps:**
1. Action: the inciting incident poses a problem and the protagonist tries to solve it with existing knowledge and habits.
2. Chaos: a disruptive turn breaks the routine so those habits fail.
3. Frame break: the protagonist sees their lens cannot solve the crisis and rebuilds a wider worldview, or refuses and regresses.
4. Catharsis: in the core event (F077) they act on the new frame, the audience feels release, and the controlling idea is proved.
**Example:** A veteran dispatcher trusts the call script. A storm floods the grid and the script fails. She abandons it, listens to a caller's background noise and reroutes the crew by sound. Her new habit saves the street in the finale.
**Sources:** Coyne

## F079 · Self, close-others and public recognition
**Also known as:** Three Levels of Positive Recognition (Coyne)
**Use when:** You build stakes and trade-offs in performance or maker stories.
**Avoid when:** The story is not about a performance; the model fits that kind mainly.
**Steps:**
1. Name what earns the protagonist's own approval (intrapersonal).
2. Name what earns approval from family, mentors and friends (interpersonal).
3. Name what earns approval from judges, critics or the public (extrapersonal).
4. Set the three against each other so winning one does not secure the others.
**Common mistakes:** Assuming that winning one approval secures the others.
**Example:** A city councilwoman votes against her own party on a housing bill. She believes the vote is right (own approval), but her husband and her campaign manager are furious (close others). The local paper and her district's voters praise her courage (public). She won two of the three approvals, and the one closest to home did not follow.
**Sources:** Coyne

## F080 · Rank versus growth dilemma
**Also known as:** Twin Hierarchies of Status (Coyne)
**Use when:** You craft moral dilemmas for status stories, or diagnose creative stagnation versus selling out.
**Avoid when:** You have no external genre to make the choice visible in a scene.
**Steps:**
1. Define the power hierarchy: rank, praise, haves against have-nots, possessions.
2. Define the growth hierarchy: learning, craft and a personal moral code.
3. Present the big choice: opportunities, challenges and a mentor, or the absence of one, push the protagonist to sell out for rank or risk failure to keep integrity.
**Common mistakes:** Over-committing to either hierarchy: refusing to ship for endless growth, or pushing out unedited work for rank.
**Example:** A pastry chef is offered a franchise deal that needs her recipe simplified. She weighs the contract against her sourdough standard, and a retired mentor refuses to advise her. She turns down the contract and risks the lease.
**Sources:** Coyne

## F081 · Entertain, conceal, reveal progression
**Also known as:** Story's Three-Part Progression (Schwabauer; Sweet)
**Use when:** The narrative must change how the audience sees reality instead of merely informing them.
**Avoid when:** The piece is an anecdote or pure information with no voice, setting or motivating moral force.
**Steps:**
1. Entertain: stir the audience's emotions first so they engage.
2. Conceal: bury the core truth in character actions and plot turns so casual listeners pass it by while attentive ones keep it.
3. Reveal: demonstrate the truth through consequences played out on stage, not a stated moral.
**Common mistakes:** Stating the moral instead of letting enacted consequences demonstrate it.
**Example:** A bank's fraud-awareness podcast for retirees opens with a comic bidding war between two neighbors at an estate sale. Buried in the banter, the winner pays with a check mailed by a relative abroad and the seller hands over the furniture that day. The episode ends with the seller at the teller window as the check bounces, and no host explains the lesson.
**Sources:** Schwabauer; Sweet

## F082 · Where the conclusion sits: deductive, inductive, abductive
**Also known as:** The Three Story Pyramids (Schwabauer; Sweet); Deductive, Inductive, and Abductive Stories (Schwabauer; Sweet)
**Use when:** You choose an architecture by whether you want to push a claim, teach a quick lesson or invite discovery.
**Avoid when:** Your audience will not ask questions; abductive pieces get misread. Brief pointed lessons suit inductive.
**Steps:**
1. Deductive: lead with the general thesis, then descend into selected supporting specifics (hard news, bullet-point talks).
2. Inductive: begin with specific events and climb to an explicitly stated moral (fables).
3. Abductive: begin in specifics, stop just short of the principle, and leave listeners to infer it (parables, novels, films).
4. Match the shape to the job; the abductive gap suits lasting change.
**Example:** A product lead at a sprint review reads a support ticket from a user who built a forty-tab spreadsheet to work around a missing export button. She shows a screenshot of the spreadsheet, stops there, and asks the team what they think should ship next, leaving the conclusion to them.
**Sources:** Schwabauer; Sweet

## F083 · Theme as a contest of principle against power
**Also known as:** Story's Moral Compass: Principle versus Power (Schwabauer; Sweet)
**Use when:** You define the thematic stakes and moral limits that give a character arc meaning.
**Avoid when:** You are tempted to extract the theme as a bare formula; it loses persuasive power outside the story.
**Steps:**
1. Set opposing ideals: relational ones (love, honesty, courage, forgiveness) against self-exalting ones (hatred, lies, revenge, control).
2. Keep the standard perfect, comprehensible and uncompromising: the character changes, never the standard.
3. Give the self-serving side real freedom and force on stage so commitment to principle is tested.
**Common mistakes:** Giving the self-serving side less than full freedom; changing the standard instead of the character.
**Example:** A newsroom drama pits an editor who insists on printing the correction against a publisher with an advertiser threatening to leave. The advertiser does leave. The correction still runs and the paper's word is proved worth something.
**Sources:** Schwabauer; Sweet

## F084 · Overlapping internal and external contexts
**Also known as:** Overlapping Contexts: The Road to Relevance (Schwabauer; Sweet)
**Use when:** You design setting, chronology and resonance so the story feels meaningful rather than escapist.
**Avoid when:** Your audience lives mainly through online avatars; relevance then comes out artificial and temporary.
**Steps:**
1. Internal context: order characters, images and events so each detail adds meaning.
2. External context: respect the audience's embodied, physical life, not just spectacle.
3. Overlap: build scenes from shared kinds of human experience so the story world meets the listener's.
**Example:** A story about a night-shift nurse plants a cold vending-machine coffee in scene one, which she gives away in the last scene. Readers who have waited in hospital corridors feel their own hours in it.
**Sources:** Schwabauer; Sweet

## F085 · Parallel narration and chiasmus
**Also known as:** Parallel Narration (Reflectivity, Chiasmus, and Four-Layer Meaning) (Schwabauer; Sweet)
**Use when:** You organize long-form stories, or read ancient oral narratives for hidden pivots.
**Avoid when:** The audience only skims; symmetry is heard better than scanned.
**Steps:**
1. Event parallelism: let visible events mirror an unseen internal or spiritual conflict.
2. Structural parallelism: arrange episodes A-B-C-D-C'-B'-A', placing the theme at the central pivot D or on the outer edges.
3. Meaning parallelism: stack four levels: literal, typological, metaphorical, revelational.
**Example:** A family saga opens with a flooded kitchen and a sister leaving, and closes with a dry kitchen and her return. The middle chapter, where the father admits his debt, is the pivot.
**Sources:** Schwabauer; Sweet

## F086 · Expectation, tension, revelation (jack-in-the-box)
**Also known as:** Jack-in-the-Box Structure (Schwabauer; Sweet)
**Use when:** You are pacing a three-act story and want an ending that seems both unavoidable and surprising.
**Avoid when:** The opening takes long to set expectations, or the revelation has no earned middle tension.
**Steps:**
1. Expectation (about the first quarter): set the hero, goal, stakes and genre promise.
2. Tension (middle): attack the story's core belief with disasters, which worsen plans, and dilemmas, which offer two terrible choices, until the belief looks foolish.
3. Revelation (climax): release a resolution that was foreshadowed yet unexpected, validating the belief because the audience suffered the doubt.
**Example:** A small crew insists that checking each other's work matters. A bad audit, then a client demanding they skip it, then a double-booked deadline. At the climax the skipped check, foreshadowed earlier, is the thing that saves the launch.
**Sources:** Schwabauer; Sweet

## F087 · Inductive and deductive story building
**Also known as:** Inductive vs. Deductive Storybuilding (Maguire)
**Use when:** You start from a vivid memory seeking its meaning (inductive) or illustrate a chosen theme or value (deductive).
**Avoid when:** Your sensory detail stays generic; deductive building then collapses into propaganda.
**Steps:**
1. Inductive: begin with concrete details of a lived event and let the point emerge.
2. Deductive: choose four or five abstractions to convey.
3. Tie every abstraction to specific people, places, objects and events, drawing on all five senses.
4. Draft only from those sensory realities and cut explicit preaching.
**Common mistakes:** Preaching explicitly; keeping the sensory grounding generic.
**Example:** A teller wants to convey patience. Instead of saying so, she gives her uncle's cracked thermos, the smell of pine shavings, and him sanding one chair leg for an hour while the radio plays.
**Sources:** Maguire

## F088 · Story within a story
**Also known as:** Story-Within-a-Story (Hybrid Tales) (Maguire); Mixed-Breed / Hybrid Stories (Maguire)
**Use when:** A personal memory feels too small or too exposed, or echoes a well-known tale.
**Avoid when:** The anecdote is short; two stories overcomplicate it.
**Steps:**
1. Pick a structure: your frame around a traditional tale; interweaving both, the tale as lead-in or running metaphor; or a story about your reaction to a tale.
2. Match on emotional resonance, not only surface plot.
3. Balance transitions so neither story overwhelms the other.
4. A blend of a high-stakes frame with a light everyday one also counts as a hybrid.
**Common mistakes:** A borrowed tale that matches plot but not feeling; a long tale that buries the personal one.
**Example:** A software team lead closing a year-end retrospective frames the release her team rescued, each engineer pooling a half-finished fix, inside the folk tale of Stone Soup. She cuts between a villager adding a carrot and an engineer adding a cache patch, and ends with the travelers walking off with their stone as the release goes live.
**Sources:** Maguire
