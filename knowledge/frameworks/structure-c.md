# Frameworks — structure (F023–F033)

## F023 · Scene and sequel units
**Also known as:** Scene Structure: Goal, Conflict, Disaster (Barker & Brown); Scene-and-Sequel (Haven); Nine-Element Scene Arc and Scene Train (Emunds)
**Use when:** Planning or fixing individual scenes, carding a breathless list of events, or chaining chapters.
**Avoid when:** Do not add an explicit crisis reflection when the action already implies the choice; it turns melodramatic (Emunds). Sequels hold feeling and decision, not plot action (Haven).
**Steps:**
1. Scene: give the viewpoint character a specific goal, with a scene question (Haven).
2. Block it with opposition, ideally from the character's own flawed choices (Barker & Brown).
3. End on an outcome, usually a setback, that changes the situation and leaves something open.
4. Sequel: a short zero-action beat answering what the character feels, what it means, and what they decide next (Haven).
5. Let the decision or outcome become the stimulus of the next scene (scene train, Emunds).
6. Fuller version (Emunds): orientation, stimulus and stakes, goal, adversity, complications, external stake shift, reflection with a choice, action, outcome.
7. Put quiet valley scenes between peaks, keeping tension simmering (Barker & Brown).
**Common mistakes:** Scenes that end resolved; every scene at the same intensity; the sequel narrating events.
**Example:** Scene: Noor must get a permit stamped before the office closes; the clerk demands a missing form. She fails. Sequel: she is furious, realizes the form was her own oversight, and decides to ask the clerk's supervisor. That choice opens the next scene.
**Sources:** Barker & Brown; Haven; Emunds

## F024 · Relationship Cycle
**Also known as:** The Relationship Cycle (Barker & Brown)
**Use when:** Designing a central bond (lovers, friends, siblings, partners, rivals) that drives a subplot.
**Avoid when:** As a rigid recipe; balance big clashes with small awkward moments to avoid melodrama (Barker & Brown).
**Steps:**
1. Initial friction: mutual dislike or resistance.
2. Forced proximity: circumstances make them interact.
3. Collaboration on a shared problem draws them closer.
4. Breach: unmet expectations cause a new conflict.
5. Crisis and repair: they reunite in a crisis, forgive and bond for good.
**Common mistakes:** Skipping the breach; resolving without a crisis.
**Example:** Two cofounders, a cautious accountant and a risky designer, are put in one office after a merger. They rescue a failing pitch together, fall out when she cuts his prototype, and settle it when a server crash needs both skills.
**Sources:** Barker & Brown

## F025 · Wound, false belief and outer goal arc
**Also known as:** Misbelief arc (Barker & Brown); Identity to Essence (Hauge); Want versus need (D'Costa); Conjoined Selves (Schwabauer & Sweet); Two Levels of Story (Storr)
**Use when:** Linking inner change to plot, so the outcome feels earned; or easing a buyer's fear of change in a sales story.
**Avoid when:** The outer conflict is strong enough alone; the inner journey is optional in marketing stories (Hauge). A flawless ideal hero reverses the rule (Schwabauer & Sweet).
**Steps:**
1. Name the early wound and the false belief or protective identity it created.
2. Give an outer goal, launched by the inciting incident, that the character thinks will fix the inner pain. Make the stakes worth suffering for.
3. Let distorted tactics backfire and worsen things.
4. Let outer crises crack inner certainty, and inner breakdowns push suppressed behavior into scenes, causing new outer chaos (Storr).
5. Add a supporting figure who gives courage at moments of doubt (Hauge).
6. At the climax, force the drop of the belief (want gives way to need) or ruin; settle both tracks, and let the outcome state the theme.
**Common mistakes:** Two unconnected plots; a flaw that never costs anything; a theme stated instead of shown.
**Example:** A veteran baker believes asking for help means failure, after a partner betrayed him. He chases a national award alone, burns out, and ruins the final batch. A young apprentice steps in; he accepts help, and the award now matters less than the shop he rebuilds.
**Sources:** Barker & Brown; Hauge; D'Costa; Schwabauer & Sweet; Storr

## F026 · One-sentence story promise
**Also known as:** The Story Promise (Emunds); One-Sentence Logline Template (Barker & Brown); 25-word High Concept test (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** After first inspiration or a first draft, to steer drafting, test coherence, audit subplots or pitch.
**Avoid when:** For marketing blurbs, omit the climax and key ability to avoid spoilers (Emunds). One sentence captures a single trajectory; a novel needs at least two (Barker & Brown).
**Steps:**
1. Name the adversity or antagonist.
2. State the stakes or inciting incident.
3. Name the protagonist and goal; use the template: when A happens, B must do C, otherwise D (Barker & Brown).
4. Add the ability that flips the climax and keep the full sentence private for drafting (Emunds).
5. Cap at 25 words covering what, who, conflict, world, stake and tone; cut whatever does not serve relevance and relatability (Sommer Paulsen).
6. Repeat the template for every subplot. If it cannot be written, you have a situation, not a story.
**Common mistakes:** A theme in place of a trigger; no stakes; no active course of action.
**Example:** When a flood closes the town's only bridge, a school bus driver, Ines, must find another route for forty children before the river crests at dusk, or they spend the night stranded.
**Sources:** Emunds; Barker & Brown; Sommer Paulsen

## F027 · Audience Transformation Journey
**Also known as:** The Audience Journey and Innermost Journey (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** The audience itself is the protagonist: experiences, attractions, programs, brand journeys.
**Avoid when:** Entry is confusing, touchpoints lack cohesion or the audience has no agency; these break it (Sommer Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. Engagement: spark awareness and draw them toward the experience.
2. Recognition of lack: reveal the curiosity, desire or gap that prompts action.
3. Threshold: remove friction so they cross from everyday life into the story world.
4. Ordeal: deliver a peak challenge, insight or transformative encounter.
5. Reinvention: return them to everyday life with lasting memory or action.
**Common mistakes:** Making guests watch an outside hero; onboarding that blocks the threshold.
**Example:** A museum's night program starts with a hallway puzzle sent to visitors days before, a door that opens only after they solve it, a final dark room where they decide a case, and a card to take home.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen

## F028 · Story Sphere
**Also known as:** Story Sphere and Integrated Story Sphere (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** Building a transmedia property, attraction, game or campaign from premise outward, or auditing how audience entry changes the world.
**Avoid when:** Layers are added without alignment to the core premise; that gives incoherent worldbuilding (Sommer Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. Write the core premise in 25 words or fewer.
2. Build outward: storyline, characters, creatures, objects, space, world, and the universe rules (physics, logic, genre).
3. Check every layer against the core.
4. In execution, place the audience and the experience concept at a double core, separated from the layers and context by an interface layer.
**Common mistakes:** Decorative objects unrelated to the premise; changing genre rules by accident.
**Example:** Premise: a village where lost things return a day late. Characters: a ferryman; objects: umbrellas that arrive after rain; rule: nothing returns to its owner.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen

## F029 · Modular Micro Stories
**Also known as:** Modular Storytelling and Micro Stories (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** Designing interactive stories, spaces, branching campaigns or adaptive narratives where order may change.
**Avoid when:** You cannot hold strict entry and exit discipline; badly bounded modules break continuity (Sommer Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. Break a linear structure (see F021, F005) into self-contained modules.
2. Describe each one through its content (the events), its context (place, time, social setting) and its control (who steers progress).
3. Give every module standard rational and emotional entry and exit states.
4. Group compatible modules into clusters with matching states; if adaptive, add observation of storyline, movement and audience traits.
**Common mistakes:** Modules that depend on one fixed predecessor; reordering that silently changes the meaning.
**Example:** A museum trail has six rooms about one shipwreck. Each room starts with the visitor curious and ends with a stated doubt, so rooms can be visited in any order.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen

## F030 · Audience Agency Matrix
**Also known as:** Levels of Influence by Points of Impact matrix (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** Balancing passive delivery against interactivity, co-creation or user content.
**Avoid when:** High influence is granted without narrative guardrails; it can derail meaning (Sommer Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. Pick an influence level: observation, exploration, interaction, influence, co-creation, creation (a seventh, regeneration, via generative AI).
2. Cross it with each point of impact: world, space, objects, creatures, characters, storylines.
3. Write each cell as a rule: my audience can [level] with the [point].
4. Trace the consequences of each allowed choice to confirm the meaning survives.
**Common mistakes:** Unlimited choice; untested branches.
**Example:** At a pop-up cafe game, guests may explore the space, interact with objects and influence characters, but only observe the storyline. We check that moving a prop cannot spoil the ending.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen

## F031 · Hybrid Design Dimensions
**Also known as:** Hybrid Thinking and the 18 Integrated Storytelling Design Dimensions (Sommer Paulsen)
**Use when:** Designing cross-functional omnichannel experiences, large attractions or organization-wide storytelling.
**Avoid when:** Treating dimensions as separate checklists instead of joint iteration (Sommer Paulsen).
**Steps:**
1. Combine analytical, creative and technical thinking; build T-shaped skills; blend concepts; frame the problem through user, business and craft lenses.
2. Align five groups: story, experience, context, mechanics, organisation (18 dimensions in all).
3. Pair horizontal integration (touchpoints) with vertical integration (staff, tech, operations, governance).
4. Back every audience-facing promise with a named operational owner.
**Common mistakes:** Promises with no staffing; story work isolated from operations.
**Example:** A hotel promises a treasure-hunt weekend. The story team writes clues, the tech team builds phone prompts, and housekeeping is trained to place the hidden items, so each promise has a back-stage owner.
**Sources:** Sommer Paulsen

## F032 · Story-to-Myth Continuum
**Also known as:** Story, Narrative, Legend and Myth Continuum (Vincent)
**Use when:** Evaluating brand assets or building a brand story from tactical moments up to timeless meaning.
**Avoid when:** Mistaking a cliché for myth; it yields shallow communication (Vincent).
**Steps:**
1. Beat: the smallest cause-and-effect action that raises and resolves or escalates a question.
2. Story: sequence beats into three acts (situation, complication, resolution) ending in a climax.
3. Narrative: add a theme or point of view that shapes interpretation.
4. Legend: expect consumer-spread stories to mutate unless they carry suspense, familiarity and a moral.
5. Myth: ground it in archetypes such as separation, initiation and return that carry universal moral truth.
**Common mistakes:** Calling scattered episodes a myth.
**Example:** A tea brand strings beats (a late bus, a spilled cup, a stranger's offer) into a three-act ad, gives it the view that warmth is shared, and checks whether customers retell it.
**Sources:** Vincent

## F033 · Plot, Theme and Point of Entry
**Also known as:** Plot, Universal Theme and Point of Entry (Leitman)
**Use when:** Structuring or reviewing a personal story so strangers find it relatable.
**Steps:**
1. Plot: write what literally happens, as a plain summary.
2. Universal theme: state the emotional truth beneath the events.
3. Point of entry: find the moments, details or relationship dynamics where a listener sees their own life.
4. Add several entry points, since each listener finds a different one.
**Common mistakes:** Only plot; a private detail no listener can relate to.
**Example:** Plot: a man teaches his neighbor's daughter to ride a bike. Theme: patience. Entry points: the wobbling moment before letting go, and the neighbor's silent thank-you wave.
**Sources:** Leitman
