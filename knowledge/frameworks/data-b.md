# Frameworks — data (F170–F180)

## F170 · Measurement by key performance questions
**Also known as:** KPQ-First Analytics Framework (Quesenberry)
**Use when:** Setting up dashboards, forecasting demand or proving the return on a story campaign.
**Avoid when:** You lack clean historical variance and proper experimental controls: predictive and prescriptive regression then cannot separate correlation from causation (Quesenberry).
**Steps:**
1. Define the vital few (Marr): 1 to 3 strategic objectives, 1 to 3 key performance questions under each, and 1 to 3 metrics under each question.
2. Descriptive analytics, what happened: summarize history with mean, median and spread, plus metrics such as bounce, share of voice, sentiment, cost per acquisition and return on ad spend. Add clustering, perceptual maps or social network analysis where useful.
3. Predictive analytics, what will happen: estimate demand by judgment, counting (intent surveys), time series, or single-variable association (Winer and Dhar).
4. Prescriptive analytics, what should we do: compare channels with multiple regression or marketing mix models, A/B tests, test markets, or real-time neuro-immersion tests.
**Common mistakes:** Tracking vanity metrics that answer no decision.
**Example:** A neighborhood cafe sets one objective: keep more regulars. One question is what makes a first-time customer return, with median basket size and visits per month as its metrics. A short intent survey estimates how many would join a coffee subscription. The owner then tries the subscription in one of two nearby shops as a test market and compares return visits.
**Sources:** Quesenberry

## F171 · Amplify Only Proven Winners
**Also known as:** Organic Crap Filter and Sponsored Stories Loop (Vaynerchuk); Seed big campaigns from social tests (Vaynerchuk); Micro-Budget Facebook Seeding (Creek)
**Use when:** Allocating paid social budget on feeds where organic reach is throttled.
**Avoid when:** The creative is weak: paid boost cannot rescue it, and link clicks without likes, comments or shares do not trigger viral reach (Vaynerchuk).
**Steps:**
1. Publish native micro-content for free, or aimed at one demographic slice.
2. Measure immediate likes, comments and shares and mark the posts that beat your own baseline.
3. Put sponsored budget behind the top performers, adding interest targeting. Drop or rework the low performers.
4. Reinvest the extra visibility in the next organic and sales posts.
5. For a flagship campaign, test three concepts on separate platforms at once and scale only the organic winner to major media (Vaynerchuk).
6. If a strong video is stuck, a small spend can push it past dormant accounts into active feeds (Creek).
**Common mistakes:** Spending on every post alike; boosting a post that nobody engaged with.
**Example:** A pet-food shop posts three short clips on a Monday: a puppy unboxing, a vet tip and a staff blooper. By evening the vet tip has five times the usual comments. The owner puts 20 dollars behind it, targeted to dog-owner interests, and retires the other two.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk; Creek

## F172 · Approach-findings-implications readout
**Also known as:** The Approach-Findings-Implications (AFI) Framework (McKinsey)
**Use when:** Presenting a research result or forecasting model where the task is to share findings rather than win a contested decision. Also labeled objectives, results, implications, or what, so what, now what.
**Avoid when:** The discovery process itself is the point, as in some scientific experiments (McKinsey).
**Steps:**
1. Act I, approach: state the objective, define baseline terms such as the base case, and outline the quantitative and qualitative method without leaking any result.
2. Act II, findings: lead with the headline finding, then break it down along the main segmentation introduced in Act I, followed by key assumptions and best and worst cases.
3. Act III, implications: translate findings into the structural changes the organization must make, the leading indicators that show which case is unfolding, and the contingency actions for each.
**Common mistakes:** Narrating the research as a chronological random walk with false starts and dead ends. Tell the time, do not build the clock (McKinsey).
**Example:** A hospital analyst presents a pilot of handwashing reminders. Act I: the goal is fewer ward infections, and the method is a six-month comparison of pilot and control wards. Act II: infections fell 18%, split by surgical and medical wards, with the assumption that staffing stayed level. Act III: extend the reminders to two more wards, and watch monthly soap refills as the early signal.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## F173 · Researcher-designer process wheel
**Also known as:** Visualizing Impact's Process Wheel (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** Researchers, writers and designers collaborate on infographics or advocacy campaigns and risk a rough handoff.
**Avoid when:** You cannot build a trusted network of outside challengers over time; the wheel depends on them (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Monitor events, studies and partner needs to pick themes that are strategically and emotionally compelling.
2. Write a living brief: communication objectives, audience, candidate stories and information hierarchy.
3. Explore the data jointly: researchers and designers extract stories and visual concepts together, not by dumping raw tables on designers.
4. Develop the designs with a regular feedback rhythm, such as weekly critique.
5. Bring in an outside challenger (domain expert, test reader or another designer) at the research, story and design stages to catch blind spots, jargon and missed emotional impact.
6. Fact-check and verify sources, and print full citations in the visual's footer.
**Common mistakes:** Handing designers a raw spreadsheet and expecting a finished graphic.
**Example:** A housing nonprofit plans a rent-burden poster. The brief names council staff as the audience and one headline, then the analyst and designer scan the data together. A tenant organizer reads the draft and flags a confusing term. Citations are printed along the footer.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F174 · Feminist data visualization principles
**Also known as:** Core Principles of Feminist Data Visualisation (Feigenbaum, after D'Ignazio and Klein)
**Use when:** Designing datasets, categories, icons or installations about human identity, inequality or marginalized communities.
**Avoid when:** Nothing forces you to drop standard charts; classic formats can be used on purpose where institutional authority serves the advocacy goal (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Rethink binaries: depict multiplicity and spectrums, not forced either-or categories.
2. Embrace pluralism: support multiple perspectives and truths, not a single authoritative view from nowhere.
3. Examine power and encourage empowerment: design so communities can act and build capacity, not just consume.
4. Consider context: show the social, cultural and material origins of the data through participatory collection and design.
5. Treat embodiment and affect as legitimate: pair 2D charts with emotional, sensory and physical forms (walks, quilts, sculptures, comics).
6. Make labor visible: expose metadata, data diaries and the behind-the-scenes work of interns, cleaners, labelers and IT staff.
**Common mistakes:** Treating community members as interview subjects rather than co-designers.
**Example:** A school district charts attendance and replaces the present-or-absent split with a spectrum: on time, late, partial day and remote. A methods page names the office clerks who hand-entered the records, and the lobby shows the same data as a floor map that visitors walk across. The district also holds a session where families explain why mornings are hard in their neighborhood.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F175 · Symbol, colour, caption, editorial pillars
**Also known as:** Four Pillars for Data Storytelling (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** Designing any visual data piece, from one chart to a multi-platform report.
**Avoid when:** Relying on metaphors that do not cross cultures: balloon icons and heat-map colors confused low-health-literacy patients, while a bare-to-leafy tree worked (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Symbol: choose icons (resemble the object), indices (show causal evidence) or symbols (learned by culture) that are resonant, felt instantly, and relevant to the audience's culture.
2. Colour: let differences in hue, value and saturation spotlight the main pattern, a single bold highlight set against muted grey. Check type-to-background contrast and greyscale legibility. Use metaphors for ordinal data, gradients for interval data, and pair color with text or shape for color blindness.
3. Caption: write captions that steer interpretation toward what you want the audience to think, feel and do, and point at what matters.
4. Editorial: plan layout at the very start, smallest display first (phone or A4 before desktop), in a modular grid. Choose or blend a genre: magazine, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show or film.
**Common mistakes:** Leaving a chart uncaptioned so readers choose their own meaning; designing for a big screen and shrinking it later.
**Example:** A clinic makes a poster on neighborhood vaccination rates. The caption steers viewers to think that most neighbors are covered, feel reassured, and book a visit this week. One bold teal bar for the under-vaccinated age group sits among grey bars, with a syringe icon that readers in the area recognize. The layout was drawn for a phone screen before the printed poster.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F176 · Data-warrant-claim evidence maps
**Also known as:** Argumentative Mapmaking, Toulmin Model (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** Building advocacy maps or contested public-interest visualizations from non-institutional data.
**Avoid when:** You cannot sustain rigor and open documentation; without an explicit warrant, citizen maps are easily dismissed (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Data: collect systematic, verified measurements.
2. Warrant: publish the collection, sampling and representation methods online so anyone can check each reading.
3. Claim: frame the spatial or temporal visualization, with past baselines, present hotspots and future projections, so that it compels a concrete policy or safety action.
**Common mistakes:** Showing the map without the method behind it.
**Example:** A wheelchair users' group logs broken elevators at every metro station for three months, with a timestamped photo at each check. It posts the logging form and the fixed check schedule online so anyone can repeat a round. The map shades each station by hours out of service, set against the transit agency's own outage reports from the year before, and projects the hours lost by next winter at the current repair pace. The claim asks the agency to publish live elevator status and fix any outage within a day.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F177 · Twelve map visual variables
**Also known as:** 12 Visual Variables for Cartographic Storytelling (Feigenbaum, after Roth and Bertin)
**Use when:** Building map legends, symbol sets and spatial overlays that go past a platform's stock pins.
**Avoid when:** Assuming an encoding will be read uniformly: readers bring prior beliefs, and physicians read John Snow's cholera map as supporting airborne miasma (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Decide what each attribute (quantity, category, uncertainty) must show.
2. Encode it with one of twelve variables: location, size, shape, orientation, colour hue, colour value (lightness), colour saturation, texture, arrangement of marks inside a symbol, crispness, resolution (level of detail) and transparency.
3. Use crispness, fuzzy versus clear edges, to show uncertainty so estimates do not seem falsely precise.
4. Combine a few variables, for example hue plus size plus fuzzy edges, and explain them in the legend.
**Common mistakes:** Defaulting to identical pins for everything; projecting false precision on contested estimates.
**Example:** A conservation group maps heron nests. Shape shows the species, saturation shows the number of nests, and a hatched texture marks private land. The legend explains the three variables in one box, so volunteers do not read the darker symbols as more important by default.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F178 · Four Cs of data team collaboration
**Also known as:** The Four Cs for Collaboration (Feigenbaum)
**Use when:** Launching or running a project with analysts, researchers, writers, designers or community partners.
**Avoid when:** Deadlines are rigid: participatory, care-centered workflows lengthen timelines (Feigenbaum).
**Steps:**
1. Curiosity: bring designers in from the start so the whole group digs into the data's history, biases and gaps together.
2. Clarity: agree on roles, resources, edit and check-in schedules and pay or budget ceilings, and hold an early jargon-busting meeting to align vocabulary.
3. Coordination: pick tools everyone can reach (shared docs and sheets, group chat), and archive manual copies in a shared repository with standard date and version filenames.
4. Care: put collaborators' wellbeing ahead of completion, including caregiving duties and invisible disabilities, and protect the team from vicarious trauma when handling graphic testimony.
**Common mistakes:** Letting versions live only in auto-saved cloud documents.
**Example:** A drought report team has a hydrologist, a writer, a designer and a community liaison. At the kickoff they sort out that baseline means different things to the hydrologist and the writer. They pick a group chat and a shared sheet that a volunteer translator can open without a work account. Interviews with farmers who lost crops are spaced out so the writer is not hit with testimony back to back.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## F179 · Data-driven content operation
**Also known as:** The Content Operating Wheel / Content Decision Engine (Snow, Lazauskas); Semantic Search & Format Drilling (Snow, Lazauskas)
**Use when:** Scaling storytelling in a complex or regulated organization where manual coordination and guesswork slow a lean team.
**Avoid when:** You expect data to replace judgment; it cannot predict off-screen scandals (Snow, Lazauskas).
**Steps:**
1. Strategy: use social audience data for broad interests, then semantic search queries for the exact questions people ask, then social analysis for the formats and channels where those topics already perform.
2. Plan: form a cross-functional editorial board with legal and compliance rules set up front, and centralize calendars, pitches, freelancers and approvals on one platform.
3. Create: use tone analytics and automatic checks (passive voice, dead links, keywords) to keep voice consistent and draft faster.
4. Activate: spend about a tenth of the production budget on paid social, targeting precise buyer personas with pieces already proven; see F171.
5. Optimize: feed engagement and conversion results back into planning for topics, writers and channel spend.
**Common mistakes:** Setting compliance rules only at review time; planning topics without search evidence.
**Example:** A state university's continuing-education office starts from search data: adult learners keep asking whether they can study while working nights, and short student-diary posts perform best for that topic on social. It forms an editorial board where the registrar sets rules on accreditation claims before anything is drafted, and all pitches and approvals run through one calendar. Automatic checks catch dead links and passive voice in the drafts. About a tenth of the budget boosts the diary post that already drew the most replies, and next term's topics follow the enrolment numbers it produced.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## F180 · Charts designed for three kinds of memory
**Also known as:** The visualization system / Three types of memory (Parikh); Pre-attentive chart highlighting (Parikh); Data-ink maximization (Parikh)
**Use when:** Putting quantitative data in slides or reports to support a decision, so viewers decode the message in 7 to 10 seconds.
**Avoid when:** Readers need to look up exact individual values; then a raw table is right (Parikh).
**Steps:**
1. Iconic memory, the instant filter: make one element stand out with contrast color, size, line thickness or position, or a big-number callout box on the single point to hero.
2. Working memory, capped near four chunks: swap number tables for the matching 2D chart, strip gridlines, decimals, redundant labels and full date strings (write Jan, not a full date), and label lines directly instead of using a legend.
3. Long-term memory: title the chart with a full sentence stating the message, so the decoded insight is stored and the audience feels confident to act.
**Common mistakes:** A topic title such as sales by region; a legend that sends the eye away from the data.
**Example:** A school fundraising team has a slide with a table of spring donations across eight channels, cents included. It becomes a sorted horizontal bar chart with whole-dollar labels on the bars and no gridlines. The text-message appeal bar is orange and the rest grey, with a big callout reading +48k. The title says text appeals raised more than mail and phone combined this spring.
**Sources:** Parikh
