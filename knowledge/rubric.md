# Rubric — scoring a story draft

Score each criterion 0, 1 or 2. The scale, the 20-point total, the rewrite threshold and the five-fix cap are story-engine conventions, not claims from the books. Score the draft as the named audience would hear or read it, not as the author intends it. Cite the R ID and the fix IDs in feedback.

## R01 · Character
**Check:** Who the story follows. Look for one identifiable person, named or clearly placed, who wants something, acts, and has a believable flaw. Ask whose shoes the audience stands in and whether that person resembles them.
**0:** No human at the center. The subject is a company, product, team, data series or process. **1:** A person exists but is generic ("a customer", "our people"), faultless, passive, or the teller or the firm plays the hero in a story meant to move a customer or colleague. **2:** One specific person with a want, a visible flaw or doubt, and real agency carries the story. The offering or the teller helps without leading.
**Typical failure:** A case study where the software is the grammatical subject of every sentence and the client never decides anything (Hauge; Parikh). A saint with no weakness, or a victim to whom things merely happen (Maxwell; Storr; Dicks). The seller as hero in a customer story (Livesay; Hall).
**Fix:** Pick one affected person and make the offering their tool (F288, T082, T589). Choose the case-study hero for resemblance to the audience and a measurable goal (F153). Give a flawed but decent protagonist a specific want (T039, F048). Where the seller must also drive the deal, let seller and customer share the lead and keep the seller active (F275; Maxwell). Make a hard choice the center when the hero is passive (T182).

## R02 · Specific moment
**Check:** Whether the draft lands on one incident at one time and place, or summarizes months, a program or a career. Look for a single instant of change the whole piece points at.
**0:** A chronology, résumé, timeline or general account ("over the years we learned"). **1:** One incident exists but is buried among summary, or the time and place are vague. **2:** One bounded moment with a stated time and place carries the story, and the draft is built so that moment of change is clear.
**Typical failure:** Birth-to-present life story, journal-style reminiscence, or a list of a dozen examples instead of one event (Apps; Leitman; Bowles). A big dramatic event used for its spectacle, while the small human feeling inside it is missing (Dicks; Hall). A thesis announced and then supported by a laundry list.
**Fix:** Zoom into one incident and slow it down (T077). Find the instant of change and rebuild around it (F061). Use the big event only as the vehicle for a small universal feeling (F157, T090). Open as close to the trigger as possible and stop right after the resolution (T234). Mine a private crossroads rather than public facts when none comes to mind (F122).

## R03 · Concrete detail
**Check:** Whether the audience can see the scene. Look for observable behavior, a few precise specifics, exact numbers, names and objects, and plain words. Check that abstract labels ("amazing", "terrible") are replaced by evidence.
**0:** Abstractions, adjectives, jargon and generalities only. **1:** Some specifics, but also stacked adjectives, vague counts and dates ("a couple of years ago"), stock imagery, or filter verbs ("she saw", "he felt"). **2:** A few chosen details (several senses, one telling object or habit) let the audience picture the scene and infer meaning. Every detail earns its place.
**Typical failure:** Naming the trait instead of showing it (Karia; Storr; Cleland). Piling on three adjectives, which dilutes the picture (Guptara). Cliché metaphors that evoke nothing (Storr). Deleting the small sensory detail in the name of brevity until a vivid story is a hollow outline (Hall). Too much description, which is its own drag (Karia).
**Fix:** Replace named traits, emotions and conclusions with behavior and sensation (T074, T638). Drop in a few sights, sounds, smells or touches (T076, F208). Prefer exact over vague (T110, F194). Use one telling detail instead of a catalogue (T221, F047). Strip filter verbs (T637, F193). Put in objects the audience already knows (T079). Keep a detail only if it pays off (T220). Use fresh physical metaphors (T089).

## R04 · Emotion
**Check:** Whether the audience can feel something specific and whether the emotion is earned and honest. Look for the teller's stake, an admitted flaw or fear, and feeling carried by action rather than announced.
**0:** No felt stake. Facts, claims or a moral stand where feeling should be, or an announced feeling with nothing behind it. **1:** Emotion is stated ("it was very hard") or forced: manufactured tears, a rant, a gag, a bragging win, or raw unprocessed pain that worries the audience. **2:** A specific, honest feeling arises from what happens. The teller admits a real struggle or flaw, and the intensity suits the setting.
**Typical failure:** Triumph stories that boast, tragedy stories that sound like a victim, logistics with no inner struggle (Dolan; Leitman; Hauge). Venting a fresh wound as a story (Bowles; Leitman; James). Perfect success with no stumble (Lazer; Dicks; Simmons). Canned humor or invented drama (Leitman; Bowles).
**Fix:** Test readiness: tell it only when the matter is over and you can see what it was about (F143, T217). Open the loop of vulnerability by admitting a real flaw first (F270, T462, T042). Reframe your own win around the struggle and credit others (F259, T486). Use mixed feelings and contrasts rather than a single note (T131). Name the feeling once only when the behavior could be misread (T639). Keep display modest in business settings (T456).

## R05 · Tension and change
**Check:** Whether something is at stake, what opposes the hero, whether events force each other, and whether someone ends changed. Look for an obstacle, a cost, a decision and an observable shift.
**0:** An incident or smooth success: no obstacle, no risk, no change. **1:** An obstacle exists but events are linked by "and then", stakes stay flat or shrink, the climax is coincidence or rescue, or the change is announced rather than shown. **2:** A clear goal meets real opposition, each beat causes or complicates the next, stakes rise, the hero decides and acts, and the change shows in behavior.
**Typical failure:** Episodic "and then" plotting (Rubin; Dicks; Edwards; Aristotle; Simson). Everything went fine (Biesenbach; Haven). Easy wins, a straw-man opponent, or a de-escalating run of risks (Rubin; Maxwell). Resolution by luck, a dream or a deus ex machina (Aristotle; Gottschall; Cleland; Barker). Growth summarized as "then I realized" (Buster; Bowles; Leitman).
**Fix:** Rewrite each link as therefore or but (F004, T033). Check the outline holds character, goal, obstacle, struggle and outcome (F006) and follows a three-act change arc (F021). Order risks from small to large (F011) and add the cost paid on the way (T133, F062). Name a concrete antagonist or two strong forces (T053, T055; Maxwell on keeping the foe beatable). Make the climax a conscious choice (F010) and show the change in an act (T118, F060). Confine chance to the opening (T151).

## R06 · Ending and takeaway
**Check:** Where the story stops, whether the central question is answered, and whether the audience leaves with the point without being lectured.
**0:** The story trails off, or the ending is a lecture, a tidy moral, a coincidence or an unexplained leap. **1:** It resolves but lingers after the climax, ties every thread in a bow, stops on a gag, or states the lesson outright. **2:** It stops soon after the turn, on an image or a plain result. The audience can state the takeaway, either implied by the events or handed over in one brief invitation.
**Typical failure:** Explaining the meaning ("the moral is…", "that's when I realized") (Dolan; Leitman; Hall; Schwabauer). Epilogues that drain the peak (Cleland; Buster; Emunds). Opening with the ending for a story meant to build curiosity (Apps; James). Leaving a business story hanging with no lesson (Biesenbach; Karia). Unforeshadowed trick endings (Cleland; Aristotle).
**Fix:** Stop at the peak and let listeners draw the meaning (T388, T027). Give premises, not the sum (F241, T092). Close on one image (T011) or one repeatable line (T113). Where an action is needed, add one sentence back to the work and phrase it as an invitation (T389). Choose an ending type and plant clues earlier (F042, T057).

## R07 · Focus and economy
**Check:** One point, a strong start and nothing extra. Look for how the first line behaves, how many ideas and people the draft carries, and whether anything could be cut without loss.
**0:** No discernible point, or several points and cast members; opens with a thank-you, an agenda, background or a banner. **1:** A point exists, but the start is slow, exposition is dumped, bystanders are introduced, tangents stay, or the piece runs over the time or word budget. **2:** One point governs the whole. It opens inside the action or the situation, every character and detail has a job, and exposition arrives inside scenes.
**Typical failure:** Packing several lessons into one story (Guptara; Kamath; D'Costa). Throat-clearing, a memoir-style introduction or a company timeline as the opening (Biesenbach; Karia; Dolan). Naming every peripheral person and every date (Dolan; Leitman). Background halting the scene (Barker; Dicks; Emunds).
**Fix:** Write the point as one sentence and test every scene against it (F142, T631, T218). Cut scenes without a point (T610). Start inside the action (T048, T234). Remove idle characters and group minor ones (T222, T293). Put backstory inside a scene (T095). Cut to the time available (T230).

## R08 · Fit to the brief's objective
**Check:** Score against the brief, not the draft's own claims. What should the audience think, feel, know or do afterward? Does the chosen story, and every beat, push toward it?
**0:** No stated objective, or the story would change nothing (it entertains but does not serve the aim), or it points at a different aim. **1:** The story fits in outline but one element pulls against the goal: a flattering anecdote, the wrong protagonist, a point never connected to the ask. **2:** The objective is stated as an outcome in the audience, and the story's hero, event, tension and ending all serve it. The link to the commercial or organizational point is explicit.
**Typical failure:** Beginning from what we want to say, not what the audience should do (Edwards; Parikh). Telling an emotional but wrong story, so listeners volunteer when the aim was a donation (Hall). A pointless story told for its own sake (Hall; Mastropierro). A story that never ties to the argument (Mastropierro). A decorative story where a plain fact was needed (Callahan; Gottschall).
**Fix:** Write the outcome you want in the audience (T527, F274). Build a brief before drafting (F146). Create the story from the audience and goal backward (F101). Pick the detail or event that supports the commercial point (T482). Give plain facts when the listener is short of time and wants a quick answer; reserve story for moments of openness (Callahan; Gottschall).

## R09 · Audience fit
**Check:** Whether vocabulary, detail, protagonist, tone, format and length suit these listeners, and whether the draft starts from their concerns.
**0:** Written for everyone, for the author, or for the firm: jargon, insider detail, or a hero the audience cannot identify with. **1:** Partly tailored but with seams: some jargon, a hero whose world is distant, tone or humor that may land wrongly, or the audience's own priorities appear late. **2:** Words, references and the protagonist come from the audience's world. The opening lands on their problem, and tone, length and humor suit their culture, rank and situation.
**Typical failure:** Presenter-centered pitch, we-focused copy and bragging introductions (Livesay; Parikh). Elite or hyper-technical examples (Callahan; Mastropierro). Jargon even when defined (Lazer). Demographics only, with no knowledge of motives (Butler; Rodriguez). Assuming the workforce shares the leader's cause (Maxwell). A story exported unchanged to another culture (Sandler; Forman).
**Fix:** Profile the audience before drafting (F158, F113, F115). Start from their strategy and problem (F224). Use plain spoken words and the reading level of the room (T317, T652). Bring in things they know (T079). Adapt to culture, rank and buyer type (F264, F229). Ask whether a listener who has just been reprimanded would hear your word as a threat (Edwards).

## R10 · Credibility
**Check:** Whether the audience would believe it and whether it is honest. Look for a story the teller lived or credited, claims that match real behavior, setbacks admitted, and respect for people in it.
**0:** Invented or borrowed as one's own, contradicted by known facts, or exposes or demeans real people. **1:** Basically true, but too neat, one-sided, inflated, missing credit, or with an unrepresentative exception passed off as the rule. **2:** True, or clearly signaled where invented. Setbacks and counter-evidence are shown, claims match what the firm does, and real people are protected or credited.
**Typical failure:** Fabricating the plot to look cooler (Leitman; Dolan). Taking someone else's story as one's own (Callahan; James; Dicks). A smooth Pollyanna pitch (Callahan; Hauge). A story that contradicts company practice (Snow; Maxwell). Airbrushed customers or hired actors (Hall; Forman). Sold hype before the offering has proof (Cowie; Maxwell).
**Fix:** Edit only in the permitted ways and keep the emotional truth (F156, T224). Tell stories you lived, or tell your part of another's (T232, T228). Show real drawbacks and flaws (T462). Signal where fact ends and invention begins (T619). Protect real people (T622). Verify company legends before repeating them (T653). Keep customers' unpolished voices (T623).

## Using the rubric

Story-engine convention, not a book claim.

- Total out of 20. A draft below 12, or with a 0 on R01, R05 or R08, goes back for a rewrite before polish.
- Score R01 to R05 first: they decide whether the draft is a story at all (F006). If two or more score 0, restructure with F021 before line edits.
- Score R08 and R09 against the brief, not the draft's own claims.
- Report the two lowest criteria first, each with one concrete change and its IDs. Give at most five fixes per round.
- Delivery (voice, pauses, slides) is out of scope here except where a written draft prescribes it; see frameworks/delivery-a.md to delivery-d.md and techniques/delivery-a.md, delivery-b.md.
