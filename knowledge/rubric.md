# Rubric — scoring a business story draft

Score each criterion 0, 1 or 2. The scale, the total out of 20, the rewrite threshold and the five-fix cap are story-engine conventions, not claims from the books. Total out of 20. A draft below 12, or with a 0 on R01, R05 or R08, goes back for a rewrite before polish. Score the draft as it would be heard or read by the named audience, not as the author intends it. Cite the R ID and the fix IDs in feedback.

## R01 · Character
**Check:** Who the story follows. Look for one identifiable person with a name or a clear role, who wants something and acts. Check whose eyes the audience sees through and whether that person resembles the audience in values or stakes.
**0:** No human at the center: the subject is a company, product, team, value or timeline. **1:** A person appears but is generic ("a customer", "our people"), faultless, or the teller or the firm takes the hero role in a sales or change story. **2:** One specific, believable person with a small flaw or doubt drives the action, and the audience can stand in their shoes; the product or firm, if present, helps rather than leads.
**Typical failure:** A case study in which the software is the subject of every sentence and the client never makes a decision (Hall; Edwards; Guptara). A one-dimensional hero with no weakness, which listeners distrust (Maxwell).
**Fix:** Recast with F21 and the hero check in F02; demote the offering with T024; for sales and pitches, make the customer the hero and the teller the guide (F03, S09); name only the central person (T105). Where the seller must also drive the deal, let the seller act alongside the customer rather than wait passively (Maxwell).

## R02 · Specific moment
**Check:** Whether the draft lands on one incident that happened at a particular time and place, or stays at the level of a summary across months or a whole program.
**0:** Pure summary or general claim ("over the years we learned that..."); no time or place marker. **1:** A time or place is mentioned, but the telling skims across several events without slowing down on any. **2:** The draft opens or quickly arrives at one scene with a time or place marker and slows down at the instant something changed.
**Typical failure:** Describing a whole onboarding project in one sweep instead of the afternoon the first shipment failed (Hall; Callahan). Opening by debating the exact year aloud, or spending three sentences on career history before anything happens (Dolan).
**Fix:** Open with T002; zoom in with T003; if the draft has many scenes, keep only the emotional high points with T004. Test against the time-or-place marker and connected events in F02 (Callahan).

## R03 · Concrete detail
**Check:** Sensory and specific nouns, real dialogue, objects the audience knows. Also check the opposite: details that serve no purpose.
**0:** Abstract throughout: adjectives, jargon, euphemisms, no object, sound or line of speech a listener could picture. **1:** Some concrete detail, but stacked adjectives, reported speech ("he said it was fine"), or clutter such as exact timestamps and distances that do nothing for the point. **2:** A few precise details, at least one the audience recognizes from their own lives, quoted dialogue at the key beat, and no detail that fails the relevance test.
**Typical failure:** Editing out the small things in the name of brevity, leaving a hollow outline (Hall). The opposite: tangents and measurements that only matter to the teller (Dolan; Parikh). Corporate wording that drains the scene (Guptara; Dolan).
**Fix:** Add one sense beyond sight with T101; swap adjective piles for one exact detail with T102; use objects from the listeners' world with T103; quote with T106; translate jargon with T104; delete what fails T028. For a big statistic, anchor it in one named person with T045 (Parikh).

## R04 · Emotion
**Check:** Whether the reader or listener is likely to feel something, and whether that feeling is shown through action and reaction rather than named or claimed. Check the dose and the direction of the emotion against the goal.
**0:** No emotional stakes, or the emotion is faked, inflated or used to manipulate (raw trauma, guilt-tripping, fear piled on fear). **1:** Emotion is named ("I was frustrated", "the team was thrilled") or muted into business euphemism; or it is real but out of proportion to the setting. **2:** Feeling is visible in what people do, say and notice; the dose suits the room; fear or urgency, if used, is brief and followed by a path to hope.
**Typical failure:** Treating emotion as unprofessional and presenting only logic, which leaves the decision without a reason to feel right (Edwards; Dolan). Overusing cliff-hangers or threats until the room shuts down (Parikh; Simmons). A tearful story the teller has not yet processed (Guptara; Dolan; Hall).
**Fix:** Show through action with T100; use plain emotion words with T104; in analytical rooms, permit a reaction with T044; dose urgency with T087; for resistant audiences, choose the emotion to address with F33. Check the story's intimacy level against the setting with S03 and T107.

## R05 · Tension or change
**Check:** Whether something goes wrong, surprises, or forces a choice, and whether the situation or a belief at the end differs from the start. Look for a problem the audience feels and an honest account of difficulty.
**0:** Nothing is at stake: a list of achievements, a smooth sequence of steps, or a timeline with no turn. **1:** A problem is stated but resolved instantly, kept vague, or every obstacle is listed before any solution, so the draft feels either frictionless or bleak. **2:** A clear turn breaks the ordinary situation, the cost of not solving it is felt, and the ending shows a visible change in circumstances or belief.
**Typical failure:** The flawless pitch or strategy story where nothing ever went wrong, which kills both tension and credibility (Edwards; Callahan). Skipping the ordinary state, so the crisis arrives without context (Hall). Inventing a straw villain the product cannot honestly beat (Maxwell).
**Fix:** Rebuild the spine with F01, drafting the turn first with T001; for decision audiences use F04 (threat) or F05 (reward); interleave problems and solutions with T010; admit weaknesses with T073 and blemish credibility per Parikh; name a real, beatable antagonist with F34.

## R06 · One point, tight shape
**Check:** Whether the draft serves a single point that can be stated in one sentence, whether every beat advances it, and whether its length suits the channel.
**0:** Several competing points, or no discernible point ("story for story's sake"). **1:** One point is present but buried among side messages, backstory or a research random walk; spoken stories run well beyond two minutes. **2:** One point, stated or clearly implied, with every beat causally linked to the next; a spoken story fits in one to two minutes, a pitch story in under a minute.
**Typical failure:** Loading three lessons into one anecdote (Guptara). Recounting every dead end of an analysis (McKinsey). A ten-minute board story (Dolan). Trying to tell buyers everything the company might ever do (Livesay).
**Fix:** Reduce to one sentence with T007 and say it before the story with T006; check the causal chain in F01; budget length with T064; cut discovery steps and move proof to an appendix with T032. For data-heavy drafts, isolate the single insight with F23.

## R07 · Ending and takeaway
**Check:** How the draft stops. Look for the link back to the business point, who draws the moral, and whether the close tells the audience where to go next.
**0:** No ending (the draft trails off, closes on "any questions?") or the moral is lectured at length. **1:** The point is landed but over-explained ("the moral of the story is..."), or the ending introduces new open questions, or there is no next step when one is needed. **2:** The action ends, a single bridge sentence connects to the business point in inclusive language, the audience is left to complete the meaning, and where the brief requires it a concrete next step follows.
**Typical failure:** Explaining the moral after the story, which takes the conclusion away from the listener (Callahan; Dolan; Guptara). Racing past the last line without a pause (Dolan). Ending a deck with a summary that raises new "how" questions (McKinsey).
**Fix:** Land with T008; close the loop to the opening with T005; pause with T053; in pitches close with T066; for memorability, consider T013. In a deck, resolve every open question before the last slide (F04).

## R08 · Fit to the brief's objective
**Check:** Compare the draft with the brief: what the audience should think, feel, know or do afterwards. Ask whether this story, told well, would move this audience to that outcome, or to a different one.
**0:** The draft pursues a different goal from the brief, or no audience outcome can be stated. **1:** The outcome is plausible but unsupported: the hero or turn models a related but wrong action, the ask is too big for the moment, or the story arrives in the wrong place in the argument. **2:** Every beat moves the audience toward the stated outcome; the hero and turn model the exact behavior wanted; the story sits where it does the most work (before the argument for skeptics, conclusion first for busy deciders).
**Typical failure:** A moving story that inspires the wrong behavior, such as a fundraising story that makes people want to volunteer instead of give (Hall). An objective framed as an activity ("inform the client") or as an unrealistic leap for one meeting (Parikh). Asking for the close when the prospect barely knows you (Livesay).
**Fix:** Rewrite the target state with T022; audit each paragraph with F46; reposition with F32 (story before argument) or T009 (conclusion first or last); size the ask with F37; if the action is wrong, change the hero or turn with F21. Use the goal map in business.md to pick the story type.

## R09 · Audience fit
**Check:** Whether the people, details, words and stakes are drawn from the audience's world, whether the draft answers what they silently ask, and whether it fits how they decide.
**0:** Teller-centered: "we" language, the teller's interests, references the audience cannot relate to, or a tone that talks down. **1:** Relevant in topic but generic: not tied to their strategy or pressures, mismatched to their decision style, or ignoring a known objection. **2:** The audience recognizes itself: its situation, vocabulary and doubts appear; the angle matches how they decide; the story stands beside them rather than above them.
**Typical failure:** Pitching the firm's features before the client's published priorities (Parikh). Elitist or hyper-technical anecdotes (Callahan). A personal childhood story the audience does not share (Edwards). Contempt for a resistant group leaking into the telling (Simmons).
**Fix:** Profile with F22; answer the silent trust questions with F19 (S07, S08); match decision style with F36; pre-empt the doubt with S11; adopt their words with T091 and T079; frame insights as reminders with T075; pair gain with safety with T074.

## R10 · Truth and credibility
**Check:** Whether the story is true, told by or credited to the person who lived it, consistent with the teller's actions, and representative of the wider facts.
**0:** Fabricated, borrowed without credit, or contradicted by known facts or the teller's own behavior. **1:** True but polished until it reads as fake, told second-hand by a spokesperson, hyped beyond current results, or an exception presented as typical. **2:** Verifiable, credited where borrowed, told in the participant's own voice with rough edges kept, claims sized to what has actually been achieved, and counter-evidence acknowledged.
**Typical failure:** Telling another person's anecdote as one's own (Callahan; Dolan). Using actors or airbrushed testimonials (Hall; Forman). One success story standing in for a weak overall record, or claims that run ahead of the evidence (Forman).
**Fix:** Run T107 before telling; let the person who lived it speak with T023; collect and keep customer stories unpolished with F18; pair a story with a fact sheet for analytical audiences with F24; replace blame or spin with true stories per S23.

## Using the rubric

Story-engine convention, not a book claim.

- Score R01 to R05 first: they decide whether the draft is a story at all (F02). If two or more score 0, restructure with F01 before line edits.
- Score R08 and R09 against the brief, not the draft's own claims.
- Report the two lowest criteria first, each with one concrete change and its IDs. Do not list more than five fixes per round.
- Delivery (voice, pauses, slides) is out of scope for this rubric except where a written draft prescribes it; see frameworks/delivery.md.
