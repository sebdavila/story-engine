# Techniques — structure (T001–T079)

- **T001 · Pair an outer plot with an inner track** — Write one external goal plus a parallel inner question (or pair an internal genre such as Morality with an external one such as Heist), then stage each inner choice as a concrete scene. Use when a plot-heavy story feels thin. (Rubin; Coyne)
- **T002 · Plot as a yes-or-no question** — Phrase the central action as a concrete yes/no question and withhold the answer until the end. Use to keep curiosity alive in any narrative. (Lupton)
- **T003 · Double storytelling** — Run an entertaining genre plot alongside an explicit ethical or social dilemma the characters must face. Use to give a series or long piece depth. (Simson)
- **T004 · State the goal early** — Say outright, near the opening, what the protagonist wants and what failure costs; repeat it if needed. The want can be tiny. Use at the top of any story or pitch. (Barker & Brown; Leitman)
- **T005 · First sentence packs hero, goal, stakes, genre** — Fit the protagonist, external goal, reason to care and genre into sentence one. Use to set clear expectations at once. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T006 · Minimal projection sketch** — Offer only a recognizable character, an obstacle and a hinted way forward; skip world-building. Use in blog intros and short copy so readers place themselves in it. (Mastropierro)
- **T007 · Cut between parallel threads** — Alternate between simultaneous storylines in different places, echoing an object, word or conflict across each cut. Use for A/B plots or long epics. (Rubin; Aristotle)
- **T008 · Push the middle to the genre's extreme** — Name the one emotion your genre promises, then take Act Two scenes to its wildest or deepest level. Use when the middle sags. (Rubin)
- **T009 · Midpoint reversal** — Put a fortune-flipping twist, false victory or false all-is-lost beat at the center. Use when the second act loses urgency. (Rubin)
- **T010 · Reveal the wound at the midpoint** — Disclose the protagonist's formative wound during the midpoint introspection, just as she finds the key ability. Use to deepen empathy at the story's center. (Emunds)
- **T011 · Close on one emotional image** — End immediately after the central question is answered with a single distilled image that seals the mood. Use on the final page, shot or slide. (Rubin)
- **T012 · Dialogue as clashing tactics** — Give each speaker a goal, then make every line an attack, plea, bribe or guilt trip, cycling tactics in static scenes; turn info-dumps into weapons; cap lines at 5, 7 or 9 words. Use to make talk carry plot and psyche. (Rubin; Storr; Haven)
- **T013 · Character motto** — Write the character's worldview as one plain rule, such as never owe anyone, and let it drive choices. Use in character design. (Rubin)
- **T014 · Make the rejected option tempting** — Give the rival or risky path real appeal: a rival firm offering easy money, or a sibling and a partner both needing the last seat out. Use when a choice feels too easy. (Rubin)
- **T015 · Dramatic versus tragic dilemma** — Decide first: two terrible options (dramatic, builds suspense) or an avoidable catastrophe from a wrong moral choice (tragic, exposes failure). Use to control what a scene does. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T016 · Crisis dilemma triggers the climax** — Push the protagonist to the negative extreme of the core value, then force a binary choice between self-interest and expressing their gift. Use at the turn into the ending. (Coyne)
- **T017 · Root mistakes in blind spots** — If a character has to fail, root the error in vanity, fear or a gap in their kind of smarts rather than plain foolishness. Use when plotting fatal mistakes. (Rubin)
- **T018 · Antagonist as hero of their own story** — Give the opponent a peace the protagonist's goal shatters, and a goal of their own. Use when opposition feels weak. (Rubin)
- **T019 · POV-shift empathy** — Reveal a hated antagonist's, rival's or acquirer's formative backstory or vulnerability until the audience understands them. Use in redemption arcs and M&A integration stories. (Terenzzo)
- **T020 · Villain with the audience's own flaw** — Give antagonists human weaknesses viewers recognize in themselves, or personify the status-quo problem that way. Use when building compelling villains. (Snow & Lazauskas)
- **T021 · Disgust markers for villains** — Add subtle signs of filth, deformity or pest-like behavior to trigger contamination aversion. Use sparingly, when strong revulsion is the goal. (Storr)
- **T022 · Making an antihero likable** — Show their punishment or capture early, make them witty and well-groomed, give a childhood tragedy, set them against a worse villain, and let them commit one costly act of justice. Use when an antihero leads. (Storr)
- **T023 · Two speakers per exchange** — Break group conversations into sequential two-person duels; a third entrant starts a new beat. Use in crowded scenes. (Rubin; Emunds)
- **T024 · Background as theme amplifier** — Let a shop sign, a radio jingle or a leaking roof quietly echo the theme. Use in scene polish. (Rubin)
- **T025 · Twist genre conventions** — List your genre's mandatory ingredients, then change how they happen or fuse two contrasting genres, such as courtroom farce. Use before drafting. (Rubin)
- **T026 · Flip the stereotype** — Invert a stock character, plot expectation or profession trait: a timid auctioneer, a dentist with a sweet tooth. Use to create novelty and suspense in visual or oral stories. (Forman; Emunds)
- **T027 · Leave the meaning for the audience** — Cut explanations, rationales and surplus adjectives; show cause and effect and stay silent on the moral so listeners infer it. Use for feeling-first pieces, short ads and skeptical audiences. (Rubin; Rodriguez; Butler; Sandler; Storr; Gottschall)
- **T028 · State the point up front** — Open with the conclusion, or a one-sentence lesson such as "a late shipment taught me X" without spoiling any twist. Use in time-pressed business settings. (Edwards; Guptara; Callahan)
- **T029 · Reveal tragic outcomes early** — If a dreadful loss is coming, say so sooner so listeners do not shut down behind emotional armor. Use for stories of severe loss or violence. (Bowles)
- **T030 · Interleave problems and solutions** — Sequence proposals as problem 1, options and objections, then problem 2, rather than all problems first. Use in pitches and strategy documents to avoid audience despair. (Edwards)
- **T031 · Draft the strategy story from the present** — Write Part 3, the present priority, first, then work back to decide how much past and pivot context it needs. Use when building a strategy story. (Guptara)
- **T032 · Draft from the turning point backward** — Write the Explosion first, build the Normal from the four-component checklist, then write the New Normal. Use when starting from raw memory scraps. (Hall)
- **T033 · Link events by cause** — Make each beat happen because of, not after, the last: connect scenes with so or but, each opening with a problem and ending thwarted. Use when outlining and cutting filler. (Aristotle; Storr; Biesenbach)
- **T034 · Reversal and recognition together** — Trigger the discovery of truth and the reversal of fortune in one event. Use at the climax of a complex plot. (Aristotle)
- **T035 · Two-sided recognition** — Write two separate reveals when one character's discovery does not identify the other. Use in reunion plots. (Aristotle)
- **T036 · Recognition just in time** — Have a character prepare to harm a relative unknowingly, then learn the truth just before acting. Use for the highest-impact tragic conflict. (Aristotle)
- **T037 · Conflict between close bonds** — Set the destructive conflict among family or close friends, not strangers. Use when choosing antagonists and stakes. (Aristotle)
- **T038 · Keep improbable premises offstage** — Place any strained launching premise outside the dramatized action. Use when a premise stretches logic. (Aristotle)
- **T039 · Flawed but decent protagonist** — Portray real faults, such as harshness or laziness, while keeping the character basically decent, with room to grow. Use for protagonists audiences will root for. (Aristotle; Gottschall)
- **T040 · Ensemble as actor** — Make choral or group passages an active participant that pushes the central plot. Use when writing group commentary. (Aristotle)
- **T041 · False-inference setup** — Show a real result B that follows from a false claim A, so the audience sees B and wrongly accepts A. Use for convincing deceptions and mysteries. (Aristotle)
- **T042 · Lead with the stumble** — Open a success story with an embarrassing flop before the win. Use for career wins and celebrity encounters so listeners root for you. (Bowles)
- **T043 · Explain why the object matters early** — Before an ordinary object is lost, show why it is irreplaceable, such as a father's handwritten recipe card. Use when external stakes look trivial. (Bowles)
- **T044 · Callback to close the loop** — Plant a small object, phrase, image or cue in the first minutes, leave it alone, then return it changed at the end, with a twist, postscript or inverted line. Use for full-circle endings and multi-story talks. (Bowles; Leitman; Cleland; Maestas; Hall; Dolan; James; Cody; Biesenbach)
- **T045 · Open an information gap** — Tease the payoff with a provocative question, contradiction or unopened fact and withhold the resolution; open two questions, close one. Never state the outcome in the opening paragraph. Use in hooks and transitions. (Mastropierro; Vaynerchuk; Kamath; Creek; Cleland; Terenzzo; Hauge; Sandler; Lazer; Emunds; Apps)
- **T046 · Delay the how** — Give who, what, where and when first, then explain mechanics once listeners are curious. Use when ordering any pitch or brief. (Cowie)
- **T047 · Juxtaposition hook** — Collide two unexpected concepts or contradictory desires in the first line or phrase, such as a quiet nightclub. Use to disrupt skepticism. (Buster; Salmon)
- **T048 · Open inside the action** — Skip greetings and thanks; start with a question, scene, crisis or specific disruption, and add context afterward. Use at the start of every talk, video or page. (Dicks; Karia; Lazer; Snow & Lazauskas; Biesenbach; Storr)
- **T049 · Five-second hook** — Show the bad guy, the struggle and why it matters to the viewer within the first 5 to 20 seconds. Use at the start of social video. (Creek)
- **T050 · Open with forward movement** — Put yourself climbing stairs, pedaling or boarding a ferry in the first two sentences. Use to create instant momentum. (Dicks)
- **T051 · Scene and chapter endings that pull forward** — End mid-step on an unresolved problem, shifted emotion, new trouble or looming obstacle, not neat closure. Use between scenes, episodes and chapters. (Barker & Brown; Cleland; Terenzzo; Haven)
- **T052 · Quiet chapter-end hooks** — End on a short unanswered implication, like "Two keys left.", instead of a cliffhanger. Use to leave curiosity lingering. (Emunds)
- **T053 · Name a concrete antagonist** — Cast a specific person, institution, landlord rule or jammed vending machine as the opponent, and yourself as the customer's ally. Use for us-versus-them bonds. (Mastropierro; Leitman)
- **T054 · Conflict types** — Anchor the plot in one of six tensions: character, society, nature, technology, supernatural or self. Use to sustain attention to resolution. (Sandler)
- **T055 · Two strong forces in conflict** — Frame the struggle as two strong forces, such as loyalty versus ambition, with an uncertain result. Use to keep listeners asking what happens next. (Karia)
- **T056 · Conflict without a villain** — Pit human ambition or innovation against technological or natural limits. Use when no villain exists. (Feigenbaum)
- **T057 · Plant before the payoff** — Introduce the helper, habit, skill or object casually in an earlier scene so the later rescue feels earned. Use whenever an escape or twist depends on something. (Barker & Brown; Cleland)
- **T058 · Foreshadowing** — Place direct hints, expert questions, early flash-forwards, red herrings or doubt about outcomes, and pay them off later. Use to build suspense between beats. (Sandler; Hauge)
- **T059 · Foreshadowing tangent before the climax** — Pause just before the payoff for a brief aside that lets listeners guess the punchline. Use in funny or suspenseful oral stories. (Simmons)
- **T060 · Fair-play twist** — Allow one major twist, with clues in plain sight inside the POV character's experience and no hidden thoughts. Use in novels with a reveal. (Barker & Brown)
- **T061 · Before–during–after experience** — Map three acts onto anticipation, the core encounter and the lingering afterglow, using parking lots and waiting rooms as Act I. Use for experiential, retail and brand storytelling. (Paulsen)
- **T062 · Kishōtenketsu** — Sequence Introduction, Development, Twist and Conclusion without relying on antagonism. Use for everyday-life, cross-cultural or reflective stories. (Paulsen)
- **T063 · Design the peak and the end** — Concentrate emotion in one high peak and a final moment, finishing on an encouraging note even in a critique. Use for long journeys and feedback. (Paulsen; Sandler)
- **T064 · Late emotional spike** — Let tension resolve and the audience relax, then deliver a sudden emotional jolt or uplift near the close. Use to lock the material into memory. (Maxwell)
- **T065 · Even beat spacing** — Space sensory and narrative beats evenly; remove long empty gaps and clumped peaks. Use when blocking spaces or multi-step experiences. (Paulsen)
- **T066 · Expectation–reality gap** — Make a character's expectation collide with objective reality. Use at turning points to avoid flat plots. (Vincent)
- **T067 · Anchor time and place early** — Limit opening context to one or two plot-setting sentences, then name when and where, with sensory specifics, in about 100 words; skip "let me share a story." Use dates only for life-changing events. (Leitman; Cleland; Dolan; Buster; Callahan; Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T068 · Segment the problem** — Split a broad problem into distinct operational parts and map each to your solution. Use when pitching complex workflows without jargon. (Kamath)
- **T069 · Suspense over surprise** — Treat surprise as a brief bang that punctuates long anticipation, not a substitute for it. Use when planning reveals. (Cleland)
- **T070 · Superior position** — Tell the audience about the danger or secret, such as a recall notice already in the mail, before the hero finds out. Use in setup or conflict to build anticipation. (Hauge)
- **T071 · Thematic opening contract** — Use the opening scene or frame to announce the theme and the kind of story, as a tour guide's welcome framing a ghost story. Use in the first 30 seconds. (Terenzzo)
- **T072 · Nested stories** — Tuck a secondary character's tale inside the main plot to deepen theme without stopping momentum. Use in layered brand or literary narratives. (Terenzzo)
- **T073 · Puzzle storytelling** — Release fragments and clues in sequence so the audience assembles the meaning, instead of a summary lead. Use in journalism, brand video and mysteries. (Terenzzo)
- **T074 · Show, don't tell** — Replace labels, adjectives and abstract trait claims with observable action, gesture, dialogue and sensory detail; narrate sensations in real time; prove a trait with a 30 to 60 second story. Use in every draft. (Butler; Sandler; Callahan; Hauge; Forman; Apps; Maguire; Biesenbach; Haven; Livesay; Guptara; Bowles)
- **T075 · Sensory anchoring** — Anchor the scene in one primary sense, or weave several into product narratives; open an invented tale with a remembered physical sensation. Use to fix memory and immediacy. (Sandler; Buster; Miller)
- **T076 · Concrete multi-sense detail** — Draft for a blind listener: pack several senses, smell included, into a few crisp sentences and the opening lines; imitate sounds aloud. Use in any oral or written story. (Forman; Karia; Apps; Parikh; Biesenbach; Simmons)
- **T077 · Zoom into one specific moment** — Swap broad summary for one physical incident, introduced with a signpost such as "It was a Tuesday in the rain when..."; add a short first-hand sensory anecdote; stretch the instant of insight. Use when a story feels vague. (Hall; Callahan)
- **T078 · Slow time with rapid small details** — Cut quickly across several small salient details just before impact to mimic a stressed brain. Use to build suspense. (Storr)
- **T079 · Audience-familiar details** — Add one or two objects, touchstones or frustrations from your listeners' world, or anchor unfamiliar settings to known landmarks. Use in the Normal phase and exposition. (Hall; Sandler)
