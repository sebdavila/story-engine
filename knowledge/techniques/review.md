# Techniques — review

- **T598 · Rule out the obvious** — Name the most predictable outcome of a setup and cross it off before writing. Use when a scene or ending feels generic. (Rubin)
- **T599 · One author controls the file** — After group ideation on sticky notes, let one person own all edits in the digital document instead of a shared live file. Use to stop "what do I want to say" bloat. (Edwards)
- **T600 · Read the draft aloud** — Read an early draft to yourself to catch jargon and clunky rhythm; read a near-final one to a colleague and note where you pause or self-correct; turn summarized conversations into spoken exchanges without repeated name-calling and read them aloud. Use in the talk and rewrite stages, and before telling a story without notes. (Edwards; Dolan; Hauge)
- **T601 · Read Aloud for Boredom** — Read descriptive passages aloud and trim where your own attention drifts. (Barker; Brown)
- **T602 · Test small before scaling** — Try new stories in cheap, low-stakes settings first: blogs and social posts before eBooks or keynotes, casual "how are you?" chats before pitches, several angles on one theme before the big video, headline and thumbnail pairs on small slices before the full email. Use before scaling the winner. (Rodriguez; Guptara; Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T603 · Outline on paper before slides** — Outline on sticky notes (one idea and a handful of words per slide), rehearse aloud, scout AV backups, preempt objections and hold back leave-behinds until after presenting; or draw a bubble map with the main idea in the center and keep pitches to three to five sections. Use to beat blank-page paralysis. (Quesenberry; Lupton)
- **T604 · Objective plan-book polish** — Cut hedges ("I think," "hopefully," "actually"), write the one-page summary last, back client critiques with consumer quotes, and keep paragraphs to 100-200 words with summary tables. Use when editing strategy documents. (Quesenberry)
- **T605 · Legal and disclosure check** — Verify client claims, secure written permissions and licenses (including reshared user content), honor privacy and children's-consent rules, and place sponsorship disclosures conspicuously. Use before any launch. (Quesenberry)
- **T606 · Narrative memo instead of slides** — Replace slides with a six-page prose memo, no bullets or images, read in silence for the first half hour of the meeting. Use for complex cross-team decisions where speakers waffle or get interrupted. (Guptara)
- **T607 · Three-source public domain check** — Treat a traditional tale as free to tell when at least three published collections carry a version. Use when vetting source stories. (Haven)
- **T608 · FAQ-driven hooks** — Draft the reader's likely questions and open sections by challenging an assumption ("you may have learned that...") with a counter-argument. Use when informative prose is dull. (Lupton)
- **T609 · Remove-or-move unity test** — Try deleting or moving each episode; if nothing shifts or breaks, cut it. Use in structural review. (Aristotle)
- **T610 · Cut scenes without a point** — Name each scene's one central point; delete any scene that lacks one. Use on rambling drafts. (Apps)
- **T611 · Hearing-only plot test** — Tell the bare plot aloud with no staging or effects to confirm events alone move the listener. Use to see whether spectacle is carrying the draft. (Aristotle)
- **T612 · Cover small flaws with charm** — Surround an unavoidable small implausibility with vivid character, emotion and polished style. Use only when the flaw cannot be cut. (Aristotle)
- **T613 · Cut jokes that do not serve** — Delete even great punchlines that stall the plot or shield you from vulnerability. Use when you default to stand-up pacing. (Bowles)
- **T614 · Per-Narrator Thread Audit** — Pull each point-of-view character's chapters into a separate file and read them straight through to confirm a full arc and no passive bystander. (Barker; Brown)
- **T615 · Write the opening last** — Keep a placeholder, finish the draft, then write the first line so it forecasts the real voice and theme; Haven writes it next to last (tone, goal, action hook, echo of the end), then picks the title from six noun, phrase or question patterns. (Barker; Brown; Haven)
- **T616 · Backstory Relocation Test** — Move all backstory out of the first fifty pages into a later chapter and see whether it can be cut or used to deepen an existing question. (Barker; Brown)
- **T617 · Find Planted Symbols** — After draft one, look for images that recurred unplanned (a scarf lost and found) and strengthen them gently without pointing at them. (Barker; Brown)
- **T618 · We-Voice Experience Script** — Write the experience in present-tense first-person plural ("we step in, we notice") with no backstage or tech terms to test the audience flow. Use when reviewing experience designs. (Paulsen)
- **T619 · Signal Fact Versus Fiction** — Mark where the documented record ends and dramatization begins through guides, captions or framing layers; invent evocative names without faking history. Use in heritage, museum and brand-lore work. (Paulsen; Terenzzo)
- **T620 · Trend-Lens Futurecasting** — Stress-test a story concept against politics, economics, society and technology before committing to a long-lived world. (Paulsen)
- **T621 · Instant Visual Legibility** — Put a sizable logo or slogan in a prominent corner and keep overlaid text near the center so mobile crops spare it; look away, glance back for under a second, and check subject and message register. Use before publishing image posts. (Vaynerchuk)
- **T622 · Protect real people** — Change names or small details (gender, major, where you met), use an obviously wrong celebrity name for a laugh, warn people beforehand and never point at them from the stage, use similar-sounding aliases and wait years before telling about students, and check whether living relatives could be embarrassed. Use whenever real people appear in what you tell or publish. (Leitman; Maestas; Dicks; Apps)
- **T623 · Keep the imperfections** — Leave giggles, a repeated word and quirky specifics in customer reviews and videos so they still sound real. Use when publishing customer stories. (Hall)
- **T624 · Unfiltered first read** — Read the whole script once for gut response and record that feeling as the reference before any technical breakdown. (Simson)
- **T625 · Read the story back to its subject** — After interviewing a real patient or customer, draft in pairs and read it aloud to the interviewee so they can flag embellishment or a victimizing tone. Use in training workshops. (Forman)
- **T626 · Handle graphic topics by allusion** — Treat sexual or bodily mishaps with humor and broad labels ("an unfortunate situation"), not explicit detail. Use when you would be uneasy telling it to grandparents. (Dicks)
- **T627 · Ration profanity** — Cut casual swearing for sharper words; keep it for verbatim quotes, a true villain, extreme fear or anger, or a rare punch line. (Dicks)
- **T628 · Plot scenes on a fortune curve** — Map each scene's rise or fall on a fortune curve and cut flat plateaus where nothing changes. Use in structural revision. (Lazer)
- **T629 · Mine AI alternatives for a stale phrase** — Ask a language model for ten alternatives to a cliché, reject nine, and use the tenth as a springboard for your own line. Use only when stuck mid-draft. (Lazer)
- **T630 · Simulated critic panel** — Load a named critic's, boss's or target reader's writing and past feedback into an AI workspace to get blunt review through their lens. Use while ideating and refining. (Lazer)
- **T631 · One-sentence core as a filter** — After a rested read, state what the piece is about in one or two sentences and cut what is unrelated; keep the same sentence in mind in Q&A as a decisive answer. Use in revision and preparation. (Apps; Maxwell)
- **T632 · Feel it while drafting** — If you do not chuckle at comedy or tear up at pathos as you write it, rewrite to deepen the feeling. (Apps)
- **T633 · Vary sentence and paragraph length for pace** — Alternate short punchy sentences and fragments (fast) with longer ones (slow), repeat key action words, and cap paragraphs at four lines except at a major turn. Use when polishing prose. (Apps; Emunds)
- **T634 · Avoid starting every sentence with I** — In first-person stories open sentences with a place, time or action so paragraphs do not repeat "I." (Apps)
- **T635 · Strong nouns and verbs over modifiers** — Strip stacked adjectives and adverbs; show movement instead of static objects ("the candle flickers"); swap flat words for sensory ones (soar for rise, seize for take); replace verb-plus-adverb pairs with one strong verb (stormed, not walked angrily). Use when cleaning flowery prose. (Apps; Emunds; Biesenbach; Haven)
- **T636 · Keep happy accidents** — Fold a slip of the tongue that earned a real laugh into later tellings. Use after live runs. (James)
- **T637 · Remove filter words** — Cut perception verbs (heard, noticed, saw) and give the stimulus directly ("gravel crackled," not "Simon heard cars") for deep point of view. (Emunds)
- **T638 · Mostly show, some tell** — Show roughly eighty percent to carry subtext, emotion and character; tell about twenty percent for clarity and transitions. (Emunds)
- **T639 · Name the feeling when behavior is ambiguous** — When a racing heart or an action could be read several ways, name the emotion once beside the sensation ("relieved, not devastated, as I signed"). (Emunds; Bowles)
- **T640 · Key word last** — Put the bombshell or punchline at the sentence end after the setup, or isolate a clue between dashes, commas or parentheses. (Emunds; Haven)
- **T641 · Short dialogue turns** — Cap each speech at three lines and stop characters from addressing each other by name. (Emunds)
- **T642 · Reskin idioms for the setting** — Swap the nouns in idioms for setting-specific ones to add mood and freshness. (Emunds)
- **T643 · Write positive and cautionary controlling ideas** — State both what makes the core value win and what makes it fail, as cause and effect, and test whether the ending proves them. Use in outlining and review. (Coyne)
- **T644 · Trace back from the final emotion** — Name the precise feeling the ending leaves (tragic loss, cathartic joy, laughter), then work backward to confirm the story earns it and serves the theme. (Schwabauer)
- **T645 · First full telling, then wait** — Tell the draft straight through once without stopping, then wait at least two hours or a day before retelling or revising. (Maguire)
- **T646 · Safe story circle rules** — Enforce confidentiality, begin with a name-story icebreaker and sign-up sheet, and allow criticism only when invited and phrased as I-statements. Use when running story circles. (Maguire)
- **T647 · Flag disputed memories** — Preface scenes others might contest with "this is how I remember it." Use when the people involved are present or might disagree. (Maguire)
- **T648 · Cut the dull stretches** — Strip routine, low-stakes passages and exit right after the climax resolves. Use for pacing in any genre. (Gottschall)
- **T649 · Outrage loop diagnostic** — Spot how serial norm-breaking keeps someone at the center of a continuous media drama. Use to diagnose how attention is hijacked. (Gottschall)
- **T650 · Pause before villainizing** — When outrage flattens someone into a villain, stop, breathe and re-imagine the story from their circumstances. Use when reviewing drafts or your own reactions. (Gottschall)
- **T651 · Ten-times quality bar** — Publish only if the piece answers the audience's question ten times better than anything online. Use before greenlighting drafts in crowded topics. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T652 · Lower the reading level** — Run a readability check and simplify syntax toward a fourth-to-eighth-grade level. Use for technical, financial or scientific topics. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T653 · Check company legends** — Fact-check appealing folklore before repeating it as official history, with a do-no-harm credibility test. Use when curating heritage stories. (Biesenbach)
- **T654 · Production budget mindset** — Treat sets, effects and speaking parts as costly: merge locations and roles and cut the two weakest scenes. (Haven)
- **T655 · Limit foreshadowing** — In stories under a thousand words drop only one or two early hints of the danger and do not repeat clues. (Haven)
- **T656 · Punctuation audit** — Turn almost every exclamation mark into a period and count commas per 400 words to flag bloated sentences. (Haven)
