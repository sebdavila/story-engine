# Techniques — data (T241–T289)

- **T241 · Chart headline above, label below** — Put the storytelling headline at the top of the chart, shrink the technical label (what, where, when) to small print underneath, and read the headline aloud word for word as the slide appears so eyes and ears get the same message. Use on every chart you show live. Alternative: T242. (Edwards)
- **T242 · Neutral chart titles** — Title each embedded chart with a plain description of what the axes show (for example "Net profit/loss" or "y versus x") and leave the argument to the slide header above it. Use when slides pair a persuasive header with a chart that should stay objective. Alternative: T241. (McKinsey)
- **T243 · Replace the chart with one big number** — After writing the headline, ask whether the headline alone or one large statistic proves the point; if so, delete the chart, especially when the breakdown by department or segment is not essential. To show a gap, draw a vertical line between two series endpoints and print the difference in large type beside it (Lo Duca). Use to prevent visual overload. (Edwards; Lo Duca)
- **T244 · Highlight the key item, grey the rest** — Pick the one bar, point or category that carries the message, render it in a single saturated color or larger scale, and push everything else to muted grey. For essential charts use simple horizontal bars with labels beside each bar and avoid pie, doughnut, bubble, heat-map and 3D charts (Edwards); add a benchmark rule and color only the items that pass it (Lo Duca); prefer bright color or bold scale over subtle rotation to make an outlier pop (Lupton). Use for non-specialist audiences. (Edwards; Lo Duca; Lupton; Feigenbaum)
- **T245 · Strip chart clutter** — Remove y-axis lines, horizontal gridlines, tick marks and legends for single series; label data directly above or below columns; keep only a zero baseline when values go positive and negative; order multi-series legends in the same direction as the bars. Choose intuitive colors (not red for profit) and attach a clear action to each visual. Use on every chart to free space for annotation. (Sandler; McKinsey)
- **T246 · Emotional words for data** — Introduce a data slide with an honest feeling word that matches the trend, such as saying the next chart is good news, or calling the numbers worrying or disappointing. Use with analytical audiences so the room feels free to react to the figures. (Edwards)
- **T247 · AI drafts, humans finish** — Ideate on your own first, then prompt a language model for a rough draft in short iterations, then edit by hand to add empathy, flaws and judgment, and check output for hallucinations and copyright. For chart subtitles run four short prompts in one chat: describe the problem, shorten, adapt for audience engagement, trim to the length limit (Lo Duca). Use to speed drafting without homogenized results. (Rodriguez; Lo Duca; Paulsen)
- **T248 · Normalize series for fair comparison** — Convert counts to percentage change from the start period when comparing entities of very different size; subtract each series' first value so all lines start at zero and divergence shows; flip metrics where lower is better (100 minus the difference) so a taller bar always means stronger. Use before charting uneven series. (Lo Duca)
- **T249 · Direct line labels** — Replace the color legend with a text label just past the last point of each line, so readers never travel between legend and line. Use on multi-line charts. (Lo Duca)
- **T250 · Rotate charts to show tiers** — Sort a column chart and turn it sideways into a horizontal pyramid of bars to expose levels hidden in the categories, such as material, moral and spiritual needs. Use when categories form a hierarchy. (Lo Duca)
- **T251 · White gridlines over bars** — After removing outer axes and background grid, lay thin semi-transparent white rules across colored bars so values stay easy to read. Use on bar charts you have decluttered (T245). (Lo Duca)
- **T252 · Consistent AI image series** — Generate one anchor image, open edit, place a new generation frame beside it, re-prompt with identical style, color and wardrobe wording changing only the subject, then crop the images apart elsewhere. Use when a visual story needs matching AI illustrations. (Lo Duca)
- **T253 · Constrained interactivity** — Keep the hero series fixed and bind dropdowns or sliders only to comparison series or time frames, so exploring never breaks the central story. Use for interactive dashboards with a message. (Lo Duca)
- **T254 · Local color per sub-chart** — Define color encodings inside each panel, not globally, so a red problem chart and a green next-step chart keep their own moods. Use in multi-panel layouts. (Lo Duca)
- **T255 · Sideways credits** — Print source and AI attributions rotated 270 degrees in small type along a side margin, building trust without interrupting top-to-bottom reading. Use on every published chart. (Lo Duca)
- **T256 · Low temperature for factual text** — Set temperature or Top P close to zero, changing only one of them per prompt, when a model writes factual commentary or answers from retrieved documents, to reduce invented details. Use for numbers-heavy copy. (Lo Duca)
- **T257 · Story-tied evidence** — Estimate a rough figure tied directly to your customer's story instead of citing exact but off-topic research, and weave interview insights, pivots and pilot results into the Problem, Business Model and Solution sections rather than parking them on a late traction slide. Use when exact data is missing or real discovery data exists. (Kamath)
- **T258 · One face after the numbers** — Pair a macro statistic with one named person's concrete change (a girl in a village putting flowers in her hair beside a new well), then link back to the scale. Buster and Parikh follow or replace the number with the person; Snow and Lazauskas center the whole appeal on one person's struggle, with the person coming first. Use against compassion fatigue when asking for donations, buy-in or action. (Buster; Snow; Parikh)
- **T259 · Story first, then the facts** — Open with a human story that sets emotional context, then follow or accompany it with specs, data or the memo; for example a short account of a patient's recovery before the trial results. Use with analytical, engineering or financial audiences, and whenever facts would otherwise slot into a defensive old story. (Forman; Simmons)
- **T260 · Follow a dire forecast with its assumptions** — Right after the extreme Act II projection, add a slide listing the critical assumptions, upside factors and downside risks, showing the forecast sits at a reasonable midpoint. Use when presenting dramatic projections. (McKinsey)
- **T261 · Chunk text into labeled blocks** — Turn vertical bullets into side-by-side columns or a 2x2 or four-box grid, give each block its own short so-what label, and add an inner label for the combined forces. Use for any slide with three or more parallel points. (McKinsey)
- **T262 · One representative quote** — Show a single real quote, or one quote in high contrast among peers, instead of five crammed together; pick one that reflects the majority interview view, not an outlier. Use for qualitative evidence. (McKinsey)
- **T263 · Match chart type to data shape** — Columns for trends up to 10 points; lines or scatter with large unfilled symbols beyond that; stacked columns for total plus one baseline part (most stable part at the bottom); clustered columns when part trends matter more than totals; pie of 2 to 5 slices clockwise from 12 o'clock with direct labels, or treemap, for static composition; horizontal bars for rankings with long labels; waterfall only for additions and subtractions between two balances; histograms with up to 10 equal bins; scatter with trendline and R-squared for correlation. Use when turning numbers into visuals. (McKinsey)
- **T264 · Annotate inflection points** — Put numbered callouts or mini tables on the turning points explaining the root cause, color the bars that prove the slide title in high contrast and render forecast bars muted with dashed fill. Use on complex charts to guide the eye. (McKinsey)
- **T265 · Clean precision tables** — Use a table only when readers need exact values or mixed text, numbers and icons; left-align text, right-align numbers with equal decimals, center icons, drop vertical lines, use ultra-thin horizontal rules, and order rows and columns by priority. Use when a graph cannot carry the precision. (McKinsey)
- **T266 · Lead with the insight** — Open with what the numbers mean for the people, places or events inside the data and move methodology to the background or a link. Use for any data story for general readers. (Feigenbaum)
- **T267 · Zoom in and out** — Alternate between single data points, which keep the complexity, and wider context, which makes the meaning clear. Use when a story is either too abstract or too anecdotal. (Feigenbaum)
- **T268 · Nonhuman or composite protagonist** — Make a place, event, object (a pair of jeans) or composite persona the central character. Use when individual data points are too abstract or too sensitive. (Feigenbaum)
- **T269 · Data diary** — Log each figure's source, transformations, cleaning steps and contributors, including support staff. Use to prove provenance and credit invisible labor. (Feigenbaum)
- **T270 · Ten tactical moves** — Choose from juxtapose, subvert, invert, materialise, compare, contrast, illuminate, provoke, parody and intrigue to close an emotional or conceptual gap between the data and the audience. Use when a chart is accurate but not felt. (Feigenbaum)
- **T271 · Naive-questioner dialogue** — Write a comic or dialogue where a naive questioner faces a resistant skeptic, so dense scientific data is explained through their exchange. Use for lay readers. (Feigenbaum)
- **T272 · Openness versus usefulness grid** — Plot candidate datasets on open-to-closed and most-to-least useful axes, and inspect variables in messy datasets before discarding them. Use when choosing which data to build on. (Feigenbaum)
- **T273 · Crowdsourcing participation budget** — Plan outreach assuming 90% of volunteers review 2 to 5 items, 9% review 20 to 40, and 1% become power users who need a direct staff forum; verify with several coders per item plus expert spot checks. Use when volunteers code or review data. (Feigenbaum)
- **T274 · Turn surveillance tools around** — Use official monitoring instruments such as satellite logs, shipping records or weather models to document official negligence or establish legal culpability. Use for accountability stories. (Feigenbaum)
- **T275 · Code new variables from text** — Read the linked summaries or news stories in a dataset and code missing variables, such as gender or incident location, into new columns before visualizing. Use when the dataset lacks the variable your story needs. (Feigenbaum)
- **T276 · Tidy spreadsheet structure** — One entity per row, one variable per column, address parts split for batch geocoding, and drop-down validation on inputs before cleaning in a tool like OpenRefine. Use before mapping or charting collected data. (Feigenbaum)
- **T277 · Sequential chart blocks** — Lay an infographic out as discrete blocks stacked down the page so each creates a pause for reflection. Use for long data pieces. (Feigenbaum)
- **T278 · Interview the dataset** — Code relationships in a network log as to, for or with, then ask who is the gatekeeper, who is vulnerable and where ties concentrate. Use for relationship data. (Feigenbaum)
- **T279 · Show nulls and zeros** — Plot measured zeros explicitly, mark absent groups as part of the story, and leave empty areas on purpose to build tension. Use when absence is the finding. (Feigenbaum)
- **T280 · Spoken versus inner caption layers** — In sequential comic panels pair spoken dialogue with separate thought or caption boxes to expose the gap between official claims and lived experience. Use for data stories about institutions and people. (Feigenbaum)
- **T281 · Translate data into the senses** — Convert numbers into body, time or city maps, soundscapes, smell maps or edible recipes so they are felt, not just read. Use for audiences that skim charts. (Feigenbaum)
- **T282 · Paid boost for proven stories** — Spend about 10% of production budget promoting stories already holding attention, through targeted social ads, optimizing for time on site and email signups, not cheap clicks alone. Use once organic analytics reveal sticky pieces. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T283 · Tag and audit stories** — Tag every story by topic, persona and format, and compare production effort with results monthly or quarterly. Use to spot weak formats (news summaries) and strong ones (quizzes, research). (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T284 · Balanced scorecard story race** — Run groups of 10 stories on a scorecard: engaged readers (15+ seconds), average attention time, finish percentage, social lift (shares divided by views, plus one), and email or lead conversions; copy the traits of the top two. Use in weekly or monthly optimization. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T285 · Scroll-depth check** — Read average scroll depth; if readers leave around the 25% mark, rewrite the misleading headline or weak lead. Use for articles with good clicks but low finish. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T286 · Short visual impact update** — Package three or four business-impact metrics with real customer feedback in a brief visual update for Slack, email or a 10-minute all-hands slot. Use to defend budgets or win leadership support. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T287 · Conclusion bar** — Put a bar at the bottom of the slide stating the takeaway or pointing to the next story section. Use on slides with several inductive points. (Parikh)
- **T288 · Colour-blind safe charts** — Never combine red and green in one chart or dashboard, so the series stay distinguishable for the roughly one in twelve men and one in two hundred women who cannot separate red from green. Use on all data visuals. (Parikh)
- **T289 · Human-scale comparisons for big numbers** — Compare vast quantities with everyday references (the area of Manhattan, the price of a morning coffee) instead of worn-out Olympic pools. Use when explaining area, volume or cost. (Biesenbach)
