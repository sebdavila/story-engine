# Techniques — selection (T217–T240)

- **T217 · Screen vulnerability before sharing** — Decide in advance how raw a story may be, agreeing limits with PR and leadership where relevant, and test whether telling it would still overwhelm you; keep the rawest material private. Use before choosing a personal story for public telling. (Rodriguez; Guptara)
- **T218 · One point per story** — Give each story one idea, keep only the few details or steps that serve it (two or three at most), and cut even loved material that does not. Use whenever a draft feels crowded or wanders. (Guptara; Dolan; Hauge; Sandler; Butler)
- **T219 · One slice of a vast subject** — Pick one unified episode from a huge history and fold the rest in as background. Use when the material is too large for one telling. (Aristotle)
- **T220 · Every detail must pay off** — Include a prop, detail or scene only if it matters later, because specificity tells the audience it matters. Use when auditing a draft for filler. (Barker; Brown; Schwabauer; Sweet)
- **T221 · One telling detail over a catalogue** — Define a character or setting by one or a few precise traits, actions or quotes, not stacked adjectives or full description. Variants: test whether a stranger could pick the character out of a crowd (Barker; Brown); one action or trait-based nickname instead of a bland name (Leitman); avoid celebrity look-alikes (Dicks); add a hobby or habit (Karia); one or two details to keep pace (Apps); one glint for setting (Rubin); fewer adjectives (Guptara); a defining detail or quote for secondary characters (Bowles). Use when introducing anyone or anywhere.
- **T222 · Remove idle characters** — Give everyone present a role in the conflict or take them out of the scene, even people who really were there. Use when a scene has bystanders. (Barker; Brown; Leitman)
- **T223 · Anniversary exclusives** — Release rare archive material on a meaningful date so sharers gain social currency. Use when you hold old material and a date to hang it on. (Vaynerchuk)
- **T224 · Reshape facts, keep the emotional truth** — Merge people, compress time, approximate forgotten words or invent connective detail where memory blurs, but only while the core events and emotional truth stay real. Use when memory leaves gaps or the true timeline drags. (Leitman; Maestas; Maguire)
- **T225 · Fixed core, tailored versions** — Hold the central story constant and vary length, emphasis, technical level or added reasons for each audience or situation. Use when one story must serve several audiences. (Hall; Dolan; Forman; Cowie)
- **T226 · Switch protagonist to fix the action** — If a story prompts the wrong action, retell the same event through a different character. Use when listeners respond but do the wrong thing. (Hall)
- **T227 · Pick the story before the speaker** — Decide which stories the audience needs, then recruit the people who lived them. Use when building a story program. (Hall)
- **T228 · Tell your part of someone else's story** — Frame another person's event around your own feelings and change so you stay the protagonist. Use when the dramatic event happened to someone else. (Dicks)
- **T229 · Compare several openings** — List several candidate openings and test each for tone, setting, humor and echo with the ending. Use before committing to a first line. (Dicks)
- **T230 · Cut stories to fit the time** — Write the full version, then trim to the slot while keeping the arc: about 30 seconds for a pitch (Salmon), one to two minutes spoken at work (Dolan), four to seven minutes on stage (Dicks; Buster); watch the room. Use whenever time is fixed. (Dicks; Dolan; Buster; Hall; Butler; Salmon)
- **T231 · Park detail in an appendix or handout** — Move exhaustive tables, rejected options and deep detail out of the main story into an appendix or leave-behind. Use for decision stories with heavy supporting data. (McKinsey; Edwards)
- **T232 · Tell stories you lived** — Choose your own experiences so reliving the feelings makes delivery natural. Use when choosing between a borrowed and a personal story. (Karia)
- **T233 · Make the ordinary extraordinary** — Center a story on a mundane object to reveal community values. Use when material seems too plain to tell. (Apps)
- **T234 · Enter late, leave early** — Begin as close to the inciting moment as possible and stop right after the resolution. Use when openings are slow or endings linger. (James)
- **T235 · Scan for workplace story gaps** — Look beyond routine narratives for weak spots in communication and prepare concise stories before meetings. Use when planning where stories would help at work. (Maguire)
- **T236 · Humour temperature check** — Make a small joke before a pitch and see whether the room laughs to gauge how much humour fits. Use before choosing the tone of a pitch. (Cody)
- **T237 · Test a prospect's respect** — Make one deadpan, slightly cheeky remark about the competition and watch the reaction to screen clients. Use to qualify prospects early. (Cody)
- **T238 · Ask "because?" to find the core need** — Keep asking why behind a stated want until you reach the human need, then hook the story to it. Use when you do not yet know what the listener really cares about. (Simmons)
- **T239 · Story or bullet points** — Use story to move entrenched feelings; use plain data when listeners already agree or want instructions. Use when deciding the form of a message. (Gottschall)
- **T240 · Write for one tribe** — Aim at the shared inside experiences of a defined subculture. Use when the audience is a specific community. (Snow; Lazauskas)
