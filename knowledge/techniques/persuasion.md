# Techniques — persuasion

- **T457 · Enrolling questions** — Ask short tag questions that can only be answered yes (Nobody here enjoys chasing late invoices, right?). Open a talk with one to establish shared experience, and use another at each agenda transition to keep the room nodding. (Edwards)
- **T458 · Frame insights as reminders** — Present a new fact or lesson as something the audience already knew or would have concluded from the same raw data, not as proof of your cleverness. Use with senior executives and clients so you never outshine them. (Edwards)
- **T459 · Pre-empt the objection** — Say the audience's likely doubt or the flaw in your own proposal before anyone else does, then answer it with a workaround or a short true story of someone who had the same fear and succeeded. Use in pitches, methodology slides and sales openers where polite people would stay silent and say no later. Variants: Hauge has the hero voice the reader's disbelief and then shows independent verification; Callahan replaces a rumor or past flop with a true account of what changed. (Edwards; Dolan; Callahan; Hauge)
- **T460 · Indirect pre-empt** — Praise something specific the listener did well, then tell a related story (say, a mentor who once took a chance on an outsider) that defuses an unspoken bias without naming it. Use when a direct denial or a token story would backfire. (Guptara)
- **T461 · Acknowledge the barrier before the ask** — Give the main implementation barrier its own framing slide, then order the recommended actions from lowest to highest barrier. Use in the final act when actions need outside approval or painful change. (McKinsey)
- **T462 · Disclose flaws to earn trust** — Admit real setbacks, risks, lawsuits or failed values next to the wins, ideally before the audience finds them, then show the turnaround. Mastropierro goes from flaw and measured result straight into the offer; Maestas waits until you are emotionally ready, frames the pain as service to others and, for organizations, opens with the bad news; Kamath lists top reasons not to invest with mitigations; Terenzzo adds one honest failure to a context-action-result founder arc; Dolan tells of a time you broke your own value. Use in About pages, investor decks, origin stories and value talks. (Mastropierro; Maestas; Kamath; Terenzzo; Sandler; Dolan)
- **T463 · Admit imperfection in a commitment** — Pair a claim of dedication with an honest limit (I answer every client email the same day, well, most weekdays). Use to replace flat corporate claims with human truth. (Simmons)
- **T464 · Anchor in one person's day** — Translate a strategy, process change or campaign into one named person's ordinary day (a customer, user or employee with a defined job). Use at the Real stage of SUPERB in pitches, emails, feedback and change programs. (Edwards)
- **T465 · Representative Sufferer** — Describe a real or typical person living through the same hardship as your reader before you present the offer, so the reader sides with both the person and your solution. (Mastropierro)
- **T466 · Gain and safety language together** — In the closing, mix forward-looking gain words with reassurance words (safe, reliable, guaranteed, don't worry). Use to win both thrill seekers and threat avoiders. (Edwards)
- **T467 · Three-sentence entry summary** — Start an award entry with three sentences: the hardest challenge, the best thing you did, the best result. Use so time-poor judges can place you as a contender at once. (Edwards)
- **T468 · Leave room for audience co-creation** — Hand part of the idea over: offer an adaptable element tentatively and ask for their view to create mild scarcity and invite advice, or let stakeholders write the organization's story or values in sessions rather than receive a finished one. Use in pitches and change programs so people feel ownership. (Edwards; Guptara)
- **T469 · Sell the benefit, not the feature** — After every feature or mission line add "what this means to you is" plus a concrete experience; rewrite we-copy as you-copy (with us, your team gets installs within the week); describe how the product changes feelings rather than listing specs. Use in proposals, decks, sites and promo videos. (Livesay; Creek)
- **T470 · Turn case studies into movies** — Ask what happened next and what would have happened had it failed, to surface the mid-project crisis and the rescue. Use when presenting past work so prospects see themselves in the journey. (Livesay)
- **T471 · Invite the buyer to imagine the future** — Paint the buyer's life some weeks after the purchase and ask whether that future appeals; or begin a creative idea with "what if..." to pull them out of analytical critique. Use to move a prospect from interesting to intriguing and into the close. (Livesay)
- **T472 · Lateral third option** — When rivals own the obvious play or a question is binary, offer an indirect alternative (sponsoring the runners' recovery breakfast while rivals fight over race-day banners; calling a cloud service the plumbing, not the water). Use for competitive positioning and explaining complex ideas. (Livesay)
- **T473 · Quote the prospect's mission** — Lift an exact line from the prospect's site about their values and show how it describes your own culture. Use in B2B pitches to prove fit and escape price-only comparison. (Livesay)
- **T474 · Yes-and pivot** — Answer pushback by acknowledging the point and adding a perspective ("yes, and...") instead of no. Use in live sales calls and objection handling. (Livesay)
- **T475 · Silence after asking** — After the closing question, silently repeat a calming phrase three times to hold three to five seconds of quiet. Use so you do not rush in with unrequested discounts. (Livesay)
- **T476 · Everyday object as a metaphor for the idea** — Hold up a familiar object as a physical model of your future product or core strength and stop talking so listeners ask and flesh out the case themselves; or compare your core strength to a familiar object before showing financials. Use when formal projections would invite hostile grilling or when opening data-heavy decks. (Guptara; Forman)
- **T477 · Turn greetings into conversation hooks** — Answer "how are you?" with a playful line or tiny story, and introduce yourself with three facets (work, a passion, a mission) so the listener has three paths to follow. Use when networking. (Guptara)
- **T478 · Name-drop through a lesson** — Mention a prestigious client in passing while the anecdote is about what you learned, keeping the client as hero. Use with prospects to signal competence without boasting. (Guptara)
- **T479 · One-minute pivot story in meetings** — Cut a longer story to setup, turning point and outcome in under a minute to model a change of direction indirectly. Use when openly rejecting a colleague's proposal would make the room defensive. (Guptara)
- **T480 · Invite the Audience In** — In the first two sentences, replace a brag with a relatable question about a shared struggle (Ever booked a nonrefundable ticket for the wrong day?); pair a stark image with "would you?" or "you know how..."; start with an uncontroversial shared observation before revealing your lack. Use in openings and cause appeals, especially when listeners lack firsthand experience. (Leitman; Kamath)
- **T481 · Turn Toward Hope** — After the trials, show the uplifting turn where the character wins. Use after problem agitation so readers feel moved to act instead of defeated. (Mastropierro)
- **T482 · Tie the Story to the Argument** — Choose the background detail that directly supports the commercial point (a bakery founder's years on the night shift in a story about a scheduling app). Use whenever embedding a story in persuasive copy. (Mastropierro)
- **T483 · One-to-One Engagement** — Reply warmly to a stranger's post about a shared non-product interest, answer without pitching, and mail a small gift or handwritten note; tag commenters by name in replies so notifications pull them back. Use to turn strangers into advocates. (Vaynerchuk)
- **T484 · Box In the Opponent** — Recast a rival's inconsistent moves inside your own coherent frame. Use in competitive positioning and campaigns. (Vincent)
- **T485 · Turn a Blunder Into a Story** — After a public failure answer with self-aware humor aimed at ordinary customers, not a dry apology; stay in an open Q&A until the room is empty; pair a refund with an in-person apology and small gift; or combine operational fixes, a relatable spokesperson, shared proof and symbolic tokens. Use in PR crises and client recovery. (Creek; Maestas; Vincent)
- **T486 · Lower your status** — Drop elite markers (a big firm, not its famous name), joke about enviable traits at the mic, understate heroic results so listeners infer the scale, and keep the humor kind (no sexist, racist or political jokes). Use at the top of stories where privilege or looks hide the underdog. (Leitman; Butler; Dolan)
- **T487 · Let others vouch for the hero** — Let a skeptical character voice the audience's disbelief and answer with a self-mocking line, pass an extraordinary premise through credible witnesses before the core story, or keep the hero silent while several witnesses state the message. Use for unlikely truths and advocacy spots. (Leitman; Terenzzo; Salmon)
- **T488 · Problem Urgency First** — Prove the market problem is getting worse now before introducing the solution, instead of insisting the solution is urgent. Use so the pitch never feels desperate. (Kamath)
- **T489 · Credit the Other Side** — Grant the merits and good intent of the mainstream view and present your case as covering its blind spot. Use for contrarian positions. (Kamath)
- **T490 · Past-Present-Future Continuity** — Link "I was," "I am," "I will be" through repeated phrasing or a recurring theme. Use to show founder-market fit or rally a movement. (Kamath)
- **T491 · One-Story Thank-You** — Thank an existing supporter with one short story or video showing their specific gift at work. Use for donor retention and growth. (Maestas)
- **T492 · Elevator Pitch Formula** — Script a three-to-five-minute pitch by filling a positioning template (for whom, who, the, how, unlike, our product, reason why) and write what listeners must know, feel and do. Use for investor and executive hallway pitches. (Terenzzo)
- **T493 · Gradual Bad-News Release** — In a crisis, show survival and adaptation steps first and introduce harsh impacts bit by bit inside the story. Use in restructurings to lower anxiety. (Terenzzo)
- **T494 · Awe and wonder** — Tie childhood wonder to an ambitious project, or pair vast imagery, moving water or uplifting music with the prose, so the audience feels invited into something larger than commerce. Use for the spirituality urge in broad audiences. (Buster; Butler)
- **T495 · Early credibility signals** — Show early that recognizable peers, awards, certifications, years of experience or a trusted past success back you; place them at the start of first-touch content, not on the About page. Use on landing pages and with sceptical prospects. (Butler; Cowie)
- **T496 · Wicked problem, then rescue** — Establish the gravity of a tangled problem (interlinked symptoms, no template, one-shot interventions), then present the design intervention as the rescue. Use for systemic change pitches. (Sandler)
- **T497 · Curiosity headline that pays off** — Use curiosity-gap headlines only on content that truly delivers the promise. Use on posts and ads to earn clicks without burning trust. (Butler)
- **T498 · Story to argument ratio** — Aim for about one part story to three parts reasoning, pairing each major opinion with an illustrative anecdote. Use in everyday workplace talks. (Callahan)
- **T499 · Story triggering by remarkable action** — Do something visible, unannounced and principled (a CEO quietly working a shift at the returns desk during the holiday rush) so witnesses retell it. Use to shift culture or build trust through firsthand experience. (Callahan)
- **T500 · Repeated small exposures** — Share different small stories about an unfamiliar idea across many casual encounters rather than one big attempt on a skeptical executive. Use to build support for a new initiative. (Callahan)
- **T501 · Cite the source's authority** — Name the researcher and institution when telling a study, so you can say where it came from. Use for any science story. (Callahan)
- **T502 · Call to action** — End with a command verb, first-person or outcome framing (book your first lesson) and the value of acting, without clutter. Use to turn emotion into action. (Sandler)
- **T503 · Low-pressure next step** — Close an introductory story with an easy, optional step (a link to a two-minute video) instead of a hard ask. Use so audiences engage on their terms. (Cowie)
- **T504 · Tie each update to its strategy chapter** — In every letter or earnings call, say which chapter of the strategic story a decision, acquisition or metric belongs to and frame setbacks as temporary hurdles in that chapter. Use during multi-year plans. (Forman)
- **T505 · A story instead of a lecture** — Where advice would trigger resistance, tell a parable or a past client's mistake with a clear moral; in family conflict, calmly share a moment from your own life instead of a lecture. Use with clients, subordinates, peers and relatives. (Forman; Maguire)
- **T506 · Feedback through an attributed proverb** — Deliver correction as a saying you heard from a named kind of person at a specific time and place (a ferry captain's mother on patching small leaks early). Use to correct a peer or manager indirectly. (Simmons)
- **T507 · Agenda verbs set the decision mode** — Start each agenda bullet with a verb: approve, adopt or authorize for decisions; explore or brainstorm for input; review or evaluate for listening. Use when drafting agendas and section dividers. (McKinsey)
- **T508 · Open on accepted facts** — Start the situation with data the audience already accepts, building mild tension without early objections. Use on the first content slide of Act I. (McKinsey)
- **T509 · Build to the conclusion last** — Lay out each driver or premise first and reveal a shocking total or recommendation only after the chain is accepted. Use when the conclusion is extreme or contradicts the audience's stated preference. (McKinsey; Parikh)
- **T510 · Show conservative actions fall short first** — Normally order recommendations by impact, but with a risk-averse audience present safe in-control actions first, prove them insufficient, then propose radical ones. Use when sequencing recommendations. (McKinsey)
- **T511 · Show the cost of the opposite behavior** — Dramatize the damage done by the bad behavior instead of urging the good one. Use for moral topics with resistant listeners. (James)
- **T512 · What is it, what is it worth, how do I get it** — Order three points or stories around clarity, value and action (head, heart, hands). Use in persuasive teaching talks. (James)
- **T513 · Bridge from a personal story to a work change** — Tell a brief true story of people rallying around a hardship (coworkers covering a colleague's shifts after a house fire) before introducing a workplace disruption. Use in staff meetings to turn friction into unity. (Maxwell)
- **T514 · Frame change as heritage** — Present an innovation as the continuation of the group's proudest tradition, not the death of its craft. Use with veteran staff. (Maxwell)
- **T515 · Drop formal status to bond** — Step out of formal authority (a plant manager loosening the tie and eating lunch on the loading dock with the crew) to signal that everyone shares the same roots. Use after shared trials. (Maxwell)
- **T516 · Rhyming catchphrase anchor** — Link an opponent's obvious weakness to a short rhyme and repeat it through every item of evidence. Use in trials, debates and campaigns when technical data overwhelms. (Maxwell)
- **T517 · Deliver drawbacks warmly** — Read mandatory warnings slowly and warmly, using familiar bodily or life experiences. Use in ads and sales to lower resistance. (Maxwell)
- **T518 · Early injustice to a decent underdog** — Early on, let a selfish, higher-status figure wrong a humble, generous character so the audience craves justice and commits. (Storr)
- **T519 · Anchor the new in a familiar place or problem** — Frame a novel offering inside a known place, sector or everyday problem (clinics drowning in paper forms). Use when audiences lack context. (Cowie)
- **T520 · Motivation grounded in facts** — Tie your why to real problems and evidence; when confidentiality blocks detail, state what you can and the continuity arrangements. Use for the why block and sensitive updates. (Cowie)
- **T521 · Adopt the critic's label** — Repeat a skeptic's dismissive phrase openly while explaining each step until it becomes a shared joke. Use when someone powerful subtly discredits you in a group. (Simmons)
- **T522 · Story that installs a pause** — Tell of someone who held back an angry rebuke so listeners learn to check whether the moment is right before reacting. (Simmons)
- **T523 · Stories before the vote** — Let opposing stakeholders tell the history behind their misgivings before the vote or plan. Use in polarized committees so hidden feelings do not sabotage later. (Simmons)
- **T524 · Shared silly movement** — Ask a resistant audience for a tiny synchronized silly movement before a tough request. Use before fundraising or cooperation asks. (Simmons)
- **T525 · Passing the torch** — Ask a disillusioned person to tell of a leader they admired, then picture that leader handing them the role. Use when coaching someone paralyzed by hopelessness. (Simmons)
- **T526 · Present-tense urgency** — Frame the threat as unfolding now, with a concrete deadline given in small doses (the next six weeks decide this). Use when a complacent audience must act. (Gottschall; Parikh)
- **T527 · Outcome-based vision statement** — Privately write one or two sentences on the state the audience should be in afterward (ready to sign the pilot), not what you will do (present results). Use at the start of planning. (Parikh)
- **T528 · Order pros and cons against your verdict** — When advocating yes, put a small dose of cons first and the stronger pros last; when arguing no, put the pros first and the outweighing cons last. Use to look balanced. (Parikh)
- **T529 · Agreement checks after each premise** — Pause after each step of the argument to ask "do you agree?" Use in talks, meetings and decks to avoid backtracking later. (Parikh)
- **T530 · Hidden backup slides** — Keep granular financial or technical proof hidden at the end and show it only if a premise is challenged. Use when you expect specific executive pushback. (Parikh)
- **T531 · Analogy from life outside work** — Map a lesson from sports, music, parenting or a hobby onto a work topic (for example, comparing onboarding to teaching a child to ride a bike). Use when a dry subject lacks a hook. (Biesenbach)
