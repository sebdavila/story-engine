# Techniques — delivery (T374–T456)

- **T374 · Edit Rhythm to Sound** — Pick the mood-matched music first and cut shots on its beat; play quiet underscore under speech, then drop to silence just before the core lesson; in 6-15 second videos use several quick cuts rather than one static shot. Use in video editing. (Creek; Vaynerchuk)
- **T375 · Staging That Signals Context** — Choose settings and light that signal context (a night-shift nurse filmed in a dim hospital corridor); keep footage deliberately raw when slick polish would signal a hidden pitch; place niche memorabilia in the background to show shared identity. Use when staging any shoot. (Creek)
- **T376 · Trailer Logic** — Post a native teaser that sends viewers to the long version elsewhere, instead of cutting one video into eight parts; in 90-second videos and 15-30 second ads show only dramatic highlights, stakes and music. Use for long-form social video and short ads. (Creek; Maestas)
- **T377 · Ready Pocket Story** — Keep one or two polished, tested anecdotes ready in case slides or equipment fail; prepare a fun fact in the story zone, neither oversharing nor bland (say, a summer spent driving a tow truck). Use in talks, networking and interviews. (Leitman)
- **T378 · Sixty-second version** — Prepare a tight version of your core story that runs under a minute and opens straight on action. Use for short TV or radio interviews. (Apps)
- **T379 · Self-Aware Ownership** — When telling of exes, jerks or bad bosses, put the blame on your own gullibility, and add short self-aware lines admitting you knew it was a bad idea and did it anyway. Use to stay relatable in grievance or high-passion stories. (Leitman)
- **T380 · Start With "So"** — After a deep breath, open the story with "So..." to sound mid-conversation with a friend rather than giving a speech. Use as the first word of a live story. (Leitman)
- **T381 · Name the Elephant** — Mention an obvious physical trait or distraction (an arm sling, a bad sunburn) in your first two sentences, then pivot to the story. Use at the top of live performance. (Leitman)
- **T382 · One message per slide** — Give each slide one takeaway, strip multi-box diagrams, legends and split comparisons; aim for 1 idea, 5-7 elements, under a minute, with takeaway headings, cited sources and a closing call to action. Use when building pitch decks. (Kamath; Sandler)
- **T383 · Address one specific person** — Write or speak to one real person: state the promise your piece makes to them and cut what wastes their time, or talk as to one close friend on radio and podcasts. Use in posts, speeches, editing and audio. (Lazer; Apps; Terenzzo)
- **T384 · Conversational delivery** — Picture one friend instead of a crowd; swap acting for interaction: pause to breathe, own stumbles, ask short questions, share personal stakes. Match persona to audience, either a roaming round-table style or formal academic diction. Use in live delivery. (Buster; Salmon; Sandler)
- **T385 · Indirect Sensory Suggestion** — Describe sensory actions (posture, breath, a blank page) instead of issuing commands, to drop listeners' defenses. Use in openings. (Terenzzo)
- **T386 · Lay-Audience Speech Rules** — Favreau: story over fancy phrasing, no jargon, the audience's own words, everyday citizens' lives, pre-empt opposing arguments. Bailey: spotlight the scientist or affected person, master the hypotheses, rely on metaphor, stress urgent dilemmas, trim technical detail. Use for leadership speeches and translating research. (Terenzzo)
- **T387 · Story-first opener** — Right after greeting, tell a short, relatable personal story, before credentials, logistics or agenda. Use in the first minutes when listeners are deciding whether to tune in. (Dolan; Hall)
- **T388 · Stop at the peak, no moral** — End when the action ends, at most restating the point briefly, never spell out what the story really means; cut right after the emotional peak. Use at the close of every story so listeners draw the meaning. (Buster; Callahan)
- **T389 · Bridge and inclusive link** — After the climax of a non-work story, add one transition sentence on why you shared it, then state the business point in one or two sentences with "we" or "I invite you", not "you need to". Use to return to the workplace without lecturing. (Dolan)
- **T390 · Third-person founder story** — As an employee or rep, open with the moment you first heard how the company began, tell the founder's journey in third person, and close with one sentence on why it mattered to you. Use when telling a Founder Story you did not live. (Hall)
- **T391 · Place stories where attention waits** — Publish customer and brand stories in idle spaces: receipts, waiting-room screens, order confirmation emails, phone hold messages. Use to reach undivided attention outside ads. (Hall)
- **T392 · Slides that cue a story** — Put a logo or vague neutral image where a story belongs so listeners build their own pictures (Hall), or build slides from a dozen photos of people doing things, never static buildings (Callahan). Use to replace bullets and prompt your own recall. (Hall; Callahan)
- **T393 · One-picture product pitch** — Tell the case for a new product to executives with a single slide showing the product. Use when pitching innovation upward. (Forman)
- **T394 · Slides as harmony** — Treat slides as harmony rather than reading them aloud, add a duplicate final slide so your desktop never shows, and hand out materials only afterward. Use whenever presenting with slides. (James)
- **T395 · Drop the old employer** — Replace openers that name your previous employer with a phrase that frames the story as a shared past experience, and collect local stories at the new workplace. Use after changing jobs. (Callahan)
- **T396 · Answer questions through stakes** — Turn a technical or bureaucratic question into lived experience, such as a fisher's daughter answering a water-permit question by recalling her father's empty nets, instead of boilerplate. Use in Q&A. (Buster)
- **T397 · Music and backdrop cues** — Shift music from minor to major with rising tempo at the turning point, and stage the speaker against an iconic backdrop. Use in video ads and televised speeches. (Salmon)
- **T398 · Break the fourth wall** — Pause briefly to speak to a doubtful listener or pass the floor to a teammate, then clearly steer back into the narrative. Use when attention drifts. (Sandler)
- **T399 · Keep the audience inside the past** — Avoid rhetorical questions, "You guys!", reacting to cheers, waving props, anachronisms and the word "story". Use throughout delivery so listeners stay transported; contrast T335. (Dicks)
- **T400 · Plain clothing onstage** — Wear simple clothes with no graphics or loud accessories. Use onstage so the audience watches the mental movie, not the outfit. (Dicks)
- **T401 · Move executive summary to the end** — Put the multi-bullet summary slide last, left up during Q&A, or in the appendix; start the live deck with a SMART title and agenda. Use when delivering live rather than sending a document. (McKinsey)
- **T402 · Plain agenda header** — Title the agenda slide with one word, "Agenda" live or "Contents" in reports, so the audience skips the header and reads the roadmap. Use on slide 1 after the title; contrast T403. (McKinsey)
- **T403 · Agenda titled by value** — Replace "Agenda" or "Overview" with a thematic title whose bullets match your argument premises one to one. Use on the opening structure slide; contrast T402. (Parikh)
- **T404 · Slide density by audience size** — Scale density inversely to audience: data-dense for one-on-ones with technical decision-makers, moderate text and charts for 5-15 people, full-bleed photos for keynotes. Use when choosing slide layout. (McKinsey)
- **T405 · Three minutes per slide** — Cap each slide at three minutes of content: 25 minutes and up to 8 slides for a 30-minute meeting, 50 minutes and up to 16 for 60. Use to pace decks and leave buffer. (McKinsey)
- **T406 · Minimal slide transitions** — Avoid animation except to reveal a causal flow, add a forecast to history, or show a closing question; if transitions are used, a 0.5-second wipe from the right or a smooth fade, silent. Use across business decks. (McKinsey)
- **T407 · Facilitate rather than present** — Deliver sitting or standing without a memorized script, ask open questions early to get the decision-maker talking, read body language for hidden objections, credit analysts by name, and redirect tough domain questions to experts. Use in live business meetings. (McKinsey)
- **T408 · Calibrate authority to rank** — Juniors: dress formally, pause instead of filler, slow down, project, use larger gestures, move toward questioners, take notes on paper. Seniors: avoid monologues, interruptions and tones that shut down dissent. Use to set a peer-to-peer dynamic. (McKinsey)
- **T409 · Break the visual pattern of the feed** — Open with stark contrast to the surrounding feed, such as a silent hand-drawn opening in a feed of loud selfie clips. Use in social video and ad breaks. (Lazer)
- **T410 · Skip describing yourself onstage** — In a first-person live story, spend the descriptive budget on the other characters, not your own appearance. Use in live presentations. (Karia)
- **T411 · Fixed gaze point to calm nerves** — Rest your gaze on one spot on the rear wall just over the heads of the audience; you look engaged while stage fright settles. Use when starting a live talk. (Apps)
- **T412 · Escalating show-of-hands opener** — Ask three escalating questions: a narrow one (who has run a marathon?), a wider one (who has watched one?), then a funny one (who has driven around one in a traffic jam?). Use to engage everyone early. (Apps)
- **T413 · Early interaction** — Run the first checkpoint, poll or open question within 2-3 minutes (does this order work for everyone?). Use in interactive sessions before listeners go passive at about ten minutes. (Parikh)
- **T414 · State length and participation up front** — Say how long you will talk (the next fifteen minutes, say) and cue verbally or by staging whether people should join in or listen. Use at the start of any talk. (James)
- **T415 · Prepare the room** — Tape "reserved for latecomers" on back chairs and invite early arrivals forward; face the brightest light, close blinds, remove spare chairs, empty pockets. Use before listeners arrive. (James)
- **T416 · Scale delivery to distance** — Measure the distance to the average listener: conversational in small rooms and on video, larger gestures in mid halls, radio-lively voice in ballrooms, rehearsed camera cues for screens. Use for any venue. (James)
- **T417 · Own the camera frame** — Raise the webcam to forehead or nose level, light from 45 degrees or facing a window, leave little space above your head, show head to upper arms, sit upright with elbows wide, lift gestures into frame, stick an arrow by the lens. Use in virtual calls. (Parikh; James)
- **T418 · Budget time for applause** — Subtract 45-60 seconds from a 5-minute slot, use walk-on music to end opening clapping, mouth "thank you" to the introducer, and hold city compliments to avoid junk applause. Use in tight slots. (James)
- **T419 · Reset attention with short stories** — Split a 45-minute slot into a 5-minute intro plus self-contained 5-minute stories, each resetting attention. Use when hosts refuse a shorter slot. (James)
- **T420 · Recover a forgotten detail smoothly** — Slip in the missed detail with a light line flagging a piece you held back until now Use live to stay fluent; contrast T421. (James)
- **T421 · Owning a blank calmly** — If you blank, pause, admit the gap briefly or bridge back with a detail you saved, or say plainly that you are nervous or crack a small joke about the room Use to release the strain of pretending control; contrast T420. (Maguire; Simmons)
- **T422 · Say the name last in an introduction** — Introduce a speaker in four or five sentences (greeting, credentials, home town, one surprising fact) and save the name for the last words to cue applause. Use when emceeing or coaching an introducer. (James)
- **T423 · Open with why you care** — Start with a 1-2 minute personal story on why you care about the topic, not a prepackaged joke. Use in the first minute to avoid freezing and build likability. (Maxwell)
- **T424 · Open with a local landmark visit** — Have hosts take you to a community landmark the afternoon before, then open by describing the visit and how it felt. Use with unfamiliar regional audiences. (Maxwell)
- **T425 · Bold physical act embodying the theme** — Do a daring onstage act that embodies your theme, such as tearing a thick policy binder in half while asking how many rules the team really needs, watching laughter so you stop before it turns nervous. Use to jolt a dull conference. (Maxwell)
- **T426 · Aim intensity at a few carriers** — Aim your most intense statements at one or two responsive people so their visible reaction carries the message to decision-makers. Use in group pitches. (Maxwell)
- **T427 · Vivid person-action-object memory images** — Encode dry sequences as bizarre, emotional visual sentences of a person, action and object, placed along a familiar walking route. Use to memorize lists or make visual arguments. (Maxwell)
- **T428 · Ear-catching language** — Sharpen spoken rhythm with crisp dialogue, vivid metaphor, purposeful facts and allusions, repetition and rhythmic catalogues. Use in oral drafting. (Maguire)
- **T429 · Family telling rituals** — Turn off phones, set predictable rituals such as "storying" the day and taking turns, and draw prompts from a suggestion jar. Use in families and personal practice. (Maguire)
- **T430 · Self-deprecating opener** — Mock your own qualifications for a hostile room, or make a brief dry relatable joke about yourself early, such as your hopeless sense of direction on the way to the venue. Use in the introduction to relax people and build likeability. (Cody; Parikh)
- **T431 · Humour inside mandatory notices** — Wrap safety or policy details in an unexpected joke or lyric. Use when audiences tune out routine announcements. (Cody)
- **T432 · Setup with attitude, surprise punch line** — State subject and emotional stance in the setup, then close on a brief, vivid twist that reverses the attitude. Use to grab attention in about eight seconds. (Cody)
- **T433 · Calling out a flop or mishap** — Name an unfunny silence in real time with a line about the quiet room; answer a stumble with a deadpan one-liner (say, that gravity remains undefeated). Use when a joke flops or an accident happens. (Cody)
- **T434 · Nerves as energy** — Reframe butterflies as caring about the outcome and channel that tension into full vocal commitment on your first line. Use just ahead of high-stakes pitches. (Cody)
- **T435 · Take the first idea** — Commit to the first idea spoken in a brainstorm without debating it. Use to break deadlocks and build momentum. (Cody)
- **T436 · Positivity rule** — Anchor scenes and meetings in abundance and shared goals, not scarcity or complaint. Use to keep brainstorms and client sessions constructive. (Cody)
- **T437 · Statements, not questions** — Make assertions that contribute facts instead of asking partners where to go or why they acted. Use in improv and brainstorming so the action keeps moving. (Cody)
- **T438 · State a constraint up front with a code word** — Tell clients of an unavoidable physical or personal constraint at the start of a pitch, with a light code word for stepping out. Use to put clients at ease. (Cody)
- **T439 · Curiosity teaser** — Drop an intriguing, unexplained remark (say, that you were once banned from a bowling alley) and wait for listeners to ask. Use to win permission to tell a story and break a tense mood. (Simmons)
- **T440 · Draw the meaning around the reaction** — Tell the story first, watch how the audience reacts, then draw the circle of meaning around their response. Use with live groups when a preset moral feels forced. (Simmons)
- **T441 · Mime from a pictured scene** — Picture the scene vividly, then use subtle hand movements to outline it; hold imaginary objects, and gesture to opposite sides when comparing two options. Use in oral delivery. (Simmons; Biesenbach)
- **T442 · Face in place of words** — Replace three or four sentences of verbal reaction with one genuine expression, such as a slow blink or a wince. Use to shorten a story and cross cultural barriers. (Simmons)
- **T443 · Timeline laid out in space** — Step right, center, left for beginning, middle and end, and step back to the first spot to return to an earlier phase; orient moves so the audience sees past-to-future left to right. Use in multi-era stories. (Simmons; Biesenbach)
- **T444 · Restart the conversation** — When you notice you began mid-frustration, apologize for the tone, ask to begin again and retell calmly from the beginning. Use when a listener turns defensive in a complaint or negotiation. (Simmons)
- **T445 · Paradoxical agreement opener** — Tell a resentful, involuntary group that you fully expect the session to be as tedious as they fear. Use to meet them where they are and disarm defenses with laughter. (Simmons)
- **T446 · Rescue a rambling talk** — When you sense you are boring people: drop hypotheticals and get specific about yourself; stop and wait in silence; or ask plainly, without blame, whether you have lost them and invite their story. Use when anxiety speeds you up. (Simmons)
- **T447 · Reveal bullets only for long talk** — Make bullets appear one by one only if you will speak to each for at least a minute. Use to stop reading ahead without ten-second pop-ins. (Parikh)
- **T448 · Brief the host** — Write the exact credentials you want shared and brief the moderator beforehand. Use at conferences where a host introduces you. (Parikh)
- **T449 · Hidden credential hook** — Open by noting that colleagues know you but not your background in [X]. Use with colleagues unaware of relevant experience. (Parikh)
- **T450 · Everyday analogy for technical ideas** — Map a technical distinction onto a household routine, such as a diner cooking each order as it arrives versus a bakery baking the whole day's bread before dawn. Use with non-specialists. (Parikh)
- **T451 · You question** — Sketch a tense, high-stakes scenario, ask the audience how they would react, and hold a few seconds of silence. Use in keynotes and recordings with no live dialogue. (Parikh)
- **T452 · Bridge then skip** — When an impatient audience wants to skip ahead, ask for a few minutes to cover prerequisites for their priority, then jump to their section. Use when priorities shift mid-presentation. (Parikh)
- **T453 · Reset pace with a question** — If nerves speed you up, breathe or ask an impromptu topical question to start a brief exchange. Use mid-delivery to break monologue pressure. (Parikh)
- **T454 · Eye-contact hold** — Speak one or two sentences (8-10 seconds) to one person or one sector of a hall before moving on; keep 70% or more lens contact on video. Use continuously in delivery. (Parikh)
- **T455 · Common-purpose intervention** — Wait to see whether a clash between two listeners fades; if it grows, step in with a line that points back to the shared aim, preview positive findings and refocus on the shared goal. Use when conflict hijacks a presentation. (Parikh)
- **T456 · Moderate emotional display** — Show modest disappointment, excitement or indignation, saving extreme vulnerability for shared crises. Use in standard business communication. (Biesenbach)
