# Techniques — finding (T159–T216)

- **T159 · Write from your own felt memory** — Recall a real time you wanted, feared or dreaded something and write the scene from inside that feeling. Use when an outline feels cold or you are blocked. (Rubin)
- **T160 · Ask questions that draw out their story** — Ask an open question with real curiosity, watch for a sigh or shrug, nudge gently ("tell me more"), then stay silent a few seconds until the real situation comes out. Edwards uses one fixed question, "How is your day going?", while people settle before a meeting or interview; Guptara fishes with open questions in networking and sales. Use to learn what the other person is facing. (Edwards; Guptara)
- **T161 · Time, place and noun prompts** — Ask "when did" or "where were you" instead of why, what or how, and name a concrete noun (a town, a person, an object) instead of a broad theme like a whole career. Use when interviewing yourself or others to get specific memories, not opinions. (Callahan; Hall)
- **T162 · Ask for an example story** — After a yes to a trait question in a hiring interview, ask for an example and listen for a specific personal story, not a generality. Use to test whether a claim is lived. (Maxwell)
- **T163 · Interview questions that reach the heart** — When procedural questions give dry routines, ask about relationships (who taught you this trade?), who depends on them at work, or what heritage makes them proud. Use for employees, customers and operational roles where "passion" questions get blank stares. (Biesenbach)
- **T164 · Ask sensory questions** — Ask operators what a precise moment sounds or feels like (the second a crane lifts its load) instead of generic job questions. Use with frontline operators and athletes. (Forman)
- **T165 · Ask what others thought** — With reserved interviewees, ask what a sibling or old classmate said about the new job, rather than how they feel. Use where direct emotional disclosure is uncomfortable. (Forman)
- **T166 · Echo the loaded word** — Repeat one charged word the speaker just used and ask what they meant by it. Use when someone glosses over the emotional core. (Lazer)
- **T167 · Interview to capture stories** — Record on two devices, bring props such as maps or old photos, bring a second listener, and ask five-sense questions without rushing the pace (Rodriguez); with elderly relatives use a phone voice memo plus photos or songs instead of a video camera (Bowles). Use for origin, customer and family stories. (Rodriguez; Bowles)
- **T168 · Interview on location** — Interview people in the places tied to their story so the setting triggers memory: a retired machinist talks at his old workbench; a flood survivor walks the street where the water rose, surfacing details a desk interview misses. Use for leadership profiles and place-based stories. (Forman; Feigenbaum)
- **T169 · Mental scrapbook mining** — Picture concrete objects from the past (a school lunchbox, a car cassette deck, the smell of chlorine) to unlock sensory memory instead of vague summaries. Use when stuck for ideas. (Bowles)
- **T170 · Build from small fragments** — Collect small origin moments and knit them together rather than hunting one grand tale; list orphan memories on one page and pair two that show a before and after in your relationship to a person or issue (Guptara). Use when founders or teams think they have no big story, or when blocked. (Rodriguez; Guptara)
- **T171 · Observe people in context** — With permission, set the scene, record behavior unobtrusively, categorize patterns, probe motives, then validate in larger samples (Quesenberry); score affect from mild to strong and rewrite shorthand notes in second person (Sandler). Use in discovery when surveys hide unconscious habits. (Quesenberry; Sandler)
- **T172 · Brainstorm warm-ups** — Before concepting run yes-and building, forced connection between two unrelated objects, and alternative-use drills. Use when a team anchors early on clichés. (Quesenberry)
- **T173 · Noticing habit** — Choose what you want to be seen paying attention to, watch for those small details in everyday life, and share short stories about them when someone asks a casual check-in question. Use to show values or competence without bragging. (Guptara)
- **T174 · Dig three layers for your why** — Ask why behind your surface ambition (a bigger title, early retirement) twice until you reach the relationship it serves, then anchor it in one real event. Use when drafting a why story so ambition builds empathy. (Guptara)
- **T175 · Rewrite your self-narrative** — Find where your inner story casts you as victim or failure and recast your role around the value you give others. Use for burnout, career detours or self-sabotage. (Guptara)
- **T176 · Borrow a fictional role model** — Recall a character from a favorite book or film who embodies a principle you value and step into their mindset during a hard decision, such as telling a team their project is cancelled. Use in painful leadership duties. (Guptara)
- **T177 · Capture and bank stories on the spot** — Record a fragment the moment it appears, give it a one-line sitcom-style title, add tags (topic, function, emotion, source), and file it where you can search it. Guptara dictates voice memos right after a conversation and tags in a notes app; Haven files each story with first and last lines and logs performances; Callahan and Hall use one-line titles on napkins or phone; Sal indexes by genre, plot, theme and character; Forman adds phone photos and problem-and-outcome notes after each customer meeting; Apps keeps 3x5 cards in pocket and by the bed; Forman also notes trivial daily incidents paired with a work lesson. Use continuously to build a retrievable repertoire. (Guptara; Haven; Callahan; Hall; Sal; Forman; Apps)
- **T178 · Project notebook** — Keep one composition book with character descriptions, family trees, town details, daily word counts and percent complete. Use for continuity on a long writing project. (Apps)
- **T179 · Rotating image file** — Keep a rotating notebook of images, dreams, childhood books, songs and objects, plus a jar of people, places, objects and events prompts, to trigger material. Use when you need a steady supply of starting points. (Maguire)
- **T180 · Write it down soon** — Record a personal story soon after it happens, check details with witnesses, and say "we" or "the team" by default, which guards against fading recall and self-flattery. Use when building a personal repertoire. (Biesenbach)
- **T181 · Broken-habit trigger** — Frame the inciting event as the day a habit or vow broke: "I always... except the day" or "I swore I'd never... but then." Use to turn routine life into plot. (Bowles)
- **T182 · Center a decision, even inaction** — Build the story around one hard choice, including the choice not to act or speak, instead of events that merely happened to you. Use for accidents, grief or confinement. (Bowles)
- **T183 · Future-Tense Questions** — Phrase research and leadership questions around what the brand could become next, not around defending past habits. Use in turnaround planning. (Vincent)
- **T184 · Draft Without Self-Censorship** — Write the first draft as if the founder, former partner or sibling in it were dead and could never read it, then tone down in revision. Use when fear of repercussions blocks honest drafting. (Leitman)
- **T185 · Drafting Momentum Habits** — Keep drafting by working toward the next near turning point instead of the whole book; use ten-minute gaps to reread the last paragraph and write one more; type xxx inline at clunky spots and fix them later by search; leave a concrete next-step note before leaving the desk and pose a specific story question before sleep. Use when drafting stalls. (Cleland)
- **T186 · Six-minute free-writing sprint** — Right after reading a prompt or concept, write fast on paper for six minutes without editing to surface raw ideas and connections. Use when mining material with coaching prompts. (Cowie)
- **T187 · Write by hand** — Write journal entries and project notes longhand on paper, not typed or dictated, to reach subconscious memories. Use in pre-writing and daily reflection. (Apps)
- **T188 · Let the people who lived it speak** — Have the people who experienced the event tell it in their own unscripted words: a farmer describing a drought, beneficiaries describing your impact in their own language, the frontline employee or patient narrating instead of a spokesperson or actor. Use in interviews, videos and panels, and when insiders default to clichés. (Maestas; Forman)
- **T189 · Mine Present Reality** — Write from honest current struggles rather than clever made-up premises from beyond your own life. Use when you have run dry of fresh material for speeches or comedy. (Maestas)
- **T190 · Walk while talking** — Hold anxious or painful conversations side by side on a walk instead of face to face, so movement eases tension. Buster uses it to coach nervous speakers; Miller for grief or teen conflict. Use for coaching and sensitive mining. (Buster; Miller)
- **T191 · Founder story mining** — Look for three things: a childhood memory that shows an early calling, the exact frustration where the status quo failed you and your first scrappy experiments, and unglamorous early sacrifices such as debt, rejection and manual labor. Use for founder stories, including investor pitches where resilience matters. (Hall)
- **T192 · Ghostwrite for the reluctant teller** — Interview a modest founder for moments of change, draft the story yourself, and read it back so they see their everyday life as a story worth telling. Use when a founder fears bragging. (Hall)
- **T193 · When did I learn this?** — Write down the lesson your team needs, then ask when in your life or career you learned it the hard way; that moment is the story. Use to align a team without lecturing. (Hall)
- **T194 · Bedtime worry test** — Imagine your prospect lying in bed, eyes on the ceiling, and pin down the precise worry that keeps them awake; that is the emotion for the story's opening. Use for value stories to prospects. (Hall)
- **T195 · Composite case study** — Combine true fears, setbacks and details from several real clients into one representative hero. Use when no single interview covers the objections prospects share. (Hauge)
- **T196 · Timeline wall retrospective** — Tape a long paper timeline to a wall, have each team member stick notes on milestone moments, and ask "what happened there?" for each. Use in project reviews to surface lesson stories when people claim there is nothing to learn. (Callahan)
- **T197 · Triangulate several stories** — Collect stories from different vantage points on the same issue before trusting one anecdote. Use when probing culture or operations problems, where the surrounding context alters what a story means. (Callahan)
- **T198 · Listen for what goes unsaid** — Notice nervousness, body language and why the speaker chose that era or episode, stop rehearsing your reply, and ask one targeted question about their feelings. Use in interviews, sales discovery and leadership conversations. (Buster; Cody)
- **T199 · Three R's of receiving a story** — Remain silent while it is told, respond warmly afterward, and later remind the teller what stayed with you. Use when someone shares a personal story with you. (Maguire)
- **T200 · Interview the people around the hero** — Interview the hero's partner or parent, or even cast the spouse as the hero, to surface unseen struggles, fears and the gains felt at home. Use when the client's problem touched loved ones. (Hauge)
- **T201 · Listen before pitching** — Leave the brochures behind, arrive with a blank notepad, record the client's concerns, then tell how your solution answers them. Use in consulting and B2B pitches. (Forman)
- **T202 · Hunt for an incongruous personal object** — Scan a technical team's workspace for an odd personal item (a vintage synthesizer tucked under a desk) and ask about it to open a relaxed human conversation and find a hook. Use with reticent, jargon-heavy specialists. (Forman)
- **T203 · Product-free photo prompts** — Show, or ask staff to bring, photos of human wellbeing with no company machines or products, and ask which one triggers a personal memory before drafting. Use at workshop starts to move technical staff from product-thinking to people-thinking. (Forman)
- **T204 · Brainstorm three levels of solution** — List resolution options as three levels: low-risk standard improvement, radical high-risk innovation, and doing nothing. Use when brainstorming the recommendations in Act III. (McKinsey)
- **T205 · Seek the quiet voices** — Look for overlooked people and unlit corners, not the loudest public facts. Use in interviewing and memory mining. (Apps)
- **T206 · Draft in third person** — Write a raw personal memory, such as a first-day-of-school panic, as "he" or "she" to gain perspective and fresh detail. Use when a memory feels too raw or cliché. (James)
- **T207 · Put designers next to the product** — Seat designers within a stone's throw of the product being built and have them feel the end user's physical risks, so commitment becomes emotional, not theoretical. Use in small R&D teams. (Maxwell)
- **T208 · Sympathetic listening to opponents** — Tell an opponent or reluctant source you know what happened and will support them if they tell their version, then listen quietly for small inconsistencies. Use to learn from an antagonist without hostility. (Maxwell)
- **T209 · Shared-observation icebreaker** — Have rival specialists silently watch live specimens in jars for ten minutes, swap childhood stories about them, then perform short plays in mixed teams using skull photos as masks. Use to dissolve competing vocabularies and spark collaboration. (Maxwell)
- **T210 · Crisis journal** — In hard times keep a journal answered with the same five story-building questions each time to find structure in chaos. Use during personal or work crises. (Maguire)
- **T211 · Childhood hero mining** — Recall a childhood hero, ask what you imitated or buried, then use that figure in one of four roles: straight anchor, contrast, metaphor or comic foil. Use when building a personal story. (Maguire)
- **T212 · Body map of memories** — Draw a body outline, mark scars, illnesses and habits, and answer three prompts about each to release physical memories. Use when mind-based recall runs dry. (Maguire)
- **T213 · Executive job swap** — Have a leader work a full day in a frontline role, such as loading-dock dispatch, and share the chaotic experience. Use to build empathy and expose operational pain points. (Cody)
- **T214 · Trend whiteboard session** — Run a 60-minute cross-team brainstorm where any cultural trend is allowed on the board, and turn winners into shoots or posts within days. Use to keep a brand newsroom timely. (Snow; Lazauskas)
- **T215 · Cliché search filter** — Search the anecdote online and drop it if it returns hundreds of thousands of hits or is a stock conference parable (the boiled frog, the bamboo that grows overnight). Use when researching anecdotes. (Biesenbach)
- **T216 · Interrogate the character** — Question a character like a skeptical detective during profiling and reject first easy answers until sharp motives emerge. Use when a character's motives feel generic. (Haven)
