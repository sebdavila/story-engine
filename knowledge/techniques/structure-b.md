# Techniques — structure (T080–T158)

- **T080 · The specific person over the category** — Describe your actual mother or mentor, with clothes, car and quirks, instead of mothers or leaders in general. Use to trigger listeners' memories of their own parallel relationships. (Simmons)
- **T081 · Before/after contrast** — Paint the painful or ordinary starting state, such as a sunny late-summer day, so the transformation or dramatic ending lands hard; add permissioned photos of the old struggle and new reward, or a family photo in a newsletter. Use in speeches, emails, blogs and sales pages. (Sandler; Buster; Hauge; Dolan)
- **T082 · Human protagonist, not the organization** — Pick one person, the executive or closest contact hit by the crisis, as hero. Use for every B2B case study. (Hauge)
- **T083 · Naming characters** — Name central characters (first names or Mum, Nana), skip names of minor people, and use a stated role plus pseudonym when privacy matters. If you forgot a name, say so rather than invent one; ask permission or quietly change a recent acquaintance's name. Use when humanizing stories without clutter. (Dolan; Callahan)
- **T084 · Late surprise setback** — Drop an unexpected disaster, such as the site crashing mid-celebration, just before victory. Use late in the story to re-grab attention. (Hauge)
- **T085 · Stack adversity at the climax** — Hit the protagonist at the all-is-lost moment with geological, biological, social, relational and psychological adversity at once. Use at the climax. (Emunds)
- **T086 · Ticking clock** — Attach a firm deadline or looming loss to the goal, such as earning $30,000 before foreclosure. Use at the commitment point to raise urgency. (Hauge)
- **T087 · Recurring object motif** — Repeat one physical prop to make a theme visible, or let an evolving personal object, like a shrinking bandage, mark passing time; flash light off the same object to bridge backstory to the present. Use instead of exposition. (Simson; D'Costa)
- **T088 · Figures of speech** — Use deliberate exaggeration ("peeling an apple with a cloud") or part-for-whole shorthand ("plastic" for credit cards). Use to make dull virtues memorable and repeatable. (Sandler)
- **T089 · Fresh physical metaphors** — Invent metaphors from texture, motion or threat, such as pain like a train in a tunnel, not clichés. Use to activate sensory-motor regions and set mood. (Storr)
- **T090 · Specific aftermath, universal meaning** — Paint one precise reward, such as a log house by a lake, that stands for freedom, peace or security. Use in the closing vision so varied buyers connect. (Hauge)
- **T091 · Verbatim commitment** — Give the hero's own spoken resolve and deadline word for word, as they decide to buy or begin. Use to finish the commitment step. (Hauge)
- **T092 · Guided deduction** — Hand listeners explicit premises and clues so they reach the right conclusion themselves. Use to introduce unfamiliar services or expectations. (Sandler)
- **T093 · Opening epigraph** — Open with a short curated quote that signals theme or mood, and explain the link later. Use as a curtain-opener before exposition. (Sandler)
- **T094 · Orienting exposition** — Orient through definition, comparison, contrast, cause-effect or classification, introducing places, supporting players and enabling tech with spoiler-free hints. Use early without a data dump. (Sandler)
- **T095 · Dramatize exposition inside a scene** — Deliver science, history, rules or backstory through a short scene of learning, a pre-battle debate, a collision with the rule, or a sensory memory cue, only when a character meets it. Use whenever background is needed. (Dicks; Emunds; Barker & Brown; Cleland)
- **T096 · Steady subject, shifting tone** — Hold the subject anchor (tenor) constant while varying mood (tone) across scenes. Use to stay clear while varying emotion. (Sandler)
- **T097 · World-building** — Detail context, resources, economics, history, regulations, diverse users, legacy tech, training and health impact around a design. Use to give a design soul. (Sandler)
- **T098 · Unexplained specific names** — Drop hyper-specific, plausible proper nouns or measurements without explaining them. Use in speculative fiction so readers infer scale. (Storr)
- **T099 · Sequence only the emotional pulse points** — Choose only the beginning, middle and end moments that carry the story's heart, write explicit transitions, and end with a question tying the lesson to the audience's own mentors or challenges. Use for personal or strategic speeches. (Forman)
- **T100 · Bottom-of-slide bridge callout** — Put a callout box at the slide bottom only to state the link to the next slide. Use when a slide could prompt several next questions. (McKinsey)
- **T101 · Ellipsis bridge across slide titles** — End one title with an ellipsis and start the next with an ellipsis plus a conjunction. Use for a two-part causal argument across consecutive slides. (McKinsey)
- **T102 · Question-answer slide chain** — Make each slide provoke one main question, and have the next full-sentence title answer it; give every slide a distinct so-what title; ask the next question aloud before clicking. Use in every section and live data talks. (McKinsey; Edwards)
- **T103 · Sentence headlines that tell the story** — Phrase every slide title, heading and chart title as a complete sentence, verb included, that states the takeaway, then read headings alone to check they are parallel and coherent. Use on every deck, report and structural review. (Edwards; Lupton; Parikh)
- **T104 · Short three-act agenda** — Limit the agenda to five items of five words, one line, no sub-bullets or footnotes, as Acts I to III plus appendix or epilogue. Use on every agenda slide. (McKinsey)
- **T105 · Keep the same business characters** — Introduce the core metric as protagonist and its drivers as friends and foes in Act I; restrict Act II to how trends affect those same characters. Use to keep situation and complication cohesive. (McKinsey)
- **T106 · Recap mid-act** — Repeat the cumulative-impact summary slide after a long run of standard actions, before radical ones. Use in complex Act III sections. (McKinsey)
- **T107 · Epilogue for an urgent side issue** — Add a labeled post-resolution slide only for an existential short-term problem outside the main timeframe, pairing the threat with solutions. Use sparingly after Act III. (McKinsey)
- **T108 · Cross character arcs** — Plot each character's rise and fall on one graph and schedule confrontations where lines cross. Use in documentaries, brand films and fiction. (Lazer)
- **T109 · Nest small conflicts inside the big one** — Place moment-to-moment struggles, like fighting for scarce lifeboats, inside the main conflict. Use to sustain suspense across scenes. (Karia)
- **T110 · Precise over vague** — Swap vague quantities and times for exact ones (500 CEOs, five days later, 6 foot 5), and literary adjectives for concrete, slightly exaggerated comparisons. Use for characters, settings and timelines. (Karia; James)
- **T111 · Quote dialogue directly** — Give the exact words spoken, not a third-person summary of them. Use in key exchanges and named-character stories. (Karia; Parikh)
- **T112 · Summarized dialogue** — Paraphrase remarks inside narration when full dialogue stalls pace. Use for low-value exchanges. (Haven)
- **T113 · Close on one repeatable line** — Compress the lesson or central promise into one short, proverb-like sentence and pause around it. Use at the close and in revision. (Karia; Kamath)
- **T114 · Punchy closing line** — Add a brief final line, such as a mock instruction to do it all again, that lifts a dull memory into a joke. Use when revising anecdotes. (Apps)
- **T115 · Date the scene with period objects** — Show the era through an everyday object, a hit song or film from that year, or era-specific nouns (an '82 Corolla), not a date dump. Use when setting past scenes. (Apps; Leitman)
- **T116 · Break the pattern on the third beat** — Repeat a comic mishap twice, then meet the third with a fresh obstacle. Use in escalating comedy. (James)
- **T117 · Breaking an established pattern** — Set up a strict numerical or naming pattern and break it at the end. Use in closings to spark curiosity and reflection. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T118 · Prove change through action** — End on an observable act that proves transformation, not "so I learned"; show what the protagonist does with the new knowledge. Use in payoffs and internal-growth climaxes. (James; Coyne)
- **T119 · Subtle bridge from anecdote to point** — Link anecdote to point with a light personal remark, or no transition. Use so listeners connect the dots themselves. (James)
- **T120 · Plain tags and action beats** — Use said or asked about every 3 to 4 lines, cut adverbs and fancy verbs, add action beats that confirm or contradict the line, and perform tone on stage instead of writing it. Use when drafting dialogue. (Emunds; Barker & Brown; Leitman; Haven)
- **T121 · Close subplots before the climax** — Open subplots in Acts 1 and 2 and resolve all of them before the climax. Use so resolutions do not dilute the climactic emotion. (Emunds)
- **T122 · Signature body part per character** — Give each major character one recurring body part tied to their psychology, such as a cheerful smile, for beats and tags. Use to make characters instantly legible. (Emunds)
- **T123 · Twist the last word** — Set up a familiar phrase and swerve on its final word so listeners invent the backstory. Use to make passive listeners active. (Maxwell)
- **T124 · Secondary value shifts under one core value** — Track one global core value start to end while subplot scenes shift secondary internal and external values. Use so subplots reinforce the main arc. (Coyne)
- **T125 · Define the fate worse than death** — Decide what is worse than death or plain failure for major characters, such as selling out or ruining a loved one. Use before the global crisis, even in action. (Coyne)
- **T126 · Plans fail, improvisation wins** — Make pre-planned tactics collapse so survival depends on spontaneous adaptation. Use in the Action core event, the hero at the villain's mercy. (Coyne)
- **T127 · Any lawless frontier can host a Western** — Set Western conflict wherever law and order are missing, such as outer space. Use to adapt individual-versus-society dynamics to modern or speculative settings. (Coyne)
- **T128 · Fill the audience with the judges who matter** — Seat the people the protagonist most wants to please in the Performance climax, with the risk of shame visible. Use for the big performance scene. (Coyne)
- **T129 · Truth revealed in the revolution** — Expose either the tyrant's hypocrisy (victory) or a leader's co-option (defeat) in the Society core event, violent or peaceful. Use at the society climax. (Coyne)
- **T130 · Retelling the familiar from another angle** — Retell a well-known story in a new setting or from the other party's view, such as the wolf or a field rep. Use when audiences assume they know the meaning or sit in departmental tunnel vision. (Schwabauer & Sweet; Simmons)
- **T131 · Mixed emotion and juxtaposition** — Load a moment or ending with conflicting yet compatible emotions, or set unrelated things side by side to imply a link. Use to reflect life honestly without tipping the scale. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T132 · Escalating test ladder** — Push the protagonist through harder tests, from cheap conformity to solitary mortal risk, until hidden flaws show. Use across the middle. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T133 · Cost paid on the way to the goal** — Make the hero lose comfort, allies or a mentor en route. Use when stakes feel cheap. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T134 · Small shared lie with spreading damage** — Give a decent hero a subtle, widely shared private lie and press until its fallout hurts those around them. Use to make flaws relatable. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T135 · Speech quality reveals allegiance** — Give arrogant or misguided characters weaker rhetoric and no intimate names, truthful voices sharper poetry. Use to show allegiance through diction. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T136 · Naming shift signals change** — Change how a character names or addresses another only after surviving the climactic trial. Use near resolution to imply transformation. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T137 · Narrator blind to their own absurdity** — Keep a flawed narrator in character while readers see what the narrator misses. Use in first-person or close narration for humor or critique. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T138 · Second-person villain viewpoint** — Narrate a familiar conflict as "you" from the villain's side, exposing blind spots while readers stay outside the villain's head. Use to create dramatic irony. (Schwabauer & Sweet)
- **T139 · Filmic word order** — Put actor before action and the transferred object before the recipient, so the sentence plays in visual order. Use in all prose action lines. (Storr)
- **T140 · Three concrete attributes** — Give three concrete attributes per key object (dark blue, carpet, worn) instead of judgments like terrible. Use to build vivid mental simulations. (Storr)
- **T141 · Pair a non-visual sense with a visual noun** — Fuse smell, touch, taste or sound to a visible noun, such as a cabbagey brown sock. Use to ground readers in a setting. (Storr)
- **T142 · Mistaken read of another mind** — Have A act on a wrong guess about what B thinks of A, causing unintended escalation. Use for interpersonal conflict or comedy. (Storr)
- **T143 · Ironic everyday detail after a turn** — Place an ironic ordinary detail, such as a bargain-shorts radio ad during a marital collapse, right after a turn. Use for subtext without commentary. (Storr)
- **T144 · Character revealed through their space** — Fill rooms with identity claims (certificates), feeling regulators (candles) and behavioral residue (hidden bottles). Use when a place must reveal its owner. (Storr)
- **T145 · Description through the character's lens** — Describe every setting and stranger through the protagonist's flawed memories and current emotion, not an objective narrator. Use from page one. (Storr; Barker & Brown)
- **T146 · Involuntary slip** — Let a giggle, glance or wrong word leak a hidden self while the inner narrator invents an excuse. Use to expose repressed desire or division. (Storr)
- **T147 · Intensify the inciting event** — Make it the final straw on the worst day, add a tight clock and forced secrecy, and set the hero against their most feared person. Use when ignition is too mild. (Storr)
- **T148 · Co-leads with opposite flaws** — Pair two leads embodying opposite versions of one flaw so their clashes heal both. Use in buddy stories and rom-coms. (Storr)
- **T149 · Plain action statement** — State what your hero does in plain concrete terms, between empty generalities and buzzwords, so a stranger would ask more. Use when drafting the what block. (Cowie)
- **T150 · Fight-or-flight cues** — Describe panting, racing pulse and tunnel vision from close perspective to trigger matching physiology. Use in suspense and danger scenes. (Gottschall)
- **T151 · Chance only at the start** — Let improbable chance create the opening predicament, then resolve the climax through character choices and values. Use to avoid deus ex machina. (Gottschall)
- **T152 · Names and nicknames** — Combine formal names with nicknames from family, friends, enemies and the character, packing backstory into introductions. Use in character intros. (Haven)
- **T153 · Repeated exaggerated reaction** — Give a character one stress reflex, exaggerate it and repeat it across scenes. Use for humor and truth. (Haven)
- **T154 · Bedroom-level visualization** — Before drafting, picture each setting's light, smells, sounds, textures, temperature and object history as well as you know your bedroom. Use in pre-draft prep. (Haven)
- **T155 · Description before action** — Place heavy description in the quieter scenes before, so the action scene itself moves quickly. Use when planning set pieces. (Haven)
- **T156 · Short sentences at the climax** — Shorten sentences and cut commas and conjunctions in climaxes to speed pace. Use in action peaks. (Haven)
- **T157 · Quiet beat before the climax** — Insert a calm beat highlighting the hero's frailty before the climax to sharpen contrast. Use in the run-up to the peak. (Haven)
- **T158 · Image reversal for humor** — Flip a dramatic visual with one final detail, such as unplugging a coin-op horse, or cut away to a down-to-earth contrasting viewpoint. Use for comic punchlines. (Haven)
