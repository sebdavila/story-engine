# Techniques — delivery (T290–T373)

- **T290 · Three-part slide header** — Give each slide an optional small section label, a required full-sentence so-what title (large, two lines at most) and an optional subheadline; the label can shift color as the narrative moves from situation to resolution. Use on every content slide. (McKinsey)
- **T291 · Inner Beats in Dialogue** — Replace physical gestures between lines with a short observation, thought or memory colored by the viewpoint character's mood. Use in written dialogue that feels like talking heads. (Barker; Brown)
- **T292 · Emotional Transfusion** — Feel the exact emotion you want the audience to feel while drafting or rehearsing, for example laugh while writing a comic passage. Use before drafting or presenting. (Terenzzo)
- **T293 · Group minor characters** — Lump passing figures together ("everyone in Accounting") and fold repeated events into a single scene as long as the truth survives. Use when real-life logistics clutter the ear. (Bowles)
- **T294 · Slice the story by channel** — Use who and what in email subject lines, add why on X, add how and where on LinkedIn, and open websites, decks and summaries with the full 50-word version. Use when one story must travel across business media. (Cowie)
- **T295 · Invite named people to comment** — Skip "questions at the end"; welcome questions throughout and ask specific attendees by name for their view at the relevant point. Use in presentations so listeners feel heard. (Edwards)
- **T296 · Engage remote listeners by name** — On calls, stand, use more slides than in person so each flip regains attention, and give a named warning ("Jane, a question for you coming up") before asking. Use in phone or remote presentations. (McKinsey)
- **T297 · Tension-resolution sentences** — Say key lines in two halves: the first raises the risk, the second resolves it. Use sparingly at major turning points. (Edwards)
- **T298 · Show progress through the deck** — Add a progress bar, color-code narrative sections, or highlight the active agenda section at each transition with one or two contrast treatments. Introduce the agenda with a pause or a four-sentence roadmap. Use in long decks. (Edwards; McKinsey)
- **T299 · Vary slide pace and position** — Follow one dense slide with five slides at ten seconds each, and change your position: beside the screen, seated, at the back looking with the audience, or back turned once to read a key slide. Use to reset attention. (Edwards)
- **T300 · Virtual shared-screen pointing** — Imagine sitting beside the viewer at one laptop and give explicit visual cues ("see the red bar", "scan to the bottom"). Use in video meetings where room body language is invisible. (Edwards)
- **T301 · Teammates introduce each other** — Each presenter gives the next a warm personal endorsement of their expertise instead of reciting their own résumé. Use in team pitches and call transfers. (Livesay)
- **T302 · Repeat the question, answer calmly** — Repeat each question and confirm it, begin the answer in the asker's own words, say "I don't know" when stumped, politely cut off question hogs, and thank challengers. Use in live Q&A. (Livesay; Apps)
- **T303 · Drop 'good question'** — That reflex implies other questions were weaker; say you are glad it came up, or explain why it matters. Use in group Q&A. (Livesay)
- **T304 · Close with a summary, not 'any questions'** — Finish the Q&A by restating the client's problems and why your team fits, then invite them to come along on the journey. Use to close any formal pitch. (Livesay)
- **T305 · Five ways to handle a question** — Choose per question: answer briefly now, defer to a named later section, take it offline, log it as an action item, or throw it to a knowledgeable listener. Use in Q&A and checkpoints to keep control of the schedule. (Parikh)
- **T306 · Question windows** — Ask an interrupting crowd to hold questions for ten-minute intervals and take two or three at each window. Use when constant interruptions threaten the briefing. (Parikh)
- **T307 · Acknowledge and park** — Thank the dissenter for their experience, calmly give the evidence that points the other way, and offer to go through detailed examples offline. Use when someone insists your approach will never work. (Parikh)
- **T308 · Finish early** — Use about two-thirds of the time given: 10 minutes of a 15, 20 of a 30. Use in investor and executive meetings to respect time and leave room for questions. (Livesay)
- **T309 · Pre-stage calming ritual** — Do one short repeatable routine just before speaking: a two-minute hands-on-hips pose while recalling 3-5 past wins, a ten-second heart affirmation, a thumb-and-forefinger press then release, three slow breaths held 1-2 seconds, or any personal ritual (song, walk, mantra). Use right before going on. (Livesay; Bowles; James; Parikh)
- **T310 · A breath before the first word** — Keep your introduction short, walk up, and take one visible breath or silent beat before the first word. Use before any live story. (Leitman; Haven)
- **T311 · Vary voice like an instrument** — Change rate, pitch and volume on purpose: punch key words, drop volume at tense moments, speed into the climax, pause before the resolution or after a punchline. Add roughly 50% more energy and a slower base pace; give characters their own pitch register; keep whispers and shouts rare. Use in any oral delivery. (Livesay; Haven; Biesenbach)
- **T312 · Voice each character slightly differently** — Nudge pace, pitch and volume a little for each speaker in quoted dialogue. Use when you want distinct speakers without full acting; see T313 for full embodiment. (Karia)
- **T313 · Embody each character** — Shift posture, voice, face and position to become each person you quote, including your past self, instead of "he said, she said". Change spine and center of gravity (Simmons), accept a broad imperfect impression if nervous (Cody), or fix an imagined partner in one spot beside you (Biesenbach). Use in live dialogue; see T314. (Dolan; Cody; Simmons; Biesenbach)
- **T314 · No borrowed accents** — Do not imitate accents of other countries or cultures; use one only for your own parents, grandparents or home region. Use in all spoken stories. (Dicks)
- **T315 · Color to steer emotion over time** — Use cool palettes for baseline narration and shift to warm ones at climactic tension; paint key storyboard frames early as a color script. Use in video, decks, games and motion graphics. (Rodriguez; Lupton)
- **T316 · Layout that groups the eye** — Set headline, spacing, type size and capitalization so ideas cluster visually before anyone reads. In print use one or two fonts, left-aligned body copy, negative space and thirds-based focal points. Use for blogs, decks and white papers. (Rodriguez; Quesenberry)
- **T317 · Plain, spoken language** — Write as one person talking: replace jargon, euphemism and inflated terms with everyday words ("scared" for "anxious"), favor short sentences with strong verbs, and move from a lead-in bridge to a low-friction call to action. Strip adjectives when an account must read as the only possible one. Use in copy, meetings and consultations. (Quesenberry; Dolan; Guptara; Maestas; Kamath; Buster; Salmon)
- **T318 · Six speech moves of a great address** — Anchor in a symbolic setting, combine logic and emotion, use vivid metaphor, contrast what is with what could be, build rhythmic repetition, close on a disciplined call to action. Use for keynotes and brand anthems. (Quesenberry)
- **T319 · Tell stories without announcing them** — Keep your normal voice, posture and pace when moving into a story; do not switch to a storyteller tone. Use in meetings and one-to-ones to avoid resistance. (Guptara)
- **T320 · Memorize only first and last lines** — Memorize verbatim only the opening line, closing line and essential refrains or names; tell the middle in your own words. Minutes before going on, speak just those lines. Use for non-literary tales. (Haven)
- **T321 · Rehearse beats, not a script** — Rehearse the emotional beats and core message but stop short of word-for-word memorizing; when adapting written work, jot sparse bullets of events, dates and names. Use to leave room for live reactions. (Hall; Callahan)
- **T322 · Read sources aloud to learn** — Read source texts aloud rather than silently so you absorb their spoken rhythm. Use when first learning a story. (Haven)
- **T323 · Break the story into labeled scenes** — Draw lines between scenes on a printed copy, number and title each, or sketch stick-figure frames, until each title triggers its events. Use when you lose plot order. (Haven)
- **T324 · Tag scenes with emotion** — For each scene ask how each character feels and how you would feel in their place. Use when learning a story so emotion anchors recall. (Haven)
- **T325 · Tell about it instead of performing** — Before performing, summarize the events and describe the main characters aloud as if telling a friend about what happened. Use in early practice and when nervous. (Haven; James)
- **T326 · Retell at spaced intervals** — Tell a new story to a colleague right away, then again within a day, a week and a month; for a name you keep forgetting, attach a vivid slightly gross mental image. Use to lock a story into memory. (Callahan; Guptara)
- **T327 · Snippet practice** — Rehearse only the shaky 30-second transition in spare minutes instead of the whole story. Use when short on rehearsal time. (Haven)
- **T328 · Silent body-only rehearsal** — Perform the whole story privately with movement and gesture but no words. Use before vocal practice when you feel stiff. (Haven)
- **T329 · Pause before the turn** — Pause and breathe between scenes; go quiet or still right before a reveal, punchline or climax; hold a frozen posture for two seconds at an emotional peak; use short pauses between sections, word-by-word pauses for emphasis, and a one-to-two-second pause (or sip of water) after your link sentence. Wait out big laughs. Use throughout live delivery; see T350 for the opposing view. (Haven; Maguire; Simmons; Parikh; Dolan; James; Sandler; Lupton)
- **T330 · Visualize the scene while telling** — Picture the setting, heat, steps or faces as you speak so voice and gesture follow without choreography; in preparation, invent unstated sensory details of the place. Use in delivery and when you blank. (Bowles; Haven; Dolan; Biesenbach; Callahan)
- **T331 · Adjust to the audience live** — Watch smiles, posture shifts and watch-glances, then change detail density, speed or energy, or cut and expand sections on the spot. Use throughout live telling. (Haven; Forman)
- **T332 · Read the room** — Check sensitivities beforehand (such as recent layoffs) and change tone or drop the story mid-delivery if the room is tense. Use in live speaking. (Biesenbach)
- **T333 · Verbatim with emotion** — When learning a literary tale word for word, pair each phrase with the character's emotion, intent and inflection. Use only for authored literary tales. (Haven)
- **T334 · Test without props first** — Practice the tale with no prop; add one only if something essential is missing; show cultural items after the story. Use when evaluating props; contrast T335. (Haven)
- **T335 · Reveal a real prop at the key moment** — Hold back a physical object or model until the beat where it pays off the story, then show it. Use in sales conversations, self-deprecating stories and when drawings fail to convey a radical idea. (Simmons; Hall; Sandler)
- **T336 · Pass artifacts around the room** — Let a live room handle or taste story objects rather than relying on slides. Use with youth or community audiences. (Apps)
- **T337 · Physical props as story prompts** — Collect small natural objects in a bag as prompts; give a recurring puppet its own voice, never your authority voice; play with a new puppet yourself repeatedly before expecting others to. Use with children and for instant group prompts. (Miller)
- **T338 · Costume on a hanger** — Bring a character garment on a hanger instead of wearing it. Use to add period context without locking into one character. (Haven)
- **T339 · Stumble-line participation cue** — Have a character fumble a familiar line or pause mid-refrain, eyebrows up, hands rolling, so listeners chime in. Use with familiar tales or young audiences. (Haven)
- **T340 · Motion in icons** — Add arrows, motion lines and asymmetry so static icons read as actions. Use in UI icon sets and service diagrams. (Lupton)
- **T341 · General, timeless icons** — Keep icons broad rather than trendy, hyper-specific or branded so they work across contexts. Use when building icon libraries. (Lupton)
- **T342 · Narrator steps aside** — Limit narrator voice to a short opening, then let characters speak and act. Use in narrative fiction and epic retellings. (Aristotle)
- **T343 · Mix plain and uncommon words** — Blend everyday words for clarity with selected metaphors or loan words for dignity; test by swapping an elevated word for a plain synonym and keep it only if it lifts the line. Use in line editing. (Aristotle)
- **T344 · Save ornate language for quiet stretches** — Put brilliant diction in calm passages, not in action, moral-choice or argument scenes. Use when pacing style. (Aristotle)
- **T345 · Younger self plus hindsight** — Let the younger self's urgent worldview sit beside adult hindsight so small past stakes feel large. Use in childhood or teen stories; contrast T346. (Bowles)
- **T346 · Past-Self Perspective** — Narrate only through the beliefs, insecurities or cockiness you held then, such as thinking at twelve that tellers keep the cash. Use in childhood or youth stories. (Leitman)
- **T347 · Voice your inner dialogue** — Say aloud the full escalating chain of thoughts you had, not the tidy surface thought, and weave your judgments into the action; pair a cool spoken line with the private meaning. Use for tension, vulnerability, or to make yourself the star of an event you only witnessed. (Leitman; Bowles)
- **T348 · Pressure-valve humor** — Include naturally absurd details from grim events to give listeners a breath without forced jokes. Use in heavy or tragic stories. (Bowles)
- **T349 · Handle laughs** — Pause through expected or surprise laughs so people do not suppress them; if a joke gets silence, move on. Use in live delivery. (Bowles)
- **T350 · Speak through the tail of laughter** — Adjust the mic calmly, project to the back wall, and start slightly before laughter or applause dies. Use in live venues to hold pace and include listeners with hearing loss. (Dicks)
- **T351 · Acknowledge tears** — If rehearsal reveals a line that makes your voice break, plan to pause, breathe or tell the room, instead of pushing through. Use in emotional stories and eulogies. (Bowles)
- **T352 · Desensitize painful lines** — Say the lines that bring on tears out loud again and again in rehearsal until they hurt less. Use when preparing grief stories. (Dicks)
- **T353 · Shift to present tense at the peak** — Start in past tense, drop to present as a zoom at peak action, return to past for backstory or distant moments; narrate history or technical events in present and second person ("It is 1896, you are in Detroit"). Skip it if it drains your focus. Use for immediacy. (Dicks; Bowles; James)
- **T354 · Repeat crucial facts** — Say a critical fact twice or slow down around it so listeners do not miss setup needed later. Use when a detail could slip past the ear. (Bowles)
- **T355 · Speaker tags before quotes** — Put "I said" or "she said" before the line and drop tags once speakers are clear. Use throughout spoken dialogue. (Bowles)
- **T356 · Use I, not you or we** — Swap generalizing "you" or "we" for "I" to stay tied to your own vulnerability. Use whenever distancing language creeps in. (Bowles)
- **T357 · Possessive personalisation** — Replace "the launch plan" with "your launch plan" or "our project". Use in client and internal narratives for ownership. (Parikh)
- **T358 · Grounded stage presence** — Leave the mic on its stand, empty pockets of coins, plant your feet, keep water near, and mention visible shaking aloud in relaxed rooms (in formal ones, pause and cover your knees). Use in stage delivery. (Bowles)
- **T359 · Mic and voice care** — Hold a handheld mic about eight inches from your mouth, wear a button-down for a lavalier, drink lukewarm water rather than ice, coffee or tea. Use before and during amplified talks. (James)
- **T360 · Alternate Emotional Polarity** — Swing between positive and negative emotions across the copy. Use in longer stories to hold attention. (Mastropierro)
- **T361 · Micro-Story Compression** — Condense a brand legacy into one tight paragraph with wordplay and tribute; or write each social post as beginning, middle, end from a recurring narrator, opening mid-action and closing on personal reflection rather than preaching. Use for About pages and daily social posts. (Mastropierro; Terenzzo)
- **T362 · Worldview-Matched Metaphors** — Draw each viewpoint character's imagery from their trade or background (a gardener thinks in roots and rain) instead of verbal tics. Use in multi-POV fiction. (Barker; Brown)
- **T363 · Early POV Signposting** — At each change of perspective, identify whose viewpoint it is in the chapter heading or opening sentence. Use in multi-POV books. (Barker; Brown)
- **T364 · Familiar Anchor for the Strange** — Pair an unfamiliar speculative element with recognizable things (horses, snow, dogs) and show characters reacting rather than lecturing. Use in world-building. (Barker; Brown)
- **T365 · Plain Invented Names** — Coin clear, evocative names ("the Wall") rather than apostrophe-laden ones, and call dog-like creatures dogs. Use for invented terms. (Barker; Brown)
- **T366 · Task-Driven Dialogue** — Give talking characters an annoying physical task, such as lighting a damp fire in wind, so emotion leaks through action. Use in dialogue scenes. (Barker; Brown)
- **T367 · One Speaker per Paragraph** — Keep a speaker's words and own action beat together; give another character's silent reaction its own paragraph. Use when formatting dialogue. (Barker; Brown)
- **T368 · Dialogue Subtext** — Never let characters simply agree; add unspoken history, silence, mishearing, fragments and parallel threads that do not align. Use in written dialogue. (Barker; Brown)
- **T369 · Guide Without Exposition** — Give hosts or digital guides consistent gestures and world-appropriate etiquette, or steer gaze with directional audio, light and motion in unframed 360 or VR space, rather than explaining. Use in tours, themed spaces, games and immersive theater. (Paulsen)
- **T370 · Sensory-Safe Tuning** — Use subdued lighting, low sun angles, predictable transitions and adjustable intensity instead of strobing or volume spikes. Use in VR and physical installations. (Paulsen)
- **T371 · Social Layer on Every Channel** — End radio, print or banner ads by pointing people to a social interaction or gamified feed. Use when planning traditional or digital campaigns. (Vaynerchuk)
- **T372 · Effortless Response Prompts** — Photograph everyday assets and gamify them ("tag who you'd sit with"); label A or B directly under comparison photos so no one must guess names. Use for cheap daily posts and poll images. (Vaynerchuk)
- **T373 · Share With a Point of View** — Take a bold, contrarian or funny stance on an article instead of posting a neutral summary; on Pinterest add honest practical comments to repins and others' boards. Use on Twitter and Pinterest. (Vaynerchuk)
