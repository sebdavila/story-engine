# Story types

## S01 · Classic plot genres
**Purpose:** Give a business story a plot the audience already knows, so the point travels on a familiar shape. **Audience:** Customers, teams, general audiences. **Teller:** Sellers, leaders, marketers.
**Choose when:** The point is grit, persistence, beating a threat, reinvention or stepping out of comfort. Match plot to point: rising from disadvantage for grit; the quest to show you do not give up without bragging (Livesay); overcoming the monster for resilience, told as transferable lessons rather than heroics or self-pity (Edwards); rebirth to help a prospect picture a fresh start; leave home and return changed to move buyers out of a safe routine, with customers as the brand's heroes (Livesay); the innovation story for persistence against resistance to a new idea (Callahan).
**Shape:** For spoken stories use F01: before is the ordinary world, the turn is the call, threat or disruption, the middle is a cause-and-effect chain, the after is the return changed. For crafted campaigns and longer pieces use F03 with the customer as hero. In monster plots, name the threat precisely (F34).
**Sources:** Livesay; Edwards; Callahan

## S02 · Everyday anecdote and topic story
**Purpose:** Make one point in conversation and show the teller as a person. **Audience:** Colleagues, clients and teams in meetings. **Teller:** Anyone, especially leaders.
**Choose when:** You have under a minute, the setting is conversational, or a recurring question comes up. This is most business storytelling. Keep a stock indexed by the topics you are often asked about, such as trust, mistakes, delegation or customer service (Callahan), in a story bank (T019). Do not add cinematic plotting or a performer's voice; at work that feels manipulative (Callahan).
**Shape:** F01 sized for 30–90 seconds: state the point (T006), tell one before and one after around a single watershed, include one detail people will remember (T102), stop and link back (T008). Check against the F02 minimum.
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan

## S03 · Story intimacy levels
**Purpose:** Decide how personal a story may be for a given room. **Audience:** Any workplace audience. **Teller:** Anyone about to share a personal story.
**Choose when:** Before telling any tragedy, transition or confession. Three levels: front porch (safe for anyone, any time) for public settings; kitchen (inner circle) only with a small trusted group; bedroom (too intimate) never at work, kept as a private source of insight. If a story still makes you cry or sounds self-indulgent, tell a milder struggle instead (Guptara).
**Shape:** Not a structure. Apply it as a filter when choosing from F20 and before S07, S12 or S18 confessions; then run T107.
**Sources:** Callahan; Guptara

## S04 · Data presentation purposes
**Purpose:** Fix what a data presentation must achieve before building it. **Audience:** Decision-makers and generalists. **Teller:** Analysts, data and research teams.
**Choose when:** You receive a data brief. Three purposes: represent (what a team does, framed by the capabilities that add value for this audience), report (walk through a dataset anchored on its single most significant fact), recommend (propose an action). Ask the requester whether they expect a recommendation, because most represent and report briefs quietly do (Edwards).
**Shape:** Represent: F12 with each capability shown in one user's day. Report: F23 to isolate the fact, then F07 with that fact as the headline (T029). Recommend: F04, or F05 if the audience already accepts the problem, organized as an F10 pyramid; wrap with F24 for mixed rooms.
**Sources:** Edwards

## S05 · Data plots: a journey or a stranger comes to town
**Purpose:** Give a dataset a plot so the numbers read as events. **Audience:** Boards, management, project sponsors. **Teller:** Analysts, project leads.
**Choose when:** Journey: the company tried an initiative, product or process and the data reports what happened along the way. Stranger: an outside disruption (competitor, technology, economic shift) arrived and the data shows its impact and the response.
**Shape:** Journey after a test: F06 (hypotheses, results, ask to scale); otherwise F01 with baseline metrics as before, launch as turn, results as after. Stranger: F04, with the situation as pre-disruption drivers, the complication as the disruption's hit on those same drivers, and the resolution as the response. Make one figure human with T045 and let the room react with T044.
**Sources:** Edwards

## S06 · Study story
**Purpose:** Make a research statistic memorable and credible. **Audience:** Skeptical or analytical listeners. **Teller:** Anyone citing research.
**Choose when:** Your argument rests on an external experiment or survey that a bare number would not carry.
**Shape:** F01: before is the setup (who took part, where, what they were asked to do) in concrete images (T101); the turn is the surprising result; the after is what it means for this audience. Name the researcher and institution so their authority reflects on the point (Callahan).
**Sources:** Guptara; Callahan

## S07 · Who-I-am and signature story
**Purpose:** Earn trust by showing character before trying to influence. **Audience:** New prospects, interviewers, new teams, stakeholders. **Teller:** The individual: seller, leader, candidate, consultant.
**Choose when:** A relationship, pitch, interview or new role starts, or someone asks you to talk about yourself. Prefer a flaw, struggle or mentor over a credential, since lessons build more trust than achievements (Simmons). Answer why you by showing where your passion started (Livesay), and show how you resemble the audience (Callahan).
**Shape:** F01 built by pulse points (T004). Mine mentors, challenges or a book or film that moved you, then audit for intimacy (S03) and self-promotion and verify each detail (Forman). For an interview self-introduction, map the career onto F12: shared ground, challenges and false starts made universal, lessons generalized, mutual benefit at the end (Edwards). Keep several lengths (T015). It is the first answer in F19 and the ethos proof in F38.
**Sources:** Simmons; Edwards; Livesay; Guptara; Callahan; Forman

## S08 · Why-I-am-here story
**Purpose:** Stop listeners guessing at a hidden agenda. **Audience:** Anyone you will ask for money, time or change. **Teller:** Sellers, consultants, change agents, fundraisers.
**Choose when:** The audience may suspect your motive. Two routes: state your stake openly, or show a deeper motive through a concrete sacrifice you made; find that motive by asking why repeatedly (T021) (Guptara).
**Shape:** Short F01 placed early, right after S07, in the F19 sequence. Before is what you could have done instead, the turn is the moment you chose this, the after is what you are giving up or gaining.
**Sources:** Simmons; Guptara

## S09 · Customer success story
**Purpose:** Let prospects conclude for themselves that it will work for them. **Audience:** Prospects and customers. **Teller:** Sellers and marketers; or the customer in their own voice (Hall).
**Choose when:** The buyer's question is whether it will work for them, after trust and liking are in place (F19 order). Also to make an intangible service concrete with names and dates (Simmons), or as the benefit story in a sales call (Dolan).
**Shape:** F01 with a named customer like the listener as hero (T105); the problem is the villain, the product only the tool that appears at the turn (T024) (Hall). Guptara's beats: what the client wanted, the problem, meeting a guide, a plan, failure avoided, success inside and out. Show feelings through moments (T100) and leave the appeal unspoken (T008) (Callahan). To revive a flat chronological case study, find the mid-project curveball, the deadline rescue and the cost of failure (Livesay). The outcome is known, so put the effort into passion, hero and antagonist (Maxwell; F02). Collect raw material with F18. If the prospect replies with their own problem, stop telling and start planning (Guptara). Fails when told from the company's viewpoint.
**Sources:** Hall; Livesay; Guptara; Callahan; Dolan; Simmons; Maxwell

## S10 · Scenario story along the customer's journey
**Purpose:** Sell an integrated solution instead of a single device. **Audience:** Institutional buyers (hospitals, campuses, facility managers). **Teller:** Solutions sales teams, co-creating with the customer.
**Choose when:** The offer spans many touchpoints of an end user's journey or space. Do not rely on old product archives when the solutions strategy breaks with the past; build fresh material by listening (Forman).
**Shape:** First map the end user's full journey and listen with the buyer for where it hurts (F17). Then F01 following one person through it: before is the pain at each stage, the turn is the integrated solution arriving, the after is that person's changed experience followed by the supporting data (F24, T045).
**Sources:** Forman

## S11 · Objection pre-empt story
**Purpose:** Dissolve a doubt before it hardens into a no. **Audience:** Skeptical prospects, resistant teams, groups hearing a rumour. **Teller:** Sellers, leaders.
**Choose when:** You can predict the doubt (price, risk, a past failure, a rumour). Direct route: say the doubt aloud early, then tell the story of someone who held the same fear and was proved wrong (Simmons; Dolan; Callahan). Indirect route for sensitive rumours: never name it; praise the listener, then tell a parallel story (Guptara). For misinformation, replace the false story with a true one that is just as coherent (Callahan).
**Shape:** F32 order: name the doubt, tell the story, then make the case. In live conversation use F35. Voice the flaws of your own proposal with the workaround (T073).
**Sources:** Simmons; Guptara; Dolan; Callahan

## S12 · Lesson and teaching story
**Purpose:** Pass on a lesson or a skill without lecturing. **Audience:** Teams, new hires, peers, guarded rooms. **Teller:** Leaders, experts, trainers.
**Choose when:** You want to prevent a repeat mistake, teach judgment where rules run out, or move a room that numbers left cold (Parikh). Before refusing a request, tell the disaster that the refusal protects them from (Simmons).
**Shape:** F01: before is what you believed or did, the turn is the mistake and its consequence, the after is what you do now; land with T008. For teaching, keep realistic characters, choices and consequences so learners reason for themselves. Choose the tragedy from F20 and check S03.
**Sources:** Callahan; Simmons; Parikh

## S13 · Parables, fables and analogies
**Purpose:** Map an unfamiliar or sensitive problem onto something familiar so the moral lands sideways. **Audience:** Non-specialists, or people who cannot be told directly. **Teller:** Leaders, consultants, technical experts.
**Choose when:** A concept is technical (map it onto a household routine, Parikh) or a direct message would cost someone face (T084). Use fresh tales sparingly and flag unverified ones as probably apocryphal (Callahan).
**Shape:** State what it is about (T006), tell the tale in F01 beats, then add one sentence mapping it back to the business case (T008).
**Sources:** Callahan; Forman; Parikh

## S14 · Self-narrative and sense-making narrative
**Purpose:** Change behaviour by changing the overarching story a person or organization tells about itself. **Audience:** Oneself, a coaching client, an organization in change. **Teller:** The individual, a coach, leaders.
**Choose when:** A victim script blocks action, a hard decision looms, or someone has lost heart.
**Shape:** Write the current script, check it against facts, and rewrite it around the value you give (Guptara). Before a hard choice, ask how an admired fictional figure would act (Guptara). For a disillusioned person, have them picture an admired mentor handing them the torch (Simmons). Use the stories you tell yourself before sleep to rehearse tomorrow (Maxwell). For an organization, F08 or F09 supplies the shared frame.
**Sources:** Guptara; Maxwell; Simmons

## S15 · Bad-news presentation
**Purpose:** Present red findings to senior people without blame and turn the room to solutions. **Audience:** Senior stakeholders, steering and audit committees. **Teller:** Project leads, auditors.
**Choose when:** Status is red or an audit found failures.
**Shape:** Facilitate neutrally, without pointing fingers, and pivot the room to solutions (T083, T065). Keep blame and victim framing out (S23).
**Sources:** Parikh

## S16 · Founder and origin story
**Purpose:** Show what the organization stands for and why it differs from copycats. **Audience:** Investors, customers, recruits, new and merged employees. **Teller:** The founder; employees retell it in the third person.
**Choose when:** Fundraising, a crowded market, onboarding, or sustaining pride through a merger (Forman).
**Shape:** F01, or F03 told as a quest. Before is the founder's roots, often in childhood; the turn is the moment of frustration that there must be a better way; the middle is the hard early years with sacrifice and failure; the after is what the company now does. Emphasize grit for investors, the unmet need for customers and the mission for recruits. Interview and ghostwrite for modest founders (Hall). Mine with F16; draw on archives and founder interviews (Forman).
**Sources:** Hall; Edwards; Callahan; Forman

## S17 · Purpose and engagement story
**Purpose:** Align a team around a goal, a course correction or the reason the work matters. **Audience:** Employees and teams. **Teller:** Leaders and managers.
**Choose when:** Launching a goal, making a sensitive correction, reviving motivation, or narrowing focus. Find it by asking when you learned this lesson the hard way, often outside work (Hall). Variants: purpose (the work helps a real end user), progress (small daily wins), trust (the leader puts the team before self-interest), focus (one priority shown in frontline action) (Callahan).
**Shape:** F01: the leader's personal before, turn and after mirroring the point, then a bridge to the team (T008). End on the change the group itself must produce (Maxwell). For the purpose variant, show one end user (T045).
**Sources:** Hall; Callahan; Maxwell

## S18 · Values story
**Purpose:** Make an abstract value concrete and believable. **Audience:** Employees, new prospects, stakeholders. **Teller:** Leaders, employees, sellers.
**Choose when:** Launching or refreshing values, onboarding, or proving trustworthiness to a new prospect (Dolan).
**Shape:** F01 centred on a fork in the road (Edwards): before is the situation, the turn is the choice where keeping the value cost money or broke a rule, the after is the consequence and what it proved. A confession of a time you failed a value earns more credibility than a perfect record (Dolan). Draw on tension stories in F20; spread them with F40.
**Sources:** Simmons; Edwards; Callahan; Dolan

## S19 · Vision story
**Purpose:** Make the future concrete and worth today's sacrifices. **Audience:** Teams, investors, partners. **Teller:** Leaders, founders.
**Choose when:** Asking for sustained effort or sacrifice, or launching a strategy. Prefer a real pocket where the future already happens (Callahan); otherwise tell a clearly labelled imagined scenario. A mission story is the rare case where the organization may be the hero (Edwards).
**Shape:** A sensory scene of one person in the future (T101, T045), tied back to present frustrations. Place it as the future step of F08 or the payoff chapter of F09; invite the buyer or team into it with T077.
**Sources:** Simmons; Edwards; Callahan

## S20 · Culture hero stories
**Purpose:** Show what the culture values and invite imitation. **Audience:** Employees, new hires, acquired staff. **Teller:** The frontline employee who lived it; managers who recirculate it.
**Choose when:** Reinforcing service culture, onboarding, or unifying people after an acquisition. Organizations also retell founding stories, disasters averted, past triumphs and changes of direction (Callahan); fill gaps among these.
**Shape:** F01 with an ordinary employee as hero: before is a routine shift, the turn is an unexpected customer or colleague problem, the after is what they did beyond the job and how it ended. Let the person tell it (T023). The routines that collect and recirculate these stories are F40.
**Sources:** Forman; Maxwell; Callahan

## S21 · Product backstory and aspirational identity
**Purpose:** Raise perceived value far above cost and escape price competition. **Audience:** Buyers and consumers. **Teller:** Marketers, product owners, sellers.
**Choose when:** The product is treated as a commodity or you need premium price tiers. Three angles: what goes into it (craft, time, origin), what it changes in people's lives, and who the buyer becomes. Reframe a mundane service around its emotional end result (Livesay).
**Shape:** Backstory: F01 from raw material to finished product. Identity: F03 with the buyer as hero and the product as mentor's gift; match imagery to the drive it serves (T089).
**Sources:** Guptara; Livesay

## S22 · Logo versus lifestyle brand story
**Purpose:** Choose how a brand tells its story. **Audience:** Customers and employees. **Teller:** A trusted spokesperson (logo) or employees and customers (lifestyle).
**Choose when:** Logo: the promise is reliability and consistency, vouched for by a spokesperson with moral authority. Lifestyle: the brand is a community that people live and co-author.
**Shape:** Logo: an F39 frame with reliability stories told by the spokesperson. Lifestyle: F40 and F43 with S09 stories in customers' own voices; create shared experiences with T093 and T094.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## S23 · Destructive narratives
**Purpose:** Detect and replace stories that damage trust. **Audience:** Reviewers and leaders. **Teller:** Not to be told.
**Choose when:** A draft casts others as villains and the teller as powerless, or presents an image that contradicts what the audience can see; or an organization circulates complaint stories.
**Shape:** Diagnose who is cast as villain and who as helpless. Replace with a true story where the teller acts (S12, S18); correct spin by moving the account toward what people can observe; counter rumours with S11. Run T107 before release.
**Sources:** Simmons

## Selection rules

| Goal | Story type | Framework |
|---|---|---|
| Sell | S07, then S08, then S09 (S21 for commodities) | F19 order; F01; F12 for a full pitch |
| Raise money | S16, S19, S09 as traction | F14; F19 |
| Align a team | S17; S19 for a long horizon | F01; F08 for a strategy change |
| Recruit | S16 told in third person, S20, S18 | F01; F40 |
| Build credibility | S07, S12, S18 confession | F01; F20; F38 |
| Explain data | S04 first, then S05 or S06 | F23; F07 or F04; F24 |
| Handle an objection | S11 | F35 live; F32 in a prepared talk |
| Report a pilot | S05 journey | F06 |
| Deliver bad news | S15 | F04 |
| Refuse a request | S12 told before the no | F32 |
