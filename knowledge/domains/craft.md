# Craft

## When to use story in craft
Use this file when the work is the story itself: plotting, character, scene, dialogue, voice, endings, revision, and preparing a story to be told aloud. It serves fiction, memoir, personal tales for a stage or table, scripts, and any piece whose quality depends on how it is built. For business, sales, marketing or data uses, read that domain's file and return here for scene-level and sentence-level fixes. Reach for craft when a draft is flat, episodic, overexplained, preachy, or ends badly, or when the teller needs to find, shape and deliver a true story.

## Principles
- A story is a character who wants something, meets opposition and is changed; an account of events with no goal or jeopardy stays an incident. Evidence: author experience, workshops. (Haven; Hauge; Edwards; Biesenbach)
- Conflict is internal first: an outer crisis matters only when the character feels it threatens what they care about, and the gap between inner need and outer want makes people believable. Evidence: author experience, film analysis. (Cleland; Simson; Hauge)
- Beats linked by but or therefore make momentum; beats linked by and make a list. Evidence: author experience with students, screen examples. (Rubin; Dicks; Simson; Aristotle)
- One action, one point and one genre promise per story. Bundling several plots, themes or lives dilutes each. Evidence: author experience, classical analysis, box-office anecdotes. (Aristotle; D'Costa; Leitman; Barker & Brown)
- Brains register change and information gaps; opening on change or a puzzle and withholding part of the answer sustains attention. Evidence: study, editorial experience. (Storr; Barker & Brown)
- Readers want suspense more than surprise: surprise ends in an instant, anticipation of a coming blow sustains pages. Evidence: author craft experience. (Cleland; Dicks)
- A reversal satisfies when earlier events caused it and a re-read finds clues; coincidence is acceptable only when it creates trouble early, never when it rescues late. Evidence: classical analysis, author experience. (Aristotle; Cleland; Barker & Brown; Gottschall)
- Risk, loss and an inescapable crucible create tension; a hero rises only as high as the opposition, and unbroken level-ten intensity numbs readers. Evidence: structural analysis, workshops. (Haven; Rubin)
- Specific, sensory detail makes stories feel true and lets strangers recognize themselves; generic setting and statistics distance them. Evidence: study, author experience, literary analysis. (Storr; Rubin; Karia; Hauge; Parikh; Leitman)
- Concrete images carry emotion and meaning; abstract labels and stacked adjectives give the mind nothing to simulate. Evidence: study, author experience. (Storr; Guptara; Maguire; Karia)
- Seeing precedes feeling: audiences cannot care about a character until they can picture the scene. Evidence: practitioner testimony. (James)
- Meaning lands harder when the audience infers it; stated morals, repeated theses and over-explained emotions raise resistance. Evidence: meta-analyses, author experience. (Gottschall; Rubin; Schwabauer; Maguire; Leitman)
- Memory keeps peaks and endings, and tellers are most effective when they stop at the peak instead of tying every loose end. Evidence: cited research, author experience. (Paulsen; Sandler; Dicks; Buster)
- Pity and fear attach to a decent, imperfect person undone by error; heroes with no flaw or antagonists with no reason lose belief. Evidence: classical analysis, author experience. (Aristotle; Barker & Brown; Storr; Emunds)
- A character with a specific flaw, a false belief and a want that cannot be met without facing it generates plot from the inside. Evidence: author experience, neuroscience citations. (Storr; Haven; Cleland)
- A teller who is vulnerable and credits others wins the room; bragging, and name-dropping, repel it. Evidence: author experience across thousands of stage stories. (Bowles; Leitman; James; Dicks; Hauge)
- Audiences sense fabrication, so invented events or flattering edits cost more trust than a plain true detail earns. Evidence: author experience, poll. (Leitman; Snow; Terenzzo; Forman)
- Spoken and written stories obey different rules: listeners cannot reread, so spoken pieces need simple time, space and cast, and memorizing a script produces blanks and stiffness. Evidence: author experience, workshops. (Dicks; Bowles; Haven; Maguire; James)
- Told stories are retained as gist and image, not words; conviction and enjoyment convince more than verbatim accuracy. Evidence: author experiments. (Haven; Miller)
- Retelling edits memory, so honest emotional truth matters more than literal accuracy, but tellers should not claim facts they cannot check. Evidence: expert testimony, author experience. (Bowles; Dicks; Biesenbach)
- Fear of feeling blocks writing, resistance grows with the importance of the subject, and the inner editor should work after the draft rather than during it. Evidence: author experience. (Rubin; Cleland; Emunds; Apps)
- Writing a story down or telling it aloud surfaces meaning not seen before; handwriting and speaking aloud free associations more than typing. Evidence: author experience, study. (Apps; Edwards; Dicks; Haven)
- Plain words reach further than jargon; simple diction speeds readers along and jargon signals exclusion even when defined. Evidence: study. (Snow; Lazer; Guptara)
- Genre promises matter: readers choose a genre for its core emotion and its payoffs, and drifting off them breaks trust. Evidence: author experience, practitioner testimony. (Coyne; D'Costa; Emunds)
- Craft is learned by doing, over many stories and drafts; one book or one draft does not give mastery. Evidence: author experience. (Coyne; Emunds; Haven)
- Action outweighs speech and static traits: plotless character speeches fail while plotted pieces with plain speeches succeed, and a tight arc beats the same story stretched. Evidence: classical analysis. (Aristotle)
- Immersion comes before caring: sensory grounding and one clear viewpoint pull readers in, and every scene, trait and subplot should interlock so removing one threatens the whole. Evidence: editorial experience. (Barker & Brown; Aristotle)
- A first-person narrator who lived the event is trusted more than a distant spokesperson, and an ordinary eyewitness moves audiences faster than an expert summary. Evidence: practitioner testimony, theory. (Forman; Apps)
- Emotion makes plot intelligible: pick the single central emotion of the genre and build it scene after scene toward a climax where need, value and emotion peak together. Evidence: editorial experience. (Coyne; Emunds)
- Universal story grammar recurs across cultures: a flawed hero in trouble facing opposition, with poetic justice in the end; experiments that drop it lose readers. Evidence: literary and quantitative surveys. (Gottschall; Aristotle)
- Short, comma-light sentences speed a climax, and a brief reflective sequel after a hard scene restores motivation and receptivity. Evidence: counting test, craft analysis. (Haven; Emunds)
- Mechanical slips and tense switches pull readers out of the world, so pick one tense, proofread, and place stimulus before reaction. Evidence: author experience. (Hauge; Emunds)

## Mistakes
- Passive or routine opening, throat-clearing, neutral biography, or a thesis announced up front → open inside a scene or on a change, with the hero's want and a jolt. (F008, F049, T049, T005, T615) (Rubin; Dicks; Storr; Barker & Brown; Karia)
- Episodic "and then" plotting → test every transition for but or therefore and cut unmotivated links. (S188, F004, F002, T001) (Rubin; Aristotle; Dicks; Simson; Storr; Barker & Brown; Biesenbach)
- Falling risks, sagging or lecturing middle → order risks from low to high and stack stakes. (F011, F062, F092) (Rubin; Haven)
- Unearned twist, dream ending, rescue by luck or outside force → plant setups early, let the protagonist's own choice resolve the climax, and test fairness on a re-read. (F063, F104, F010, T058, T060, S026) (Rubin; Cleland; Aristotle; Gottschall; Schwabauer)
- Reciting information in dialogue, infodumping backstory → give each line a speaker need, put backstory inside active scenes, relocate it and see if it can go. (F015, T012, T366, T368, T095, T616) (Rubin; Barker & Brown; Emunds)
- Straw-man villain or flawless hero → give both talents and flaws; treat the antagonist as the hero of their own cause. (F076, F090, T039, T020, T019) (Rubin; Barker & Brown; Emunds; Storr; Gottschall)
- Stating the moral or "then I realized" → stop at the peak and show the change through action or contrast. (T388, F042, F047) (Dicks; Leitman; Apps; Buster; Maguire; Schwabauer)
- Slogan theme preached to the audience → argue a debatable stance through a clash of principles. (F083, S189, F033) (Rubin; Schwabauer; D'Costa; Gottschall)
- Vague goal and passive protagonist → set a specific, checkable goal tied to an inner need and force hard choices. (F006, F037, F058) (Barker & Brown; Haven; Storr; Hauge)
- Too many characters, details or points → cut anything that does not serve one point and use a one-sentence core as the filter. (F142, T631, T610, T221) (Dicks; Leitman; Bowles; Apps; Guptara)
- Head hopping, filter words and generic gestures → one point of view per scene, cut perception verbs, express emotion through how a character does a task. (F071, F193, T637) (Barker & Brown; Emunds)
- Chronicling a life, a period or an epic as one plot → build around one action and fold the rest in as subordinate episodes. (F002, F055) (Aristotle; Apps; D'Costa)
- Over-explaining motives, mystery or emotion → cut explanation to leave room for the audience to project. (T074, T638, F016) (Rubin; Storr; Hauge; Maguire)
- Stacked adjectives, abstract labels and clichés → one precise detail or action, fresh physical metaphors, and plain words. (T221, T076, T089, T343, F194) (Guptara; Karia; Storr; Maguire; Aristotle)
- Informational backstory or travelogue opening → start at the first essential scene and give context only when needed. (T095, T616, F016) (Cleland; Haven; Apps; Barker & Brown)
- New business in the last act, extra subplots, detached subplots → mine Act One for threads and limit to two subplots that feed the main plot. (F041, F055, F039) (Barker & Brown; Cleland; D'Costa)
- Several genres or off-genre climax → lock one genre or a deliberate hybrid, deliver its payoffs in a fresh way, and keep tone consistent. (F117, F077, T025) (D'Costa; Coyne; Emunds)
- Punch line or pun as the ending, or funny word early → save laughs for the open and middle and put the key word last. (T640, F042) (Dicks; Leitman)
- Forced jokes, bragging, or using others as props → let humour come from true detail, cut gags that do not serve the arc, own your part first. (T613, T462) (Bowles; Leitman; Dicks; James)
- Tying everything neatly, epilogue after the climax, or a forced full circle → stop on the real change and cut lingering. (F010, S034, T648) (Dicks; Bowles; Leitman; Haven; Buster)
- Raw wounds told as trauma or therapy → wait until it is a scar, test aloud, then focus on your agency. (F143, F122) (Bowles; Leitman; James; Biesenbach)
- Inventing or flattering events → trust real life, change only what the permitted edits allow, and verify facts later. (F156, F157) (Leitman; Maguire; Apps; Biesenbach)
- Writing a birth-to-present summary or a list of examples → pick one episode and zoom in on one moment. (F061, F106, T077, F144) (Apps; Dicks; Leitman)
- Word-for-word memorization or winging it with no draft → draft, reduce to beats, memorize only first and last lines. (F181, F089, T320, T321) (Bowles; Dicks; Leitman; Miller; Maguire; Haven; Parikh; James)
- Memory slip treated as disaster, or apologizing before and after → pause, smile, bridge, and leave the story intact. (F182, T310) (Haven; Maguire; Miller)
- Rushed, thin or theatrical voice → vary rate, pitch and volume naturally and speak in the conversational register. (T311, T312, T353) (Maguire; Haven; Bowles)
- Mirror rehearsal or crowd feedback from relatives and many readers → practise before real listeners and ask one or two trusted people. (F181) (Haven; Dicks; Leitman; Apps; James)
- Editing while drafting or confusing copyediting with revision → draft first, rest it, cut structure before grammar. (F102, F132, F057, T648) (Cleland; Emunds; Apps; Haven; Miller)
- Overusing the gleaming detail or foreshadowing → keep one or two, each paying off. (F047, T220, T655) (Buster; Cleland)
- Writing with the first idea or without knowing the ending → lock concept, character, ending and moral before drafting. (F026, F046, F102) (Terenzzo; Haven)
- Telling someone else's story as your own → reframe around your own relationship to it, ask permission and credit them. (S066, F122) (Dicks; James)
- Polishing prose, slides or technology before the story works → settle the premise in one sentence and test it before investing in the telling. (F026, T631) (Terenzzo; Paulsen)
- Reaction written before stimulus, tense switches, typos → show the cause first, hold one tense and proofread. (T637, T638) (Emunds; Hauge)
- Settings left as fog or generic backdrops → anchor each scene early with a few concrete sensory details and a time stamp. (T075, T076) (Schwabauer; Simson; Sandler)
- Writing activity instead of action, with nothing opposing the goal → cut beats where nothing resists and add a clashing agenda. (F056, F023, T610) (Emunds; Simson; Hauge)
- Easy wins and one-sided conflict → pair flaws with high-risk problems and raise the antagonist until defeat looks likely. (F011, F037, S008) (Haven; Rubin; Emunds)
- Omniscient or second-person narration by default → choose close third or first for intimacy. (F071, F193) (Haven; Emunds)
- Plain stories without shape: events reported with no voice, setting or meaning → add voice, stakes and a change. (S058, F006, F091) (Haven; Apps; Edwards; Biesenbach)

## Formats
### Short personal story told aloud (three to five minutes)
S066 or S068. Mine a turning point with F122, F126 or F129 and screen it with F143 and F144. Place the moment of change with F061 and open inside a scene with T049. Link beats by but and therefore (F004) and keep one point (F142). Rehearse as beats (F181, T321), memorize only first and last lines (T320), stop at the peak (T388).

### Short story or chapter in fiction
S022 with S012 or S010. Plot with F002, F021 or F039, open with F008, escalate with F011 and F062, and give the crisis a decision then confirmation (F010). Alternate scenes and sequels (F023). Show with T074, T075, T076, and pull readers forward with T051.

### Character and scene dialogue
S008 for opposition types and S007 for dilemmas. Build the person with F058, F103, F093 and a flaw via F090 or F025. Write conflict with F015, T012, T366 and T368, and keep turns short (T641). Test the antagonist's sympathy with T020 and T019.

### Twist and ending design
S002, S057 and S026. Choose the ending with F042, plan backwards with F046, plant clues using F063 and F104, test fairness with T060, and limit foreshadowing with T655. Land on a controlling image (F067) and stop.

### Genre and structure exemplar study
S038, S039, S040. Study titles with F117, map their beats with F056, set need, value and emotion with F077, and use S013 only as checkpoints. Fit tone or twist conventions with T025.

### Revision pass on a flat draft
S058. Card every scene (F057), analyse beats (F056), cut pointless scenes (T610) and dull stretches (T648), delete weak jokes (T613), run the cliché filter (T215), write the opening last (T615), and read aloud.
