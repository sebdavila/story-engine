# Business

## When to use story in business
- The audience must decide, change behaviour or back a plan, and a list of reasons would invite counter-arguments. Open with a story, then give the logic inside it (F255, F278).
- A strategy, change or new idea must be understood and retold by people who were not in the room (F239, F240, F266).
- Trust has to exist before a sale, hire or investment: who you are, why the company exists, what a customer gained (S156, S120, F228).
- A data point or finding must move people, not only inform them (T258, T259, F159).
- Skip story when the listener is short of time and needs a plain factual answer; lead with the fact and keep stories for moments when people are open to learning (Callahan).
- Skip story when the piece is a compliance notice or a status report whose sections are independent; use S091 and keep any story to one short example.
- A business story needs an outcome for the audience, not only for the teller: decide what they should think, feel or do first (F274, F284, T527).

## Principles
- A forceful list of reasons triggers reactance and counter-arguing; narrative transport lowers the guard, and leading with a story sidesteps confirmation bias. Evidence: study (Brehm; Green and Brock). F255, F265. (Edwards; Hall; Quesenberry; Callahan; Simmons)
- Decisions are made on feeling and justified afterwards; patients with damaged emotion centres cannot choose. Evidence: study (Damasio). F261, F246. (Edwards; Callahan; James; Hauge; Butler; Biesenbach; Maestas)
- Emotional campaigns beat purely rational ones on profit effects, and combining both beats rational alone. Evidence: study (analysis of 400 campaigns, 31 versus 16 percent). F246. (Quesenberry; Dolan)
- Ads with a complete dramatic arc outperform humour, sex appeal, animals and celebrities for liking and sharing; four to five acts earned about four times the shares of zero to three acts. Evidence: study (Quesenberry and Coolsen). (Quesenberry; Edwards; Hall; Creek; Snow)
- Facts set inside a story are remembered far better than bare figures; cited multiples run from 2x to 22x depending on the source, so treat them as direction, not a constant. Evidence: study (as cited). T259, T498. (Callahan; Edwards; Maxwell; Kamath; Bowles; Rodriguez)
- A listener's brain activity mirrors the teller's, and listeners who anticipate what comes next understand best. Evidence: study (Hasson). F265. (Callahan; Hall; Bowles; Guptara; James)
- Tension in a story raises cortisol (attention) and oxytocin (empathy, generosity). Evidence: study (Zak). F252, T086. (Quesenberry; Hall; Snow; Lazauskas; Dolan; Parikh)
- Stating the point before the story roughly doubles recall and comprehension, and a known ending can ease processing of complex material. Evidence: study (Leavitt and Christenfeld, via Edwards; Callahan's cited work). T028, T388. (Callahan; Edwards)
- Buyers judge in order: do I trust you, do I like you, will it work for me. A tenth of a second shapes the trust judgement. Evidence: study (cited by Livesay). F228. (Livesay; Guptara; Simmons)
- One named person beats a statistic: donors give more to an individual, and big numbers numb. Evidence: study (cited by Snow; Parikh). T258, T464, T465. (Snow; Lazauskas; Parikh; Kamath; Biesenbach; Hauge)
- Specific detail builds belief and universality; vague goals and summarised emotion keep the audience outside the scene. Evidence: author experience. T221, T080, T081. (Hauge; Simmons; Karia; James; Biesenbach)
- Admitting flaws before the audience finds them raises credibility; a small dose of negative detail makes a recommendation more believable. Evidence: study (blemishing effect, via Parikh). T462, F329, T510. (Parikh; Hauge; Kamath; Maestas; Cody; Dolan)
- Lies, spin and contradiction between story and conduct are punished; consistent core values over time build trust, and brand consistency has been tied to revenue gains. Evidence: industry studies and cases. F363, F324. (Terenzzo; Snow; Forman; Dolan; Rodriguez; Maestas)
- Purpose stories drive output: telling workers how their work helps end users more than doubled call-centre results compared with pay reminders; purpose-led sellers outsell quota-driven ones. Evidence: study (Grant; McLeod). S159, S164. (Callahan; Quesenberry; Hall; Kamath)
- A strategy that is not turned into a story does not reach the frontline; without an explanation employees invent rumours. Evidence: study (one firm where 97 percent of leaders but 25 percent of managers could state the strategy) and anecdote. F239, F240. (Guptara; Callahan; Forman; Salmon)
- Giving a reason lifts compliance sharply (60 to over 93 percent in a photocopier study), and people who help build an idea own it. Evidence: study (Langer; endowment effect). F376, T468. (Callahan; Guptara; Edwards; McKinsey)
- Hard-to-read material is judged hard to do; fewer ideas per slide, sentence headlines and a few visual chunks cut effort. Evidence: study (cited by Edwards; Parikh; McKinsey). T382, T103, T290. (Edwards; McKinsey; Parikh; Kamath; Guptara)
- The customer is the hero and the brand is the guide; centring the product or company leaves buyers indifferent. Evidence: author experience. F288, F275, T589. (Hall; Butler; Guptara; Hauge; Maestas; Creek)
- Know the audience beyond demographics: motives, fears, objections and buying style decide what lands. Evidence: author experience. F158, F229, F274. (Butler; Livesay; Parikh; Feigenbaum)
- Platforms reward engagement, not asks; people share to signal who they are, so content must fit each platform and carry a high-arousal emotion. Evidence: platform observation and study (cited by Creek). F191, F251. (Vaynerchuk; Creek; Rodriguez; Snow)
- Sagas that keep characters and add real progress hold their value, while repeats of old plots fall 20 to 40 percent. Evidence: study (Snow and Lazauskas, 600 sequels). S176, S175. (Snow; Lazauskas; Paulsen)
- Top sellers pause longer, keep their pace and ask a question after an objection; silence after the ask prevents self-negotiation. Evidence: study (67,149 recorded calls, via Livesay). F232, T475. (Livesay)
- Humour and psychological safety help teams share failures and accept change; levity must fit the room. Evidence: study (cited by Cody) and author experience. F331. (Cody; Parikh; Callahan)
- Retelling alters memory and tends to inflate the teller's role; write stories down early. Evidence: author experience and expert testimony. T647. (Biesenbach; Bowles; Dicks)
- Writing by hand and drafting before using AI produce fresher ideas, and labelled AI authorship lowers ratings. Evidence: study (cited by Edwards; Lazer). F372, T603, T573. (Edwards; Lazer; Dicks; Lo Duca)
- Spoken stories follow different rules from prose: sequence and rehearsed beats beat memorized text. Evidence: author experience. F181, T320, T321. (Bowles; Dicks; Maguire; Callahan; Leitman)
- Facts and charts need a point and an audience: exploratory charts do not persuade, so cut to one insight and add context and an action. Evidence: author experience and case. F159, T266, T244. (Lo Duca; McKinsey; Feigenbaum; Parikh)

- Investors back the jockey as well as the horse: founders who pitch with energy and a story win more often, and visible passion signals staying power. Evidence: study (academic work cited by Livesay) and author experience. F272, F235. (Livesay; Hall)
- Negative stories travel faster and weigh more than positive ones, so an organisation needs a steady supply of positive stories about its own people. Evidence: study (cited by Callahan). F316, T572. (Callahan)
- People copy visible behaviour, so a short story showing one concrete act moves conduct where a mandate fails. Evidence: study (imitation research, via Callahan). T118, T511. (Callahan; Guptara)
- B2B buyers remain emotional: they hire teams whose values and character fit theirs, so shared-values stories earn trust beyond the transaction. Evidence: author experience. S165, T082. (Biesenbach; Guptara; Livesay)
- Facts presented before a story get slotted into the listener's old story; a story first supplies the frame in which facts are read. Evidence: author experience. F255, T259. (Simmons; Callahan)

## Mistakes
- Persuading with a stack of reasons → open on the problem and its human cost, then bring facts as support (F255, T259, T498). (Edwards; Mastropierro)
- Company, product, analyst or data as hero → one named customer or affected person is the hero and the offer the tool (F288, T082, T589, F162). (Hall; Butler; Guptara; Feigenbaum)
- Starting from what we want to say → start from what the audience should do, think or feel (T527, F274, F284). (Edwards; Parikh; Terenzzo)
- Calling timelines, mission lines, testimonials or fact sheets a story → check for a time and place, a character acting, conflict, cause and a point (F339, T218). (Callahan; Hall; Biesenbach; Hauge)
- Several points in one story or slide → one point per story and one message per slide (T218, T382, F142). (Guptara; Kamath; Bowles)
- Explaining the moral at the end → state relevance before the story or stop at the peak (T388, T028, T119). (Callahan; Leitman; Maguire; Dolan)
- Smooth, problem-free case with no setback → add the mid-project crisis and real obstacles (T470, F238, T510). (Edwards; Livesay; Hauge; Callahan; Biesenbach)
- Raw tear-jerkers or unprocessed trauma → test readiness first and keep the rawest material private (F143, T217). (Guptara; Biesenbach; Dolan; Bowles)
- Bragging in triumph stories → shrink the win, credit others, admit a flaw early (F259, T042). (Dolan; Dicks; Hauge; James)
- Opening with agendas, thanks, bios or market tables → open on a story or a day in one customer's life (T387, T464, T423). (Kamath; Karia; Parikh; Dolan; Biesenbach)
- Starting a deck in slide software → outline on paper first, then sequence by depth (T603, F284). (Edwards; McKinsey)
- Executive summary first in a live talk, or ending on open questions → put the answer where tension has been earned and close with a summary (T401, T304, F064). (McKinsey; Parikh)
- Label titles and bullet slides → full-sentence takeaway titles (T103, T290, T382). (Edwards; McKinsey; Parikh; Kamath)
- Dumping every chart, 3D or pie → one insight per chart, headline above, clutter cut (T241, T243, T245, F163). (Edwards; Lo Duca; McKinsey; Parikh)
- Rational-only change memos or emailed slide decks → turn strategy into a short oral story, co-created with leaders (F240, F239, F376). (Callahan; Guptara; Salmon; Lazer)
- Listing all problems then all solutions → alternate a problem with its solution (T496, F235). (Edwards)
- Pressing solution urgency or promising effortless results → prove the problem is getting worse now and name the risks (T488, T462, T461). (Kamath; Hauge)
- Asking for the sale too early → earn trust and liking first, close with a low-pressure next step (F228, F227, T503, T502). (Livesay; Snow)
- Defensive monologue or "good question" at an objection → slow down, pause, ask a clarifying question (F232, T302, T303, T305, T307). (Livesay; Simmons)
- Breaking silence after the ask with discounts → hold calm silence (T475). (Livesay)
- Talking to a buyer type in the wrong register → read cues and adapt the angle (F229). (Livesay; Butler)
- Too little or too much exposition → give only who, where, when and why it matters (T094, T095, T067). (Livesay; Sandler; Cowie; Dolan)
- Outshining senior stakeholders with a finding → frame it as a reminder and ask for their input (T458, T468). (Edwards)
- Treating a pitch as a contest → treat it as the start of a working relationship and listen first (T201, F228). (Edwards; Maxwell)
- Meeting the room only with your own view → interview or listen to the audience before drafting (F115, F119). (Maestas; Butler; Creek)
- Demographic-only targeting and invented personas → use psychographics, interviews and micro surveys (F158, F154, F112). (Butler; Sandler; Creek)
- Responding to a rumour or bias defensively → tell an indirect story that praises specific work (S124, T460, F363). (Guptara; Biesenbach)
- Hiring actors for real customers, or presenting an exception as the rule → use real people and composites declared as such (T535, T195, F363). (Forman; Hall; Rodriguez)
- Retiring or stripping the founder story → keep it alive; it is new to each first listener (S156, F337, T568, T390). (Hall; Biesenbach; Snow)
- Fabricated history or tourism-style myth → separate verified record from invention (T619, T653, F363). (Paulsen; Terenzzo; Forman)
- Sudden social stance at odds with past conduct → audit values alignment and explain the shift live (T539, F363). (Rodriguez; Cody)
- Cross-posting one asset, buying followers or chasing streaks → create natively per platform (F191, T294, T371). (Vaynerchuk; Creek; Rodriguez)
- Right hooks before jabs, several asks per post → give value first and one ask per post (F250, F249, T502). (Vaynerchuk; Quesenberry; Sandler)
- One campaign then silence → budget for continuing stories and sagas (S176, F321, T538). (Maestas; Snow; Rodriguez)
- Goals out of sync between executives and marketing → interview stakeholders and plan backward (F315, F170, F376). (Butler; Quesenberry)
- Counting one vanity metric → set key questions and compare stories on several measures (F170, T284). (Quesenberry; Snow)
- Launching a story without internal believers or training → build the internal program first (F316, F124, T572, F292). (Rodriguez; Snow; Forman)
- Storytelling as a one-person or late-stage task → embed it from kickoff across departments (F316, F324, F302). (Snow; Paulsen; Cowie)
- Memorized scripts or theatrical voice → rehearse beats and speak plainly (F181, T320, T321, T317). (Bowles; Dicks; Maguire; Maxwell; Callahan; Parikh)
- Overloading data, jargon or stock photos → plain language and real protagonists (T317, T535, F163). (Lazer; Sandler; Rodriguez; Snow)
- Outsourcing drafts to AI or hiding AI use → draft yourself, use AI to critique, disclose use (F372, T247, T573). (Lazer; Lo Duca)
- Inventing a story, borrowing someone else's or presenting it as yours → use your own lived events or credit the source plainly (T232, T228). (Dolan; Callahan; Dicks; James)
- Famous parables and celebrity examples (Lincoln, Jobs, the starfish) → choose a peer's fresh story from your own context (T215, T080). (Callahan; Karia; Biesenbach)
- Fear, shame or scare tactics to motivate → pair a realistic risk with a move toward something wanted (T466, F280). (Simmons; Butler)
- Faked humility, straw villains or an antagonist the hero cannot beat → keep claims honest and pick a problem the offer actually solves (F276). (Butler; Maxwell)
- Risky humour: put-downs without consent, canned bits, jokes about protected traits → check the room and keep the joke on yourself (T236). (Cody; Dolan; Leitman)
- Tone-deaf campaigns or ignoring cultural codes → test against category codes, social context and an outside view (F264, F363). (Quesenberry; Rodriguez; Sandler; Cody)
- Leaders whose conduct contradicts their stories → align actions with the stories told about them, since the stories repeated in their absence define them (F363). (Callahan; Forman; Dolan)
- Letting committees pile on edits → cap approvals and protect the story's core (F381, T599). (Biesenbach; Bowles; Kamath)

## Formats
### Customer success story
S120. Use F238 for beats and F288 for casting. Techniques: T080, T081, T195, T470, T487, T257. Keep the supplier as guide, not star; collect with F119.

### Investor or new-idea pitch
S133. Use F235 for slide order, F268 when the audience already knows its problem, F272 for a founder story, F254 for a pattern break that keeps the spine intact. Techniques: T492, T464, T488, T496, T387, T502, T503.

### Strategy or change story
S133, S134, S135. Use F239 (before, change, now, next); F266 gives numbered chapters for multi-year plans, F240 turns the analysis into narrative before rollout, F271 rallies a team. Techniques: T031, T504, T514, T527, T510, T511.

### Decision brief or bad-news presentation
S138, S099, S091. Use F064 or F225; F285 for a deductive chain, F286 for an inductive group. Techniques: T028, T401, T103, T382, T493, T528, T529.

### Founder and origin story
S156, S164. Use F337 for beats and F306 to keep it consistent across the brand. Techniques: T568, T563, T564, T390, T462, T423.

### Failure or lesson story for internal use
S127, S145. Use F019 for a short build and F329 to own failures openly. Techniques: T042, T485, T588, T345, T388.

### Brand and content program
S162, S169, S171, S176. Use F290 for one story across channels, F321 for ongoing value content, F191 for platform fit, F315 to plan backward. Techniques: T589, T538, T371, T282, T284.
