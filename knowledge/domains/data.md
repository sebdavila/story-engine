# Data

## When to use story in data
- A finding must change what a decision maker does, and a chart alone leaves them to guess the meaning. Choose the one insight, then build the story around it (F159, F160, T266).
- A large or abstract number fails to move people. Pair it with one named person, a human-scale comparison or a short study story (T258, T289, S084).
- A research, model or status readout must be understood fast by people with little time. Use a conclusion-first structure with sentence headlines (F172, F064, T103, S091).
- The audience is non-technical, or sceptical of numbers. Wrap the evidence in a story and set the human frame before the statistics (F255, T259, T257).
- The data comes from a community, an institution or a contested source, and how it was made matters as much as what it says (S086, F174, F176, F374).
- Skip story when the reader only needs a lookup of exact values; use a clean precision table (T265). Skip the story frame when sections are independent status items; use S091 and keep any anecdote to one short example.
- Decide first what the audience should think, feel and do after seeing the data, and write that as a gap to close (F274, F348).

## Principles
- Stories carry facts better than lists of facts. Wrapping data in a causal story raises recall many times over; the multiples reported (7 to 12 times, 22 times, 67% against 5% after ten minutes) come from different sources, so treat them as direction rather than a fixed ratio. Evidence: study and author experience. (Edwards; Bowles; Parikh; Quesenberry; Snow)
- Decisions are made on feeling and justified afterwards, so a bare analysis meets resistance. Evidence: study (Damasio, split-brain work) cited by several authors. F255, F261. (Edwards; Sandler; James; Quesenberry; Parikh)
- A forceful list of reasons provokes counter-arguing, and cleverer audiences counter-argue better. Narrative transport lowers that guard. Evidence: study. (Edwards; Quesenberry)
- Complete dramatic arcs beat humour, celebrity, sex appeal and animals in ads. Four- to five-act videos earned about four times the shares of zero- to three-act ones, yet few ads used them. Evidence: study of Super Bowl and viral ads (Quesenberry; Snow; Edwards). F001.
- One named person moves people more than aggregate numbers. Donations rose for an individual's story; a single dog or worker outweighed a tsunami toll in the authors' account. Evidence: study and anecdote. T258. (Snow; James; Parikh)
- Specific detail makes a claim believable and big numbers numb. Concrete, named cases stay in mind where 100,000 abstract deaths do not. Evidence: author experience. (Parikh; James)
- Hard-to-read visuals are judged as hard-to-do actions. When a chart or instruction is difficult to decode, audiences assume the recommended act is hard too and refuse it. Evidence: study (Song and Schwarz). T245, T264. (Edwards)
- Working memory holds about four visual chunks; one highlighted element and a stripped chart shorten time to insight. Pictures plus speech reach far higher recall than speech alone. Evidence: cognitive research cited. F180, T244. (Parikh; McKinsey)
- Spatial reading order and area perception are physical constraints: eyes scan top-down and left-right, group by proximity, and misjudge small differences between pie slices beyond a few categories. Evidence: perceptual principle with case analysis. F163, T263. (McKinsey)
- Consistent chart design teaches the audience a visual language, so later charts decode faster, and splitting a dense slide costs no delivery time while each change re-engages the eye. Evidence: author experience. T382. (McKinsey)
- Spoilers aid comprehension: knowing the conclusion lowers load and does not reduce enjoyment. For time-poor business readers, state the main point first. Evidence: study (Leavitt and Christenfeld). F172, T266. (Edwards; McKinsey; Parikh)
- Opening on the audience's own strategy and problems holds attention, and validating a logic tree with stakeholders before building saves weeks of analysis and wins buy-in. Evidence: author experience and case. F130, F101. (Parikh; McKinsey; Edwards)
- Vertical inductive arguments survive the loss of one reason; horizontal deductive chains break at the weakest link. Three to five independent reasons is the working range. Evidence: logic and author experience. (Parikh; McKinsey)
- A small dose of negative detail makes a recommendation more credible than a one-sided pitch, and early disclosure of risks builds trust. Evidence: research cited and case. T462. (Parikh; Edwards; Forman)
- Context decides whether people engage with a chart: interest, trust in the source, existing beliefs, time and data literacy all matter. Evidence: qualitative study of 46 focus-group participants and diaries. F164, F274. (Feigenbaum)
- Data is never raw. Every dataset reflects choices about what to count and omit, and admitting partiality gives stronger claims than a view from nowhere. Evidence: scholarly and case evidence. F374, S086. (Feigenbaum)
- Messy or unstandardised records often hold the story; clean is not the same as useful. Evidence: practitioner action research. T272, T279, T276. (Feigenbaum)
- Comics and purposeful embellishment can raise engagement and recall over plain slides, while decorative clutter hides the evidence. Evidence: small experiment and expert debate. T271, T245. (Feigenbaum)
- A context-free exploratory chart fails to persuade a funder; the same data works once framed with a reason to act. Evidence: case. F159, T253. (Lo Duca)
- A hero with a stated want holds attention, and direct labels beat separate legends. Evidence: cited authority (Miller; Knaflic). F162, T249. (Lo Duca)
- Match vocabulary and detail to audience expertise; a code-heavy session failed with non-technical students. Evidence: author experience. F164. (Lo Duca; Feigenbaum)
- Generative AI speeds drafting but invents methods and carries bias, so a human must verify every output. Evidence: author experiment and efficiency trials. F165, T247. (Lo Duca)
- Narrators who lived the event carry the most credibility, and employees or customers are trusted over executives and corporate voices. Evidence: study and case. (Forman; Snow)
- Words must match deeds: facts grounded in verified data make a story fluent without deceiving. Evidence: author experience and corporate cases. F363. (Forman; Snow)
- Inviting the audience to contribute or advise lowers rejection and lifts the speaker's standing. Evidence: study (Elsbach and Kramer; Berger). T468. (Edwards; McKinsey)
- Early interaction, within two to three minutes, prevents passive listening. Evidence:  author experience. T413. (Parikh; McKinsey; Edwards)
- Incongruent stories collapse trust, and mediocre content vanishes in crowded feeds, where the top few percent take most engagement. Evidence: poll and platform report. F179, F171. (Snow)
- Measurement should start from a few key questions, not a long list of metrics. Evidence: author experience. F170. (Quesenberry)
- Stories connect numbers to lives and a fixed hero series keeps a core message; both rest on author experience. S084, T253. (Lo Duca; Bowles)
- Persuasion is a large share of economic work, and a data owner who cannot persuade leaves the analysis unused. Evidence: study of GDP composition (McCloskey and Klamer, cited). (Edwards)
- The same word lands differently by listener: innovative reads as untested to someone recently punished for risk, so frame outcomes as both excitement and safety. Evidence: author experience. F224. (Edwards)
- People retain one impression from a flood of messages, so a data story should aim at a single memorable message. Evidence: author experience and anecdote. F159. (Forman)
- Stories let listeners draw the moral themselves, which avoids resistance to authority; leave the final inference to them when the evidence is strong. Evidence: anecdote. T027. (Forman; Edwards)
- Hand-drawn sketching on paper before software speeds ideas, forces a second draft and avoids pasting old slides. Evidence: study (Sakai fMRI) and author experience. T603. (Edwards; McKinsey)
- Co-creating the story with the people in the data improves trust and reuse; extractive participation blocks both. Evidence: practitioner action research. F173, T273. (Feigenbaum)
- Heart-rate, sweat and brain coupling studies show listeners physically mirror a told story, and sensory detail drives that coupling. Evidence: fMRI studies (Hasson; Stephens, Silbert and Hasson). T281. (Bowles; James; Parikh)
- Peak and end are what memory keeps, so place the strongest finding and the next step where the audience will remember them. Evidence: theory (Kahneman). F160. (Sandler; James)
- Trust in a chart's maker rises with disclosed method: publish the warrant so others can check it. Evidence: scholarly case. F176. (Feigenbaum)

## Mistakes
- Showing the exploratory chart to the audience → filter to one insight, add context and a next step (F159, F160, T266). (Lo Duca; Edwards; Feigenbaum)
- Persuading with a list of reasons and data → lead with the story, then supply data inside it (F255, T259, T257). (Edwards; Snow; Parikh)
- Making the analyst, the company or the data the hero → cast the audience or the affected people as hero and the data as guide (F162, S085). (Edwards; Feigenbaum; Lo Duca)
- Narrating the research diary, false starts and sensitivity afterthoughts → report conclusions, integrate risk as scenarios (F172, F064). (McKinsey; Edwards)
- Topic or label titles such as Findings or Sales by month → write a full-sentence takeaway as the headline, with the neutral label below (T103, T241, T242, T290). (McKinsey; Parikh; Edwards)
- Bullet lists, dozens of bullets or crowded slides → one message per slide, chunk into labelled blocks (T382, T261, T277). (Edwards; Forman; McKinsey; Parikh)
- Dumping every chart or table into the deck → keep only what supports the insight, move proof to hidden backup or an appendix (F159, T530, T265). (Edwards; Parikh)
- Using pie slices beyond two or three, 3D, bubble or dual-axis charts → pick by data relationship, default to simple horizontal bars (F163, T263). (Edwards; McKinsey; Parikh; Lo Duca)
- Clutter: gridlines, decimals, separate legends, red-green palettes → strip, label lines directly, use colour-blind safe palettes (T245, T249, T251, T288). (Parikh; McKinsey; Lo Duca; Feigenbaum)
- Truncated or offset axes and waterfalls for non-additive figures → start at zero, convert to bars (T248, T263). (McKinsey; Lo Duca)
- Comparing raw counts across unequal populations, or larger bars meaning worse → normalise and flip polarity (T248). (Lo Duca)
- Forcing viewers to compute differences → pre-compute and state the gap, or use one big number (T243, T248). (Lo Duca; Edwards)
- Cherry-picking, treating one coincidence as causation, or an unrepresentative exception as the rule → show contradicting evidence, test the pattern with the skeptical lens (F161, T462). (Lo Duca; Forman)
- Burying bad news under flashy visuals or piling data around a needle → select the essential proof, disclose the downside (T462, T260). (Forman; Parikh)
- Ignoring missing, zero or messy data → show nulls and zeros, parse the mess (T279, T272, T275). (Feigenbaum)
- Treating timelines, maps and composites as neutral or complete → state criteria, verify projections, reveal the variance (F177, F374, T268). (Feigenbaum; Lo Duca)
- Targeting the general public → segment by think, feel, do and literacy (F274, F164, F158). (Feigenbaum; Lo Duca; Parikh)
- Presenter-centric opening, self-introduction or thanks first → open on the audience's challenge with a hook (F101, F224, T495). (Parikh; Edwards)
- Smooth, problem-free narrative or a single flawless option → alternate problems and solutions, weigh three options, raise your own objections (F035, T462). (Edwards; Forman)
- Outshining senior stakeholders with a discovery → frame it as something they would have seen (T458). (Edwards)
- Executive summary slide opening a live deck → move it to the final recap or an appendix (F064, F172). (McKinsey)
- Slides for hostile audiences, brainstorming, or reading from a laptop → hold a slide-free dialogue, add a scribe (T468). (McKinsey; Edwards)
- Jumping straight to PowerPoint → build an issue tree and paper storyboard first (F130, T603). (McKinsey; Parikh; Edwards)
- Sloppy sourcing or unchecked AI output → audit, cite and credit; use low temperature for facts (T256, T255, F168). (Lo Duca; Forman; Feigenbaum)
- Fabricating scenes, hiring actors as customers, or spin ahead of the facts → use real people and match claims to progress (F363, S189). (Forman; Snow)
- Over-using suspense or fear, or holding questions to the end → use them sparingly and invite questions throughout (T468). (Parikh; Edwards)
- Over-explaining the meaning or using hyperbole that sounds literal → let evidence carry the point and keep jargon plain (T027). (James; Sandler)
- Writing dense academic prose, or storytelling as a one-off → simplify the reading level and build a recurring habit (F179). (Snow)
- Treating measurement as vanity metrics → anchor to key questions and test causality (F170, F171). (Quesenberry; Snow)

## Formats
### Insight brief for decision makers
Use S081 (recommend) with F159 to find the insight and F064 to order it. Write headlines as sentences (T103), one message per slide (T382), lead with the insight (T266) and hold proof in hidden slides (T530). Add a flaw or risk line to earn trust (T462).

### Data story with a human face
Use S084 and S085, with F255 and F162. Open with the person or place (T259), place figures inside the story (T257), follow the aggregate with one named face (T258), and scale big numbers to human terms (T289). Zoom between case and context (T267).

### Research or model readout
Use S091 for status or S081 for findings with F172. State the method without results, lead findings with the headline, and end with implications. Use F066 to drill one driver at a time, and T264 to mark inflection points on charts.

### Chart and dashboard story
Use S083, F163 to pick the chart family, F180 to cut clutter and highlight one element, and F167 for emotion and placement. Apply T248, T249, T244, T245 and T288. For dynamic stories, fix the hero series and limit controls (T253).

### Community or investigative data story
Use S086, S088 and F176 with F174 and F374. Record sources in a data diary (T269), tidy the spreadsheet (T276), show missing values (T279), and publish the method so others can check it.

### AI-assisted data story
Use F165 to structure prompts, F166 to choose a model approach, F168 for the ethical check. Draft with a model, finish by hand (T247), keep temperature low (T256), keep image style consistent (T252) and credit sources (T255).
