# Brand

## When to use story in brand
- The brand sounds like every rival, and buyers compare on specs or price. A character, an origin or a belief gives them something to choose. S162, F314, F337, T532.
- Trust is thin: audiences scroll past ads, doubt reviews and punish gaps between words and deeds. Real people, admitted flaws and consistent behavior carry more than slogans. F329, F363, T462, T623.
- Many people and channels speak for the brand and it must read as one voice. Agree a position, a character and a filter for ideas. F312, F313, F290, F325.
- The story has to be believed inside before it is shouted outside: employees, leaders and agencies carry it. F316, F292, S167, T572.
- The product is physical, a place or an experience, and doing lands harder than telling. S180, S182, F300, F310.
- The brand wants loyalty beyond purchase: shared beliefs, rituals and a world that fans extend. F304, F307, F318, S176.
- Skip or shrink story when the reader needs a fact, a price or a legal notice; state it plainly and save story for the moment of resistance. Story also cannot repair a broken product, price or distribution; fix that first (Terenzzo). A story that contradicts what the company really does backfires (Snow and Lazauskas; Salmon).

## Principles
- Feeling decides and logic justifies: buyers choose on how a brand makes them feel and then rationalize, so open on emotion and bring facts after. Evidence: study (Gallup hotel survey, via Sandler; Damasio, via Biesenbach and Dolan) plus author experience. F261, F246. (Rodriguez; Sandler; Butler; Biesenbach; Dolan)
- Emotion-led campaigns paid off more than rational-only ones in profit analyses, and a mix of both beat logic alone. Evidence: study (campaign case analysis, via Quesenberry; UK practitioner data, via Dolan). F246. (Quesenberry; Dolan)
- Facts inside a story are remembered far better than bare facts; quoted multiples run from about six to twenty-two times. Evidence: study (Bower and Clark, via Lazer; Heath and Heath, via Biesenbach; Maxwell). T259, T257. (Rodriguez; Lazer; Biesenbach; Maxwell)
- A complete arc outperforms the usual ad tricks: full narrative spots beat humor, sex appeal, animals and celebrities on ratings and shares, and four or five acts earned about four times the shares of shorter ones. Evidence: study (Quesenberry and Coolsen content analyses). F247, F021. (Quesenberry; Hall; Creek; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Tension plus a character in need moves people to act: dramatic stories raised attention and empathy chemistry and increased giving. Evidence: study (Zak). F252, T464. (Hall; Quesenberry; Snow and Lazauskas; Biesenbach)
- Transported audiences argue less; immersion lowers counter-arguing and moves attitudes toward the story. Evidence: study (Green and Brock; Hall). F265. (Hall; Quesenberry)
- The customer is the hero and the brand the guide; audiences cool when the company stars in its own story. Evidence: author experience. F288, T589. (Rodriguez; Hall; Creek; Maestas; Terenzzo; Butler)
- A named person beats a crowd of statistics, and faces beat logos: founders, mascots and representatives earn trust faster than a corporation. Evidence: study (charity-letter experiment, via Snow and Lazauskas) plus case (Geico, Subway, Porsche). T563, T258. (Snow and Lazauskas; Butler; Mastropierro; Hall)
- Consistency pays and contradiction costs: steady brand behavior is tied to higher revenue, while messaging that contradicts history or practice triggers backlash and discounting. Evidence: study (industry data, via Rodriguez) plus case (Gillette, Nike, Subaru-Isuzu). F312, F313. (Rodriguez; Maestas; Salmon; Snow and Lazauskas; Sandler)
- Cross-channel mismatch creates dissonance: the voice, conduct and values a person meets must match at every touchpoint. Evidence: theory (consistency theory, via Sandler). F290. (Sandler; Maestas; Paulsen)
- Buyers reward belief and punish hollow stances: a large share buy on values, and brands seen as neutral or hypocritical are boycotted. Evidence: study (Edelman, via Rodriguez and Quesenberry). F363, T539. (Rodriguez; Quesenberry)
- Audiences test stories against reality: invented pseudo-history or fake testimony is punished, while openly owned flaws disarm critics. Evidence: study (authors' 3,000-person poll, via Snow and Lazauskas; student surveys, via Terenzzo) plus cases (Southwest, SAWS). F329, T462. (Snow and Lazauskas; Terenzzo; Maestas; Forman)
- Story cannot substitute for doing: narrative holds only when product, price and conduct back it, and artifacts, rituals and roles let audiences enact the story themselves. Evidence: case (TOMS; HBO Bleed For The Throne; Harley-Davidson). F300, S180. (Terenzzo; Paulsen; Vincent)
- Myth needs something tangible: abstract values land only when tied to a person, place or product people can touch, and cues that echo familiar archetypes let audiences co-author meaning. Evidence: author experience and cases (Disney, Harley-Davidson, Apple). F304, F309. (Vincent; Butler)
- Consumers own brand culture and brands only steward it; dictating it breeds skepticism, serving a faithful tribe sustains loyalty. Evidence: case studies. F307, F305. (Vincent)
- Physical detail is part of the story: a neglected corridor, faded prop or off-world sign breaks immersion like a flaw on a stage set. Evidence: case (Schrager hotels, Disneyland, Castle Gillian). F310, T557. (Vincent; Paulsen; Lupton)
- Peak and end govern how an experience is remembered, so design the high point and the finish, not the average. Evidence: study (Kahneman, via Sandler; Heath and Heath, via Paulsen). T063, T064. (Paulsen; Sandler)
- Employees and peers out-reach corporate channels: staff-shared messages travel further and convert better, and earned or peer content out-trusts paid ads. Evidence: study (industry statistics, via Rodriguez; Edelman, via Quesenberry). S167, T572. (Rodriguez; Quesenberry; Forman)
- Internal belief comes first: strategic stories fail when culture and reinforcement systems do not match, and values on posters mean little without personal stories of living them. Evidence: study (organizational research, via Forman) plus author experience. F316, F331. (Forman; Dolan; Rodriguez)
- Feeds reward interaction, not asks: posts that draw comments and shares earn the visibility a later ask needs, so give value repeatedly before asking. Evidence: author experience and platform mechanics. F249, F250, F191. (Vaynerchuk; Creek)
- Shares are identity currency: people pass on what makes them look informed, funny or good, so test whether sharing flatters the sharer. Evidence: author experience (Creek, 1,200 viral videos). F251, F357. (Creek; Vaynerchuk; Butler)
- Only exceptional content wins: a thin top slice of branded content takes nearly all engagement, and paid reach multiplies strong work but cannot rescue weak work. Evidence: study (Beckon report, via Snow and Lazauskas). F171, T651. (Snow and Lazauskas)
- Channel rules differ: print is processed more deeply than screens, direct mail draws more response and trust than email, and boards with two elements are noticed more than five. Evidence: study (industry benchmarks). T549, T550. (Quesenberry)
- Sagas hold value where sequels sag: repeating an old plot cuts returns, while known characters in fresh plots perform like originals. Evidence: study (authors' box-office analysis). S176, S175. (Snow and Lazauskas; Paulsen)
- Moral stories need an adult counterpart: candid, consenting treatment of indulgence or tension can work, while deception around it destroys trust. Evidence: case analysis (Marlboro versus Joe Camel, Carl's Jr.). F311. (Vincent)
- Jargon backfires: specialist words provoke resistance even when defined. Evidence: study (Ohio State experiment, via Lazer). T317, T652. (Lazer; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Meaning raises perceived value: stories attached to cheap objects multiplied resale prices, and narrative context shifts taste. Evidence: study (Significant Objects experiment, via Terenzzo). T532, S163. (Terenzzo; Vincent)
- Human voice protects trust: content labeled as AI is rated lower, and drafting by hand before using AI avoids sameness. Evidence: study (University of Florida and Vienna, via Lazer). F372, T573. (Lazer; Paulsen)
- A brand is the stories told about it when you are absent, and silence invites rivals, media and gossips to fill the gap. Evidence: author experience. T568, F317. (Maestas; Dolan)

## Mistakes
- Making the brand or product the hero → rewrite so one customer wins and the brand is guide or sidekick, with plugs short and late (F288, T589, F275). (Rodriguez; Hall; Creek; Terenzzo; Butler; Maestas; Hauge)
- Calling a mission statement, timeline or award list "our story" → build a person, an emotion and a moment, or keep only the peaks of the history (F339, F150, T597). (Hall; Terenzzo; Mastropierro; Biesenbach)
- Launching the story outside before inside believers exist, or dumping a portal of assets with no practice → train staff first with short hands-on prototyping (F316, F292, T576). (Rodriguez; Forman; Dolan)
- Sudden stances or stories that clash with past behavior → audit values and operations first, explain the shift live, and own earlier failures (F363, F329, T539). (Rodriguez; Snow and Lazauskas; Salmon; Sandler)
- Tone-deaf or edgy campaigns that ignore the customer's view → test the thesis through customers' eyes and with outside reviewers before release (F363, T236). (Quesenberry; Cody; Sandler)
- Faking authenticity: actors as customers or employees, airbrushed testimonials, fake underdog status → use real people and keep their rough edges (T623, T625, F363). (Forman; Hall; Butler)
- Pseudo-history, overpromised transformation or hidden sponsorship → ground the story in documented fact, signal fiction playfully, disclose paid content, and match promises to the real offer (S189, T619, T605). (Terenzzo; Quesenberry; Salmon)
- Treating story as a rescue for a weak product or contradicting corporate reality → fix fundamentals first, then tell the story the company can live (F363, S189). (Terenzzo; Salmon; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Treating storytelling as a one-off campaign or one team's job → run it as a standing habit with shared tags, calendar and training, and keep stories in perpetual prototype (F316, F302, T283). (Rodriguez; Maestas; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Hiding behind corporate polish, bragging About pages and buzzwords → tell the founder's struggle or a short human line (T563, T597, T462). (Mastropierro; Maestas; Cowie)
- Letting a story drift outside its lane or a voice shift mid-ask → write rules for accepting or rejecting ideas and hold one archetype (F313, F314, F293). (Creek; Butler; Maestas; Rodriguez)
- Chasing the latest idea and abandoning proven core brand traits, or going silent between campaigns → keep one core message and budget for fresh stories that reinforce it (F312, T538). (Maestas; Rodriguez)
- Advertising as the whole narrative: slogans first, story never → write the brand bible first and let ads be episodes of it (F306, F304). (Vincent; Salmon)
- Asking focus groups to write the story, or reducing buyers to demographics → use research to find meaning, then hire storytellers; profile buyers by motives and real biographies (F114, F305, F112). (Vincent; Butler; Mastropierro; Creek)
- Forcing product placement into drama or buying empty logo sponsorships → weave the brand into real dramatic choices and sponsor only where beliefs are shared (F295, F308). (Vincent; Quesenberry)
- Letting a fad burn out or leaning on one hero product, CEO or spokesperson → protect scarcity, reward core fans, and codify the myth so secondary agents can carry it (F307, F309). (Vincent)
- Ignoring back-stage detail in physical spaces → audit thresholds, staff tone and overlooked corners as carefully as the flagship entrance (F310, T557). (Vincent; Paulsen)
- Spending on spectacle or technology before the story core is proven → strip the story to a short premise, test it on cheap platforms, and only then scale (F303). (Paulsen; Terenzzo; Rodriguez)
- Selling in the first contact, stacking several asks, or sending clicks to a generic page → give value first, one ask per post, and deep-link to a message-matched page (F249, F250, T503). (Vaynerchuk; Creek; Quesenberry; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Cross-posting identical creative everywhere, or shipping blurry, stock or logo-less images → make each piece native to its platform and keep a visible brand cue (F191, T371, T535). (Vaynerchuk; Creek; Rodriguez)
- Chasing streaks, bought followers, prize giveaways or a copied viral stunt → raise response-worthy quality first and build from your own story (F357, F147, T651). (Creek; Vaynerchuk; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Over-producing candid formats or writing at a dense reading level → match production to real positioning and simplify toward plain language (T652, T623). (Creek; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Publishing into the void: no goals, no distribution, one magic metric → set objectives first, map channels, promote winners and judge on several measures (F170, F171, F335, T284). (Snow and Lazauskas; Quesenberry)
- Triggering strong emotion with no path for response → prepare community and support workflows before release (F334, F249). (Rodriguez; Creek)
- Preaching the moral or over-explaining a viral message → show the scene, leave the conclusion to the audience, and offer an incomplete pattern to solve (T027, F330). (Rodriguez; Maxwell; Hall)
- Forcing an antagonist, or building buzz for an offering without substance → name a defeatable obstacle and verify the product first, since cheated buyers spread complaints more than praise (F276, F330). (Maxwell)
- Letting AI write the outline or draft, or hiding its use → write the thesis and first draft yourself, then use AI for critique and research, and disclose it (F372, T573). (Lazer)
- Over-stuffing details, dates and bit players → keep one point per story and start inside the action (T218, T221, T048). (Rodriguez; Biesenbach; Terenzzo)

## Formats
### Brand story and origin story
Use S162 with S156 and S164. Build the founder or maker as one person with a problem (F337), publish your origin before others write it (T568), name people before scale (T563), and keep the maker's backstory next to the product (T564). Admit past failures openly (F329, T462). Replace About Us with a title that tells a story (T597). Check the draft against the ethics test before release (F363).

### Brand character, position and voice bible
Use S162 with S166 and set the position and promise (F312). Give the brand a character with traits the audience can picture (F314), match its personality to the buyer's identity (F293), and write the Brand Bible that ads then serve as episodes (F306). Write three to five rules for accepting or rejecting ideas (F313) and use a tagline as a filter, not a censor (F325). Keep one theme across channels (T538) and calibrate voice by measured traits when scaling writers (T594).

### Internal rollout and ambassadors
Use S167 with S159 and S160. Train staff before launch (F292), choose and brief ambassadors deliberately (F291), and run the program across the whole organization (F316). Circulate employee value stories on a steady rhythm (T572), anchor culture stories to objects (T576), and seat a customer chair in reviews (T537). Diagnose capability with F302 and F324.

### Content program and funnel
Use S169, S170 and S171 with S104 and plan backward from the goal (F315). Give value first and ask once (F249, F250), pull audiences from rented feeds to owned channels (F335, F328), and map topics by funnel stage and timing (F336). Run create, connect, optimize (F334), tag and audit stories (T283), and boost only proven winners (F171, T282). Keep the quality bar high before publishing (T651).

### Short video, social and share-worthy content
Use S101 or S178 with F251, F357 and F191. Open on a five-second hook (T049), give a six-second spot one idea (T543), break the feed's visual pattern (T409), and add an effortless response prompt (T372). Credit fan work (T566). Check that sharing flatters the sharer (F357) and prepare a response path before high-emotion releases (F334).

### Experience, space and world
Use S180 with S182, S181 or S179 and F300, F310, F301. Stage preparation in the open (T557), wrap a commodity experience in an adventure (T593), name things by sense (T559), and give waits and endings small rewards (T560, T561). For a long-lived world, grow layers in order (F303, F318), keep heritage anchors when refreshing (T565), and give fans rituals and vocabulary (F307, T567, T569).

### Stance, crisis and trust repair
Use S177 with S189 and F329. Explain a value shift live before a bold ad (T539), answer critics where you are absent (T540), circulate your own failed bets (T588), and run the disclosure and ethics checks (T605, F363, T619). Keep actions in line with the narrative (F312), and vet partners and edgy positions first (F308, F311).
