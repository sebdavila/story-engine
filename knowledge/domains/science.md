# Science

## When to use story in science
Use this file when the job is to explain, defend or apply what research says about stories, or to communicate evidence (data, studies, findings) to people who will not read it raw. It covers four situations: choosing between story and facts for a given audience, moving a resistant or skeptical audience, telling honest data stories, and using AI in the writing process without losing quality or trust. It is also the file to consult when someone asks why a craft rule works (attention, memory, simulation, transportation). Evidence quality varies: many claims come from lab studies and meta-analyses (Storr; Gottschall; Zak as cited by Lazer), others from author experience. The Principles below label which is which. For scene and sentence craft go to the craft domain; for business and sales uses go to those domains.

## Principles
- Brains register change and gaps in knowledge, not stasis. Unexpected change anchors attention, and curiosity peaks when people hold partial clues and think they nearly know the answer. Evidence: study (Storr, citing Loewenstein and fMRI reward work). (Storr)
- Concrete language makes the brain simulate the scene; abstract labels and dead metaphors do not. Specific sensory detail is more emotive than adjectives like terrible. Evidence: study (Storr, citing Bergen's simulation and eye-tracking work). (Storr)
- Minds impose cause and effect on anything: random shape movement becomes a plot, two adjacent words become a causal link, and retold odd stories get regularized. Evidence: study (Storr; Gottschall, citing Heider and Simmel). (Storr; Gottschall)
- Pursuit of a goal powers attention and recall. Dopamine rises during the chase, an explicit goal roughly doubles recall of a passage, and bestsellers use want-and-need verbs about twice as often. Evidence: study, corpus analysis. (Storr)
- Words embedded in a story are recalled several times better than the same content alone, and immersive arcs engage many brain regions. Evidence: study, with reported figures from a 1969 experiment and neuroscience vendors. (Lazer; Guptara)
- Transportation lowers counter-arguing. Immersed listeners test the message less and shift beliefs, and the effect holds across a large meta-analysis. Evidence: meta-analysis, lab studies. (Gottschall; Storr)
- Open preaching breaks the effect. When persuasive intent is obvious, resistance rises; when the story leaves the lesson open, listeners build the conclusion afterward (retrospective reflection). Evidence: review, studies. (Gottschall)
- Narrative changes beliefs and attitudes more reliably than statistical argument, and statistics can trigger backfire in partisan audiences. Evidence: meta-analyses, experiments. (Gottschall)
- Threatened beliefs feel like objective reality, so contradicting evidence activates threat circuits; people defend identity, not just opinions. Evidence: fMRI study. (Storr)
- People see themselves as the hero. They misremember selfish acts as fair and rate their own morality highly, which is why villains also act as the righteous party in their own account. Evidence: studies. (Storr; Gottschall)
- The inner narrator confabulates. Conscious speech invents plausible reasons for behaviour driven by unconscious motives, so stated motives are weak evidence. Evidence: split-brain studies. (Storr)
- Emotional and arousing stories travel and predict action. Heart rate and skin arousal during a charity story predicted donors with about 80 percent accuracy, and false news spreads far faster than true news online. Evidence: study. (Gottschall; Lazer, citing Zak)
- Fictional contact with likable out-group characters lowers prejudice more durably than diversity training, and hostile portrayals carry hostility the same way. Evidence: field and lab studies. (Gottschall)
- Facts alone leave a void. Dry debunking of a thrilling myth loses to the myth because it offers no purpose or ego reward; a counter-story does. Evidence: author reasoning from studies. (Gottschall)
- Audiences everywhere expect escalating trouble and punishment of offenders; stories that strip out predicament and moral tension lose readers. Evidence: quantitative studies of plots and ratings. (Gottschall)
- Jargon signals exclusion even when defined beside the term, and provokes counter-arguing. Evidence: study (Ohio State, 2020). (Lazer)
- Content labeled as AI-made is rated worse whatever its true authorship, while honest disclosure can raise ad trust. Evidence: studies, survey. (Lazer)
- Writing first and using AI for research and springboards beats AI-first drafting, which breeds sameness and reduces engagement. Evidence: studies, author experience. (Lazer)
- Data and story together reach both thought and feeling, but engagement depends on viewer context: interest, trust in the source, existing beliefs, time and data confidence. Evidence: qualitative study (46 participants, diaries). (Feigenbaum)
- Data is never raw. Every dataset reflects choices about what to count and omit, and admitting partiality gives stronger, less biased claims. Evidence: scholarship, case evidence. (Feigenbaum)
- Comics and sequential panels raised engagement and whole-story comprehension against slides in a small experiment, and purposeful thematic embellishment can aid recall even though decorative clutter hides evidence. Evidence: small experiment, expert debate. (Feigenbaum)
- Messy public data often holds the best story once its variables are parsed. Evidence: practitioner action research. (Feigenbaum)
- Fabricated stories damage trust and perceived value claims rest on narrative context, as shown when thrift items sold for far more with invented stories attached. Evidence: experiment, survey of opinion-makers. (Terenzzo)
- Storytelling cannot rescue a broken product, price or distribution; it holds long term only when behavior matches the story. Evidence: author experience, case critiques. (Terenzzo)
- Attention is scarce and goes first to threats, reproduction cues and emotionally charged events over routine facts. Evidence: cited studies, author synthesis. (Terenzzo)
- The paradox of comprehension: people grasp only what they attend to, attend only to what seems useful, and judge useful only what they understand. So bridge relevance first, then name the abstraction. Evidence: author theory, education cases. (Terenzzo)
- Social storytelling supported cooperation. Skilled storytellers in a forager group were preferred as campmates and cooperation rose, and gossip and myth regulated behavior beyond small-group size. Evidence: field study, anthropological models. (Lazer; Gottschall; Storr)
- Being actively listened to activates reward regions and speeds trust, so story gathering that starts with listening yields better material. Evidence: small fMRI study, psychological safety research. (Lazer)
- Novelty triggers learning and memory circuits; a pattern break in structure or visuals holds attention. Evidence: fMRI study, ad testing. (Lazer)
- Perceived control buffers pain and anxiety, which is why endings that restore agency feel satisfying. Evidence: experiments. (Storr)

## Mistakes
- Forcing narrative onto routine information. Listeners who want fast facts or instructions get annoyed → use bullets or a transparent account for instruction and save dramatization for resistant minds (S058, T239). (Gottschall)
- Using story to protect a weak claim, or dressing a lecture as story. Preaching alerts the listener and kills transportation → embed the point in concrete scenes and leave a margin of ambiguity (F265, F255, T027). (Gottschall)
- Arguing against a gripping myth with a story void ("nothing happened") → counter with an activating true story and a clear role for the listener (S155, F283, T526). (Gottschall)
- Framing a huge problem as hopeless so people freeze → translate abstract statistics into present-tense human stakes with a reachable action (F168, T526). (Gottschall)
- Flattening opponents into monsters, which breeds sanctimony → render them as round people shaped by circumstance, or tell trouble without villains (F098, T650, S008). (Gottschall; Storr)
- Treating the inherited storyverse as objective truth → name the master narrative, test claims, and show competing accounts (F283). (Gottschall)
- Opening with neutral biography or a slow wind-up. A change-seeking brain disengages → open on unexpected change, a threat of change or an information gap (F008, F049, F092, T048). (Storr; Lazer)
- Naming feelings with abstract labels, passive syntax or dead metaphors → give three concrete details, active order and fresh physical imagery (F047, F355, T140, T089). (Storr)
- Episodic and-then scenes → link events by because and but, each starting from a problem (F004, T033). (Storr)
- Over-explaining motives, which shuts out audience projection → leave calibrated gaps (T027). (Storr)
- Writing for everyone, or for a bloodless persona slide → name one real person or segment, and map what they should think, feel and do (F274, F164, F158). (Lazer; Feigenbaum)
- Jargon to sound credible → hunt jargon in decks and drafts and translate to plain speech (T317, T386, T600). (Lazer)
- Sanitizing tension, with smooth success boasts → lead with real conflict, mistakes and stakes (F270, T462). (Lazer)
- Making the analyst or the method the protagonist → lead with the insight and recenter on the people, place or event in the data (F162, F160, T266, T268). (Feigenbaum)
- Template plugging, chartjunk and 3D or neon charts → sketch first, match chart to data shape, highlight one item, strip clutter (F163, F180, T263, T244, T245). (Feigenbaum; Lo Duca)
- Red and green as the only difference, unfamiliar metaphors, uncaptioned visuals → dual-encode, test metaphors with real readers, caption the intended takeaway (F175, T288). (Feigenbaum)
- Treating composite characters, timelines or maps as neutral or complete → reveal variance, pair numbers with real testimony, state selection and coordinate choices (T268, T269, F374). (Feigenbaum)
- Discarding messy data or ignoring zeros → inspect variables, plot nulls, and annotate missing groups as part of the story (T279, T272). (Feigenbaum)
- Extractive participation, treating affected people only as interviewees → invite them as co-designers and keep a tidy log of every figure (F174, T269). (Feigenbaum)
- Outsourcing outline or first draft to AI → write thesis, outline and draft yourself, then invite critique; use AI for springboards and rough passes (F372, T247, T629). (Lazer)
- Asking a default chatbot to coach with no guardrails, since it drifts to writing for you → command blunt feedback from a defined persona (T630, F165). (Lazer)
- Hiding AI use, or overclaiming → state when and how AI contributed and what human choices were kept (T573, T255). (Lazer; Lo Duca)
- Fabricated origin myths or promises the product cannot deliver → keep stories documented, mark fiction clearly and align promises with reality (F156, F298, T619, F363). (Terenzzo)
- Spending effort on telling (polish, slides, media) before story → build the story first, validate it, then pick channels (F149, F360). (Terenzzo)
- Chronology instead of story, every date and award included → narrate only the peaks (F150, F339). (Terenzzo)
- Treating the audience as passive recipients of a number → name the one thing they should think, feel or do and design each slide for it (F274, F348). (Feigenbaum; Terenzzo)
- Giving a puzzling claim no relevance bridge, so listeners disengage before the abstraction lands → open with why it matters to them, then the concept (F158, T383). (Terenzzo)
- Leaning on coincidence or luck to resolve a story of evidence or argument → earn the conclusion through cause and character action (T151, F004). (Gottschall)

## Formats
### Evidence-to-story brief (explaining research to a lay audience)
Use S084 and S086 when the finding is the point. Build with F159 then F160: pick the single valuable insight, state it first, then add background and a hook, and end with next steps. Cast with F162 and calibrate with F164. Techniques: T266 lead with the insight, T267 zoom in and out, T258 one face after the numbers, T259 story first then facts. Tell the story of what the findings change for a person, not the sequence of analysis steps.

### Persuading a skeptic or resistant audience
Use S148 or S058 and F255 or F279. Open from a shared experience or a concrete human moment, give the data inside that feeling, and stop before stating the moral. For entrenched identity beliefs add F283 to name the storyverse behind the reaction and F282 for repeated exposure to rounded characters. Techniques: T505, T487, T027, T239. Check the result against F363.

### Debunking a myth or conspiracy
Use S155 as the opponent and design a rival true story in the same register. Diagnose with F283, give the listener a role in the true account (F255), and keep stakes present-tense (T526). Avoid S154, which flips villains without adding truth. Pause before casting anyone as the villain (T650).

### Data story with charts and maps
Use S082 or S083 and S085 for the protagonist. Build with F159, F160, F161. Choose charts with F163 and F180, design with F175. Techniques: T263, T244, T245, T249, T288, T277. Add the honesty layer with F374, F176 and T279, T269, then close with F168.

### Data story for advocacy or a funding ask
Use S147 and S084. Follow F169: a personal why, one long story with data dropped in, or a short story at the key metric. Include one or two figures, a named person and a clear ask (T258, T262). Run the ethical check in F363 before release.

### AI-assisted writing workflow
Use F372 and F165. Draft the thesis and outline yourself, ask AI for research and critique from a named reviewer persona (T630), mine it for alternatives only when stuck (T629), finish by hand (T247), and disclose its use (T573).
