# Marketing

## When to use story in marketing
- The audience is scrolling, skipping or distrusts ads, and the piece must earn attention before it asks for anything. F191, F248, F249.
- The brand is faceless or interchangeable and needs a character, a founder or a customer people can root for. S162, S156, F314, F288.
- A claim ("we care", "best quality") needs proof the buyer can picture: one named customer, one concrete moment. S120, F238, T080.
- The goal is sharing, not only viewing: the piece must give viewers something that says who they are. F251, F357, S101.
- Content must run for months across channels, funnel stages and teams, with one story core. S168, S169, S170, S171, F290, F336.
- Skip or shrink story when the reader wants a fast fact, a price, a how-to step or a legal notice (Gottschall); give it plainly and keep story for the moment of resistance. Skip it also when product, price or service is broken: story cannot cover that (Terenzzo).

## Principles
- Emotion leads, logic justifies: buyers decide on how a brand makes them feel and then rationalize, so open with feeling and bring facts after. Evidence: study (Gallup hotel-guest survey, via Sandler; Damasio, via Biesenbach) plus author experience. F261, F246. (Rodriguez; Sandler; Biesenbach; Butler; Hauge)
- Facts anchored in a story are remembered far better than bare facts; the commonly cited ratios range from about six times to twenty-two times. Evidence: study (Bower and Clark, via Lazer; Heath and Heath, via Biesenbach; Aaker, via Kamath). T259, T257. (Lazer; Biesenbach; Kamath; Maxwell)
- A complete arc beats the usual ad tricks: full narrative ads outscored humor, sex appeal, animals and celebrities on ratings and sharing, and ads with four or five acts earned about four times the shares of ads with fewer. Evidence: study (Quesenberry and Coolsen, Super Bowl and viral-ad content analyses). F247, F021. (Quesenberry; Hall; Creek; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Transported audiences argue less. Immersion lowers counter-arguing and moves attitudes toward the story, and open-ended showing works better than a stated moral. Evidence: study (Green and Brock; van Laer meta-analysis). F265, T027. (Gottschall; Hall; Quesenberry)
- Tension plus a character in need moves people to act: dramatic stories raised attention hormones and oxytocin, and increased donations. Evidence: study (Zak). F252, T464. (Hall; Quesenberry; Snow and Lazauskas; Biesenbach)
- One person beats many: a named individual raised more money than statistics about a whole problem. Evidence: study (University of Pennsylvania charity-letter experiment, via Snow and Lazauskas; Zak). T258, T465. (Snow and Lazauskas; Kamath)
- Customers are the hero and the brand is the guide: audiences switch off when the company stars in its own story. Evidence: author experience. F288, T589. (Rodriguez; Hall; Creek; Maestas; Mastropierro; Hauge)
- Feeds reward interaction, not asks: platforms boost posts that draw comments and shares, so repeated no-ask value earns the visibility a later ask needs. Evidence: author experience (Vaynerchuk; Creek). F249, F250. (Vaynerchuk; Creek)
- Shares are identity currency: people pass on content that makes them look informed, funny or good, so test whether sharing flatters the sharer. Evidence: author experience (Creek, 1,200+ viral videos) and anecdote. F251, F357. (Creek; Vaynerchuk; Butler)
- High-arousal emotions travel: anger, awe, elation and amusement spread; sadness fades quickly, and false stories out-spread true ones online. Evidence: study (Vosoughi et al., via Gottschall; Creek's own video data). F251, T649. (Gottschall; Creek)
- Attention goes to what is useful and understood: viewers abandon boring or irrelevant videos, not all long ones, so length is a function of value. Evidence: author experience and platform data. T651. (Creek; Butler; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Only exceptional content wins: a small top slice of branded content gets nearly all engagement, and paid reach multiplies strong creative but cannot rescue weak creative. Evidence: study (Beckon report, via Snow and Lazauskas). F171, T282. (Snow and Lazauskas; Vaynerchuk)
- Consistent character builds trust and inconsistent behavior destroys it: consistency is tied to higher revenue, while a story that contradicts a company's actions or history draws backlash. Evidence: study (industry data) plus case (Gillette, Nike). F312, F313. (Rodriguez; Maestas; Snow and Lazauskas; Salmon; Sandler)
- Buyers expect stance and honesty: a large share of consumers buy on belief, and the audience scrutinizes stories for truth; invented pseudo-history is punished. Evidence: study (Edelman; authors' 3,000-person poll). F363, T605. (Rodriguez; Quesenberry; Snow and Lazauskas; Terenzzo)
- Faces beat logos: people bond with a named founder, mascot or representative faster than with a corporation, and vulnerability reads as courage in others. Evidence: study (Brown, via Kamath) plus case (Geico, Subway). F314, T563, T462. (Butler; Mastropierro; Kamath; Hauge; Cody)
- Specific beats general, and specificity creates universality: one narrow person and moment lets many viewers project themselves into it. Evidence: author experience. F047, T077, T080. (Hauge; Biesenbach; Hall)
- Channels set the rules: print is processed more deeply than screens, direct mail draws higher response and trust than email, and billboards with two elements get noticed more than five. Evidence: study (industry benchmarks). T548, T549, T550. (Quesenberry)
- Peers out-trust paid media: earned coverage, employee voices and micro-influencers outperform corporate channels. Evidence: study (Edelman; Experticity, via Quesenberry; employee-sharing statistics, via Rodriguez). S170, S184. (Quesenberry; Rodriguez; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Story is not a substitute for doing: audiences reward stories backed by action and punish gaps between narrative and practice. Evidence: case (TOMS, Nike, Volkswagen). F363, S189. (Terenzzo; Salmon; Paulsen; Kamath)
- Moves that hold: keep a low-friction ending (a link, an invitation), not a hard commitment at first contact. Evidence: author experience. T503, F250. (Cowie; Vaynerchuk; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Meaning attached to an object raises its perceived value: stories written for cheap thrift items multiplied their resale price, and narrative context shifts taste in blind tests. Evidence: study (Significant Objects experiment, via Terenzzo). T532, S163. (Terenzzo; Vincent)
- A story exists whether or not you tell it: silence lets competitors and media fill the gap, and owning flaws first disarms critics. Evidence: author experience and case (Southwest, SAWS). F329, T568, T462. (Maestas; Forman)
- Keeping customers costs less than winning new ones, so ongoing fresh stories on one core message compound value. Evidence: study (Bain retention research, via Maestas). F316, T538. (Maestas)
- Peak and end shape memory of an experience, so design the high point and the finish of a campaign or visit, not the average. Evidence: study (Kahneman, via Sandler; Heath and Heath, via Paulsen). T063, T064. (Paulsen; Sandler)
- Doing outperforms telling: giving audiences artifacts, rituals and roles lets them enact and spread the story. Evidence: case (HBO Bleed For The Throne, Harley-Davidson). F300, T569, S180. (Paulsen; Vincent; Butler)
- Jargon and many messages dilute: specialist words trigger resistance even when defined, and extra messages weaken the core one. Evidence: study (Ohio State experiment, via Lazer; de Vries et al., via Kamath). T652, T365. (Lazer; Kamath; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Admitting obstacles and doubts makes promises believable, while promising effortless results triggers disbelief. Evidence: author experience. T462, T459. (Hauge; Kamath; Cody)
- Humor eases a guarded audience and can make routine notices read, but it must match the room and punch up, never at a person. Evidence: study (University of Kansas laughter study; Southwest safety announcements, via Cody). T585, T236. (Cody; Kamath)
- Cross-channel contradictions create dissonance: the voice, behavior and values a person meets must match at every touchpoint. Evidence: theory (consistency theory, via Sandler). F290, F312. (Sandler; Maestas; Salmon)
- Disclosing AI use and keeping a human voice protects trust: content labeled AI is rated lower, and drafting by hand before using AI avoids sameness. Evidence: study (University of Florida and Vienna; MIT). T573, F372. (Lazer; Paulsen)

## Mistakes
- Making the product or brand the hero → cast one customer as hero and the brand as guide, and keep plugs brief and late (F288, T589, F275). (Rodriguez; Hall; Creek; Terenzzo; Butler; Hauge; Maestas)
- Calling a timeline, mission statement or list of awards a story → build a person, an emotion and a moment instead, or compress the history to its peaks (F339, F150, T597). (Hall; Terenzzo; Mastropierro; Biesenbach)
- Selling in the first contact and stacking asks → give value first, then one clear ask on a page that matches the promise (F249, F250, T503). (Vaynerchuk; Creek; Snow and Lazauskas; Cowie)
- Posting the same creative on every network → make each piece native to its platform, and keep the brand voice steady through the ask (F191, T371). (Vaynerchuk; Creek)
- Chasing daily streaks, followers or giveaways instead of response-worthy content → raise quality first; fake or freebie-driven followers depress organic reach (F357, T651). (Creek; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Forcing a trend or copying another brand's viral stunt → join only conversations with a genuine link to the brand, and build from your own story (F147, F357). (Vaynerchuk; Creek)
- Drifting outside your brand lane or sounding like a different brand → write three to five rules for accepting or rejecting an idea, and hold one archetype (F313, F314, F312). (Creek; Butler; Maestas)
- Switching stance suddenly or telling stories that contradict real practice → audit values and operations first, explain a shift live, and own past failures (F363, F329, T539). (Rodriguez; Snow and Lazauskas; Salmon; Sandler)
- Over-producing candid or grassroots formats, or sending stock photos with human stories → match production to the brand's real positioning and photograph the actual people (T535, T623). (Creek; Rodriguez; Vaynerchuk)
- Telling a story to fit a point but over-stuffing it with background, dates and bit players → trim to what serves one point and start inside the action (T218, T221, T048). (Rodriguez; Biesenbach; Terenzzo)
- Treating a viral hit as a strategy or skipping the follow-through for strong emotion → prepare a community or support path before releasing high-emotion content (F334, F249). (Rodriguez; Creek)
- Hiding behind corporate polish, bragging About pages or buzzwords → tell the founder's struggle or a short human line instead (T597, T563, T462). (Mastropierro; Maestas; Cowie)
- Leading with specs, discounts or a technology slide → open with the customer's tension and benefit, then justify with features (T469, T259, F262). (Quesenberry; Kamath; Sandler; Cody)
- Preaching the moral or over-explaining a viral message → show the scene, leave the conclusion to the audience, and give an incomplete pattern they can solve (T027, F330, F265). (Gottschall; Rodriguez; Maxwell)
- Stuffing every selling point into one ad or post → unfold the story in acts across spots, mailers or scheduled posts (T554, T550, F247). (Quesenberry; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Writing for a demographic or an imagined persona → listen to real audience members, use psychographics and co-create with insiders (F115, F114, F112, T240). (Mastropierro; Butler; Creek; Rodriguez; Vincent)
- Letting focus groups write the story or committees dilute it → use qualitative research to find meaning and have skilled storytellers draft, with at most two approvers (F114, F381). (Vincent; Biesenbach)
- Faking authenticity: actors as customers, fake underdog status, airbrushed testimonials → use real people and keep their rough edges (T623, T625, F363). (Forman; Hall; Butler)
- Sending clicks to a generic homepage, 404s or long forms → deep-link to a message-matched page with minimal fields (F250). (Quesenberry; Vaynerchuk)
- Publishing into the void: no distribution, no goals, one magic metric → set objectives, map channels, promote winners, and judge on several metrics (F170, F171, F335, T284). (Snow and Lazauskas; Quesenberry)
- Treating storytelling as a one-off campaign or one team's job → run it as a standing habit with shared tags, calendar and training (F316, F302, T283). (Rodriguez; Maestas; Snow and Lazauskas)
- Going external before internal believers, or dumping assets without training → train employees first and run short hands-on prototyping (F316, F292, T542). (Rodriguez; Forman)
- Tone-deaf humor or edgy campaigns, and manipulation tactics such as scare, outrage bait or hidden sponsorship → test through the customer's eyes, disclose paid content, deliver what the headline promised (T236, T497, T605, F363). (Cody; Quesenberry; Butler)
- Letting AI write the draft or assuming AI saves the strategy → write the thesis and first draft yourself, then use AI for critique and research (F372, T573). (Lazer)

## Formats
### Brand story and origin story
Use S162 with S156 and S164. Build the founder or maker as one person with a problem (F337), tell the story first on your own terms (T568), name the people before the scale (T563), keep the maker's backstory next to the product (T564), and admit past failures when asked (F329, T462). Keep emotional anchors when you refresh the story (T565). Ground it in real action, not a claim (S189).

### Customer proof story
Use S120 with F238, choose the hero by F153 and collect the story with F119. Open on the customer's pain, not the product (T589), show the before and after (T081), keep rough edges (T623), and read the draft back to the customer (T625). Use T469 to end each point on what changed for the buyer.

### Short video, ad or feed post
Use S101 or S100 with F251, F252 and F254. Open with a hook in the first seconds (T049), break the visual pattern of the feed (T409), and give a six-second spot one idea (T543). Frame everyday frustration as a recognition people want to share (T547), and add an effortless response prompt (T372). Check shareability with F357. Keep it native to the platform (F191) and add a point of view when reposting (T373).

### Trust ladder and funnel content
Use S169, S170 and S171 with F336 and F328. Give value first and ask once (F249, F250), pull attention toward owned channels such as email (F335), teach with useful content near the purchase (F297), and keep the closing ask low pressure (T503). Fill the calendar with timely, seasonal and evergreen stories (S172, S173, S174). Sequence the acts of one story across posts (T554) rather than repeating a call to action.

### Print, mail, outdoor, radio and PR
Use S168 with F290 and F247. Map mail and email onto five acts (T550), pair a bent headline with a straight image (T548), cap boards at about seven words and two elements (T549), layer sound for radio (T545), and write press pitches that lead with facts then human story (T552). Plan events with aligned stunts (T553) and check claims, permissions and sponsorship disclosure before launch (T605, F298).

### Brand universe and community
Use S162 with S166 and S176 and build with F304, F306 and F318. Tie beliefs to a tangible person, place or product (F309), give the audience insider vocabulary and rituals (T567, T569), credit fan work (T566), vet partners for story fit (F308), and grow slowly enough for core fans to stay (F307). Add sequels as sagas with fresh progress, not repeats (S176, S175).

### Measurement and amplification
Use F170 and F171 with T282, T283, T284 and T285. Publish cheaply, tag stories by topic, persona and format, compare batches on several metrics, spend only on the winners, and fix the headline or lead when readers leave early. Hold a high quality bar before publishing (T651).
