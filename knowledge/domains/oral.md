# Oral

## When to use story in oral
Use this file when a story will be spoken to a live listener: a stage or open-mic telling, a workplace anecdote, a pitch, a keynote, a toast, an interview answer, a meeting, a sales call. Spoken stories obey different rules from written ones. The listener cannot reread, so time, place and cast stay few, and the teller's voice, pauses and presence carry as much as the plot. Choose the register first. Workplace stories sit at the conversational end (one to three minutes, plain talk), stage stories can run longer and be shaped more, and persuasion talks need a point the room can act on. For scene-level craft, memoir mining and written drafts, read the craft domain; for pitches and decks, the business and sales domains; this file covers what changes when the audience is in the room.

## Principles
- Live telling plants richer mental pictures than reading or video; children shown a told and a read version drew the told one 78 to 86 percent of the time. Evidence: author experiment (Haven; Apps).
- Listeners keep the gist, goals and feeling of a heard story, not the wording; they also think they remember exact phrases. Evidence: neuroscience citations, author experience. (Haven; Bowles; Callahan; James)
- Spoken stories flow like a river: no pause button, so keep time, place and cast simple, cap scenes at about seven, and keep any scene-setting short because listeners hold only a few seconds of text verbatim. Evidence: author experience. (Dicks; James; Maguire; Leitman)
- Learn the order of scenes and images, not sentences. Memorize at most the opening, the closing and refrains, then tell it aloud to real people many times. Evidence: author experience across hundreds of stage tellers. (Haven; Bowles; Dicks; Leitman; James; Maguire; Parikh)
- Rehearsal aims differ: strict accuracy helps the teller recall, while telling it for entertainment makes it stickier for listeners. Evidence: study (Callahan citing Dudukovic, Marsh and Tversky).
- Conviction, energy and enjoyment convince more than verbatim perfection, and sincerity covers imperfect delivery better than polish covers weak content. Evidence: workshop experiment, author experience. (Haven; Biesenbach; Bowles)
- Vulnerability earns trust and boasting repels. Admit the flaw or stumble, credit others, and pair any admission with what changed. Evidence: author experience across tens of thousands of stage stories, studies cited. (Bowles; Leitman; Dicks; James; Hauge; Kamath; Livesay; Cody)
- Tell stories as scars, not wounds: process pain before telling it, and test by speaking it aloud calmly. Raw grief, venting or crying for sympathy makes listeners anxious. Evidence: author experience. (Bowles; James; Leitman; Biesenbach; Guptara)
- Audiences sense fabrication and reward candor, so the true detail beats the flattering invention; credit anyone else's story. Evidence: anecdote, author experience, survey. (Leitman; Dolan; Callahan; Terenzzo; James)
- Specific beats general, and small universal feelings beat spectacle. One named person, one moment and one telling detail let strangers recognize themselves. Evidence: author experience, film analysis, listener emails. (Leitman; Dicks; Karia; Simmons; James; Parikh; Biesenbach; Hauge)
- Seeing precedes feeling. Sensory detail, ideally one dominant sense or a brisk blend of several, builds the picture; listeners imagine better than a photo on screen supplies. Evidence: practitioner testimony, author experience. (James; Karia; Simmons; Callahan; Apps)
- Stories are recalled far better than lists or statistics (about 63 to 67 percent against 5 percent in the Heath study; six to seven times more words in Bower and Clark), though the popular 22-times figure is an unverified estimate. Evidence: study. (Callahan; Edwards; Lazer; Biesenbach)
- Decisions are felt first and justified after; leading with a logical case triggers counter-arguing, while a story first pulls listeners in before defenses engage. Evidence: study (Damasio; Westen). (Edwards; Simmons; Callahan; Guptara; Dolan; Hauge)
- Facts delivered before the story get fitted into the listener's old story or discarded, so give the context-setting story first and the numbers inside it. Evidence: author experience. (Simmons; Maestas; Buster)
- A listener's brain activity aligns with the teller's during a coherent story, and comprehension is highest when the listener anticipates what comes next. Evidence: study (Hasson; Silbert). (Edwards; Guptara; Callahan; Dolan; Bowles; Parikh)
- Rising tension releases cortisol (attention) and oxytocin (empathy); donations rose in Zak's experiments. Evidence: study. (Callahan; Dolan; Edwards; Lazer; Parikh)
- Conflict needs an uncertain outcome. Stories without opposing forces produce no suspense, and links by but or therefore keep momentum where and chains stall. Evidence: author experience, anecdote. (Karia; Dicks; Biesenbach; Hauge; Edwards)
- Where the point goes depends on the room. Workplace talks gain from a relevance statement or stated destination up front, and Bransford and Johnson found it doubled recall. Stage stories hold meaning and ending back so curiosity survives. Evidence: study, author experience. (Callahan; Edwards; Parikh versus Dicks; James; Apps; Bowles)
- Unresolved endings linger, memory keeps peaks and ends, and adults resent being told what to think. Stop at the peak and let listeners infer the meaning, with at most one light bridge sentence in business settings. Evidence: author experience, Kahneman peak-end citation. (Dicks; Sandler; Buster; Leitman; Callahan; Dolan; Schwabauer)
- Open inside the story. The first 30 to 60 seconds decide whether the room settles in, so skip thanks, agendas and topic announcements. Evidence: author coaching experience. (Karia; Parikh; Maxwell; Dolan; Biesenbach)
- Humor releases tension and bonds a room if it grows from true detail. Place laughs early and around pain, then close on heart. Evidence: author experience, studies cited. (Cody; Dicks; Bowles; Kamath; Parikh)
- Pauses, a steady pace and a lower calm register read as confidence. Top salespeople paused five times longer after an objection and held near 173 words per minute instead of speeding to 188. Evidence: study of 67,149 calls (Livesay). (Maguire; Simmons)
- Nerves can be reframed as caring; public speaking raises heart rate and adrenaline, so personal stories that are easy to recall steady the opening. Evidence: studies cited, author experience. (Maxwell; Karia; Parikh; Livesay)
- Practice standing, aloud, with a supportive listener or two; a mirror and a crowd of critics distort the telling, and early critique prunes fledgling stories, so ask for what worked. Evidence: author experience, study (Texas A&M standing study). (Haven; Dicks; James; Bowles; Simmons; Leitman)
- Start from the audience. Ask what they should think, feel or do and what value they get, and open on their strategy or shared ground. Evidence: author experience. (Edwards; Parikh; Livesay; Maestas; Hauge)
- Cast the customer or listener as hero and yourself as guide; Maxwell argues for two active heroes so the story still drives toward the deal. Evidence: author experience, case studies. (Edwards; Hall; Guptara; Terenzzo; Maxwell)
- Interaction keeps a room awake. Invite questions throughout, hold the first checkpoint within two or three minutes, and use names; people judge a meeting by how heard they felt. Evidence: study, author experience. (Edwards; Parikh; Cody; Livesay)
- Name doubts before the audience does. Small doses of negative detail raise credibility. Evidence: cited study (Pink). (Parikh; Hauge; Edwards; Livesay)
- On slides, one idea each, sentence headlines and little text help; hard-to-decode visuals make the action itself feel hard. Verbal-plus-visual presentation lifts recall from about 10 to 65 percent. Evidence: studies (Song and Schwarz; Whitehouse et al.). (Edwards; Parikh; McKinsey)
- Short wins at work: one to three minutes, one point, one telling detail. Evidence: author experience. (Callahan; Dolan; Guptara; Livesay)
- Mine stories continuously: log a daily moment, bank fragments with tags, keep ordinary memories, record a voice memo, and avoid inventing on the spot. Evidence: author experience. (Dicks; Dolan; Guptara; Hall)
- Retelling edits memory, and tellers drift toward heroic versions; write it soon and check with witnesses. Evidence: study citations, anecdote. (Biesenbach; Dicks; Bowles)
- Assigned participation lines can displace memory of the story: 75 percent of children's drawings showed the non-participation tale. Evidence: author study of 1,400 students. (Haven)
- Listening precedes telling and drains resistance; a story invites a story back. Evidence: author experience. (Maguire; Simmons; Guptara; Maxwell)

## Mistakes
- Memorizing word for word, which brings blanks and stiffness → learn scenes, first and last lines, and refrains (F181, F089, T320, T321, T326). (Haven; Bowles; Leitman; Dicks; James; Maguire; Maxwell; Parikh; Hall)
- Reading a script or reciting on autopilot → internalize the beats, picture the scene fresh and tell to one person (T330, T384, T454). (Apps; Biesenbach; Maguire)
- Rehearsing in a mirror, only right before showtime, or with relatives as critics → rehearse by telling real listeners and thank them (F181, T326). (Haven; Bowles; James; Dicks)
- Treating a memory slip as a disaster → pause, smile, bridge, or name it briefly (F182, T421, T420). (Haven; Bowles)
- Long introductions, apologies, or announcing the story → keep the intro short, breathe, slide in conversationally (T310, T319, T380, T387). (Haven; Maguire; Dolan; Callahan; Bowles; Leitman)
- Opening with thanks, bio, agenda or housekeeping → open with a story tied to the room, then credentials briefly (F221, T387, T423, T422). (Karia; Parikh; Dolan; Biesenbach)
- Opening with a thesis, hype or the moral → start in a scene as late as possible and leave early (T048, T234, T380). (Dicks; Bowles; James; Biesenbach)
- Telling a chronology of events linked by and → find the moment of change and link beats by but and therefore (F061, F004, T218). (Bowles; Dicks; Leitman; Biesenbach; Apps)
- Explaining the moral, or announcing a realization → stop at the peak; add only a bridge or an invitation in business (T388, T389, T027, F241). (Leitman; Callahan; Dolan; Bowles; James; Maguire; Schwabauer; Buster)
- Tidy or redemptive endings and epilogues → cut closure so the story lingers (T388). (Dicks; Bowles; Buster)
- Making yourself the hero or name-dropping → admit flaws, credit others, lead with the stumble (F259, T486, T042). (Leitman; Dicks; James; Bowles; Hauge; Dolan; Maguire; Biesenbach)
- Making the company, data or product the hero → one affected person is the hero, you are the guide (F288, S120, T082, T589). (Edwards; Hall; Guptara; Maestas; Hauge; Terenzzo; Biesenbach)
- Telling raw, unprocessed pain or playing the victim → apply the readiness test, screen the story, show what you did next (F143, T217, T351). (Bowles; James; Leitman; Dolan; Guptara; Biesenbach; Maestas)
- Fabricating, or telling another person's story as yours → reshape only surface facts, credit the owner, or tell your part (F156, T224, T228, T622). (Leitman; Dolan; Callahan; James; Dicks)
- Detail and cast overload (dates, names, bystanders) → keep one point, group or cut minor people (T218, T221, T222, T293). (Bowles; Leitman; Dolan; Biesenbach; Apps)
- Staying at summary level or hiding in jargon → zoom into one moment with a sensory detail and plain words (T077, T075, T080, T317). (Hall; Karia; Hauge; Guptara; Livesay)
- Rapid, high or theatrical delivery, or a performed storytelling voice → slow down, pause before the turn, speak in your natural register (T311, T329, T319). (Maguire; Livesay; Callahan; Simmons; Dolan)
- Stage mannerisms such as hiding hands, pacing, clutching the mic, rattling pockets → plant your stance, free your hands, travel by purpose (T358, F222, F211). (Haven; Bowles; Parikh; Maxwell; James)
- Canned or put-down humor, or humor from the top → build it from true detail, test the room, target yourself (F207, T432, T236). (Bowles; Cody; James; Dolan; Dicks)
- Leading with facts, lists or fear and guilt → story first, hope-driven, with the data inside it (F255, T259, T481). (Edwards; Simmons; Callahan; Maestas; Buster; Hauge)
- Talking at the audience and ending with any more questions → invite questions early, use names, close with a summary (T295, T413, T304, T306). (Edwards; Parikh; Livesay; Simmons; James)
- Reacting to objections with speed or monologue → pause, slow down, clarify, then answer (F232, T302, T303, T475, T459). (Livesay; Edwards)
- Asking for the sale before trust, or leading with product → origin story, company, then product; match the rung (F227, F228, F230, F233). (Livesay; Edwards; Hauge)
- Slides full of text, topic labels and flashy animation → one idea and a sentence headline per slide, about three minutes each (T103, T382, T405, T392). (Edwards; Parikh; McKinsey; Hall)
- Opening a live deck with an executive summary or a teased cliffhanger → state the destination early for work audiences, keep the summary for the end (T401, T028, F064). (McKinsey; Edwards; Callahan)
- Stories without a point, or non-stories labeled stories → check character, goal, conflict, cause and meaning, and name the relevance up front (F339, F006, S058, T028). (Callahan; Hall; Biesenbach; Edwards; Hauge)
- Going long, repeating one story to a team, or telling old-employer tales → cut to time, finish early, rotate stories (T230, T308, T395, F216). (Dolan; Callahan; James; Parikh)
- Cliché parables, borrowed internet stories, famous names → search for what is already everywhere and mine your own life (T215, T232, S062). (Callahan; Karia; Biesenbach; Leitman; Dolan)
- Props and participation overused → test without props first, use fast cues, and avoid assigned lines when memory of the story matters (T334, F184, T335). (Haven; Dicks)
- Ignoring the room, setup or audience → check sensitivities, set up seating and sightlines, adjust live (F183, T415, T331, T332). (Haven; Apps; Callahan; Cody; James)
- Inventing stories on the spot, or having no bank → bank fragments, voice-record them, keep a set for the questions listeners hold (T177, F237, F196). (Guptara; Dolan; Biesenbach)

## Formats
### Stage personal story (Moth-style, five to ten minutes)
S066, with S068 to judge scale. Find the five-second change and open on its opposite (F061), build three to seven scenes (F089), learn them by telling (F181), and open on So or a plain scene (T380). Test the material with F143 and F156. Stop at the peak without a moral (T388), and prepare for a blank with F182 and T421.

### Workplace anecdote (one to three minutes)
S067, S145. Follow F019 or F006: state the relevance first (T028), tell one incident with one telling detail (T218, T221), pause, then add one inclusive bridge sentence (T389). Keep a sixty-second version ready (T378). Rotate stories (T395) and judge the room first (T332).

### Sales, investor and client pitch
S120, S121, S164, S156. Run trust, like, fit (F228) and order self, company, product (F230). Tell a customer who resembles the buyer (F238, T082), disclose real flaws (T462), pre-empt doubts (T459), and handle objections calmly (F232, T302, T303). Close with a low-pressure ask and hold silence (T503, T475). For investors, use the deck sequence in F235 and a compressed founder arc in F272.

### Keynote or talk with stories placed through it
F209, F221. Open with a story tied to the audience's problem (T387, T423), put short stories between dense sections (T419), run the first interaction early (T413), and close with a full story or a callback. Use stage zones (F222) and voice and body channels (F210, F211, T311, T329).

### Slide-led presentation and bad news
F064 with S099 for unwelcome findings. Write sentence headlines (T103), one message per slide (T382), about three minutes each (T405), move the executive summary to the end (T401), and park detail in an appendix (T231). Use slides as story cues (T392) and keep dialogue open (T295).

### Humor, toasts and occasion speeches
S095, S097, S105, S106. Place laughs with F207 and use T432; stack punch lines with F219. Build tributes on one trait and one story (F223), save the name for last when introducing (T422), handle laughs and tears (T349, T351), and end on heart (T388).
