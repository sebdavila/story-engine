# Story types — data (S081–S091)

## S081 · Data presentation purposes
**Purpose:** Pick how far a data talk goes: describe, adapt, or push to action. **Audience:** Colleagues, clients or executives who receive numbers. **Teller:** Analyst, data team lead or department head.
**Choose when:** The brief arrives as a data task. Edwards names three purposes: Represent (what a data team does and which capabilities help the audience), Report (walk through a dataset around its single most significant fact), Recommend (propose an action). Most Represent and Report briefs quietly want a recommendation, so check for one. Lo Duca grades depth: Inform (raw insight), Communicate (insight and visuals adapted to the audience), Inspire (adds a persuasive message that motivates action and retelling).
**Shape:** Represent: capability, then the value it creates for this audience. Report: lead with the most significant fact, then supporting numbers (F082 for where the conclusion goes). Recommend or Inspire: findings, then the action, using F035 or F064. Choose Communicate at minimum whenever the audience is not technical.
**Sources:** Edwards; Lo Duca

## S082 · Journey versus stranger data plots
**Purpose:** Frame what the numbers say as one of two plots. **Audience:** Leadership or teams reviewing results. **Teller:** Analyst or business owner.
**Choose when:** Use A person goes on a journey when the company chose a new initiative, product or process and the data reports what happened along the way. Use A stranger comes to town when an outside disruption (a competitor, a technology, an economic shift) hit and the data shows its impact and the company's reaction (Edwards).
**Shape:** Journey: the company sets out, meets obstacles, results arrive (F060 fits). Stranger: the disruption arrives, the data shows the impact, then the response. Either way, the plot choice decides which numbers open the story.
**Sources:** Edwards

## S083 · Static versus dynamic data story
**Purpose:** Decide whether the reader follows a fixed path or explores. **Audience:** Readers of reports, dashboards or articles. **Teller:** Data author.
**Choose when:** Static (Lo Duca): fixed charts, the author controls the path from context to insight to call to action; suits print, simple reports and one clear message. Dynamic: viewer-light interaction (tooltips, hover highlights) or viewer-driven interaction (sliders, dropdowns, click filters) for large datasets or personal comparisons, within boundaries the author sets.
**Shape:** Static: context, insight, call to action in author order. Dynamic: keep the same three beats as the default view, then let controls open comparisons without breaking the main message.
**Sources:** Lo Duca

## S084 · Story that humanizes a data point
**Purpose:** Make a statistic memorable. **Audience:** Listeners who would forget the bare number. **Teller:** Presenter, manager or researcher.
**Choose when:** A metric needs a human frame. Guptara's version narrates an experiment or economic study with concrete imagery so the result sticks. Bowles extends it: a roughly three-minute meeting opener tied to the work, meeting theme or company values; a failing-forward story for onboarding; or a human frame around a presentation metric.
**Shape:** Tell the study or the person first, in scenes, then reveal the number as the outcome (F087 works inductively). Close by tying it back to the decision or value at hand.
**Sources:** Guptara; Bowles

## S085 · Data protagonist types
**Purpose:** Give a dataset someone to follow. **Audience:** General readers of data stories. **Teller:** Data storyteller or journalist.
**Choose when:** A dataset feels abstract. Feigenbaum offers four protagonists: a person, an event, a place, or a composite character.
**Shape:** Pick the protagonist, then let the data points become what happens to that person, event or place (F060 for the change). Use a composite when no single case represents the pattern.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## S086 · Stories with data versus about data
**Purpose:** Separate reporting findings from investigating how data came to be. **Audience:** Readers of data journalism, research or advocacy. **Teller:** Analyst, researcher or journalist.
**Choose when:** Stories with data communicate what a dataset shows. Stories about data investigate the systems, categories, labor and politics that produced the data (Feigenbaum, after Gray). Choose the second when the origin of the numbers is part of the point.
**Shape:** With data: finding, evidence, implication. About data: who collected it, which categories were chosen, who was counted or missed, then what that means for any finding.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## S087 · Illness narrative structures
**Purpose:** Represent health experiences without false neatness. **Audience:** Patients, caregivers, public readers. **Teller:** Health communicator or data storyteller.
**Choose when:** Telling data or personal accounts about illness. Frank's three structures: Restitution (treatment restores the prior normal, common in medicine ads), Quest (illness transforms the person into an enlightened new normal), Chaos (disjointed, no resolution) (Feigenbaum).
**Shape:** Restitution: illness, treatment, return. Quest: illness, struggle, new self. Chaos: fragments with no arc; do not force an ending. Pick the one that matches lived reality.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## S088 · Grid map versus story map
**Purpose:** Choose between an objective map and a lived one. **Audience:** Map and data readers. **Teller:** Data storyteller or cartographer.
**Choose when:** A grid map is an abstract geometric mesh for objective navigation. A story map is subjective space shaped by a person or culture moving through it, favoring events over scale (Feigenbaum, after MacFarlane). Choose the story map when experience matters more than position.
**Shape:** Story map: mark the places where things happened, order them by the journey, label with events rather than coordinates.
**Sources:** Feigenbaum

## S089 · Heritage and Historical Narrative
**Purpose:** Bring past places, traditions and eras to life without erasing the record. **Audience:** Visitors, learners, communities. **Teller:** Curator, guide or historian.
**Choose when:** Cultural Heritage reanimates monuments, sites and intangible traditions, including underserved adult history. Historical Narratives dramatize past eras to show human choices, marking documented record apart from speculation (Paulsen).
**Shape:** Heritage: site or tradition, the people who lived it, why it matters now. Historical: a choice in its era, consequences, labeled evidence versus conjecture.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## S090 · Factual Reporting Narrative
**Purpose:** Inform through verifiable events. **Audience:** Viewers, citizens, readers. **Teller:** Documentary maker or news service.
**Choose when:** Documentary shapes verifiable events and testimony into arcs that inform or provoke inquiry. News balances accuracy with relevance so citizens can orient themselves in unfolding events (Paulsen).
**Shape:** Documentary: question, testimony and evidence, deepening arc. News: what happened, why it matters to this audience, what is still unknown.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## S091 · Inductive status report
**Purpose:** Report monthly progress or KPIs. **Audience:** Managers and stakeholders. **Teller:** Team lead or project owner.
**Choose when:** Sections have loose dependencies and can be presented in any order (Parikh). Vertical, non-sequential structure.
**Shape:** KPI summary statements first, then key messages per area (F082 covers inductive conclusion placement). Each area stands alone, so order by audience interest.
**Sources:** Parikh
