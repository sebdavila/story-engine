# Story types — persuasion (S111–S125)

## S111 · Who-I-am story
**Purpose:** Earn trust before you try to influence, by showing a three-dimensional person: where your values or work ethic came from, a past flaw, an early struggle, a formative mentor. **Audience:** New teams, skeptical or hostile rooms, prospects. **Teller:** The seller, leader or executive, about themselves.
**Choose when:** The listeners do not yet know you, or are hostile (a turnaround town hall, before the plan is shown). Pick the flavor: origin of passion or work ethic for introductions (Livesay); a likeable background (who story) or a motive, even a plainly financial one (why story) (Guptara); a specific past mistake and its lesson to show strength through vulnerability (Simmons); early struggles, pride and hopes before the business plan (Biesenbach).
**Shape:** A brief anecdote. For a flaw, name the specific mistake, then what it taught; check readiness with F143. Versions and development: F106. Career-arc variant: F338. The motive-only variant is S112.
**Sources:** Livesay; Guptara; Simmons; Biesenbach

## S112 · Why-I-am-here stories
**Purpose:** Remove suspicion of a hidden agenda by stating your stake or proving your motive through sacrifice. **Audience:** Listeners who suspect exploitation: buyers, donors, followers. **Teller:** The person asking for cooperation.
**Choose when:** Selling, fundraising or leading, and the audience may wonder what you really gain.
**Shape:** Say first what you stand to gain, money or ambition included, then what is in it for them (Simmons). Or tell a story of a real sacrifice you made that shows the motive is not only self-interest. Fits the trust step of F228 and the speaker-first part of F230.
**Sources:** Simmons

## S113 · Signature and connection story
**Purpose:** Build rapport by showing character and how you resemble the audience. **Audience:** Stakeholders, a room at the start of a talk or meeting. **Teller:** The professional, about themselves.
**Choose when:** Opening a talk, pitch, meeting or new relationship.
**Shape:** An autobiographical story about a mentor, challenge, accomplishment or failure, kept in several tailored versions (Forman). Open with it and make the similarity to the audience explicit (Callahan). Build it with F106; check readiness with F143.
**Sources:** Forman; Callahan

## S114 · Career self-introduction story
**Purpose:** Turn tell-me-about-yourself into a short story that links your path into a coherent arc and shows character and fit. **Audience:** Interviewers and networking contacts. **Teller:** The candidate or professional.
**Choose when:** Interviews and networking, or when explaining résumé transitions.
**Shape:** Edwards maps it onto F224 (SUPERB): start from experience you share with the listener, treat your challenges and false starts as universal, state the lesson in general terms rather than naming colleagues, and end with mutual benefit. Bowles adds single problem-solving stories that prove character and cultural fit. Five-beat career structure: F338.
**Sources:** Edwards; Bowles

## S115 · One-sentence what-I-do story
**Purpose:** Replace a name-title-company introduction for clarity and referrals. **Audience:** Networking contacts and listeners of quick verbal pitches.
**Choose when:** Any quick introduction where a job title would be forgettable.
**Shape:** One spoken sentence naming a client or a specific problem, what you did, and the measurable result (Hauge). Guptara's version states the customer problem you solve as bait, to make you relevant. No F needed.
**Sources:** Hauge; Guptara

## S116 · How-my-way-works story
**Purpose:** Remove barriers to working together by letting people feel how your process works. **Audience:** Prospects not yet clients.
**Choose when:** Barriers stand in the way of people working with you.
**Shape:** Explain how your process works through an experience or an analogy (Guptara). Analogy support: F050.
**Sources:** Guptara

## S117 · Light self-revealing icebreaker story
**Purpose:** Break tension and invite the other person to share. **Audience:** Feeling-type buyers (Livesay), first meetings and social settings (Bowles). **Teller:** You, about a small embarrassment.
**Choose when:** The room is stiff, or at a first date-like meeting.
**Shape:** A short true story where you tried to look cool and ended up looking foolish, kept lightly vulnerable (Livesay); a brief greatest-hits story that makes the other person want to reciprocate (Bowles). Place the humor with F207. Keep it short and let the other person answer.
**Sources:** Livesay; Bowles

## S118 · Vulnerable Underdog Story
**Purpose:** Make the audience root for a real human instead of a polished logo or expert. **Audience:** Readers of About pages, podcast listeners, stage audiences. **Teller:** The founder or storyteller, first person.
**Choose when:** You need friend-like trust (Mastropierro) or a stage story built on shortcomings (Leitman).
**Shape:** Show the flaws or uphill fight, name the nemesis (a person, an institution or a thing, Leitman), and for web copy end on the success (Mastropierro). Build empathy with F048; test that the wound has healed into a scar with F143.
**Sources:** Mastropierro; Leitman

## S119 · Application story
**Purpose:** Set you apart from other applicants by showing what was once on the line and how you came through. **Audience:** College and grant reviewers. **Teller:** The applicant.
**Choose when:** A high-stakes application asks for a personal story.
**Shape:** One personal moment with real stakes, then how you came through. The turn can follow F060.
**Sources:** Bowles

## S120 · Customer success story
**Purpose:** Make an intangible offer concrete: a named customer with a painful problem reaches a better result, so the prospect pictures the same. **Audience:** Prospects asking will this work for me. **Teller:** The seller or marketer (guide role), or the customer in their own voice.
**Choose when:** Selling, or building sales and landing pages.
**Shape:** Core pattern F238 and the end-user version F267; choose the hero with F153 and cast them with F288. Variants: a mid-project crisis for drama (Livesay); six fixed beats with the speaker as guide (Guptara); before, during, after in a few sentences (Butler; Mastropierro: problem, discovery, impact for reviews); the seller-told Value Story, where customer is knight, problem is dragon and product is the sword, versus the customer-told Customer Story in first-person raw detail (Hall); a hero's journey case study (Snow; Lazauskas); a human Meet-Bob story built from a written case (Callahan); six-step testimonial of struggle, discovery, process, obstacle, win, aftermath, or a loved one as hero (Hauge); before-and-after of a named customer to show an intangible benefit (Simmons); a care-cycle story built by listening to the user's pain (Forman).
**Sources:** Livesay; Guptara; Butler; Hall; Callahan; Hauge; Simmons; Snow; Lazauskas; Mastropierro; Forman

## S121 · Sales stories
**Purpose:** Commercial story whose outcome, the purchase, is fixed in advance. **Audience:** Buyers.
**Choose when:** The ending is already decided; effort goes to the other elements.
**Shape:** Since the transformation is predetermined, put the work into passion, hero and antagonist (Maxwell); F060 holds Maxwell's five-element model. Goal-based selection of sales stories: F152.
**Sources:** Maxwell

## S122 · Contrasting pair story
**Purpose:** Show a failing path beside a succeeding one so the method and the cost of mistakes are visible. **Audience:** Buyers choosing a method. **Teller:** The seller.
**Choose when:** You want to teach a system while steering buyers from common errors, or prove you beat rivals without boasting.
**Shape:** Two characters run step by step side by side: one repeats common mistakes, the other follows your system (Hauge, crediting Chaperon). Variant: put the rival attempts inside one hero's earlier failures at the uncertainty step of the six-step success story F238 and say why they fell short. Rival comparison: F365.
**Sources:** Hauge

## S123 · Common-Enemy Story
**Purpose:** Bond teller and audience against a shared opponent that harms the customer. **Audience:** Customers, readers. **Teller:** The brand or writer.
**Choose when:** You want camaraderie and to be seen as the customer's advocate.
**Shape:** Identify the enemy that writer and reader oppose together and position the brand as the customer's advocate. Naming the antagonist: F276; customer as hero: F288.
**Sources:** Mastropierro

## S124 · Pre-empt story
**Purpose:** Defuse an unspoken objection, rumor or negative attitude without confronting it. **Audience:** Doubters. **Teller:** The speaker or proposer.
**Choose when:** Open confrontation would provoke defense. If you can name the doubt openly, use S125.
**Shape:** Tell an indirect story that disarms the objection without open confrontation. Related: F281, F232.
**Sources:** Guptara

## S125 · Naming the audience's objection first
**Purpose:** Voice the doubt or suspicion the audience holds, about the issue or about you, to disarm resistance without a debate. **Audience:** Skeptics, for example sales reps wary of an executive.
**Choose when:** Facing skeptics before you make your case.
**Shape:** Tell a brief, humble story that states the main doubt, such as being an out-of-touch executive (Biesenbach), validating unspoken fears up front (Simmons); the common-ground version shows you once walked in their shoes. Then present the message. Related: F233 (feel, felt, found), F237.
**Sources:** Simmons; Biesenbach
