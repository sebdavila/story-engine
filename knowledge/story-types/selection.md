# Story types — selection (S066–S080)

## S066 · True personal story built on inner change
**Purpose:** Create deep connection through a true first-person story whose meaning lives in how the teller changed. **Audience:** Listeners at a stage, a workshop, a team or a family gathering. **Teller:** The person who lived it, or a family member passing on a family tale.
**Choose when:** You have a real episode with genuine stakes and vulnerability and want the audience to feel it without being told the lesson.
**Shape:** Anchor the story in one five-second moment of change and build backward so the beginning is the opposite of the end (F061); raise stakes with F062 and drive scenes with F004. Include characters, senses, dialogue, conflict and a beginning, middle and end, so it is not gossip, a news report or a summary (Apps). Let the meaning emerge; it needs no explanation and makes the strongest closer (James). Real stakes and honest vulnerability separate it from bragging or party tales (Lazer). Family stories are an easy starter for practising book-free telling (Haven). For a stage slot, expect a timed, unscripted arc of about 5 minutes at a StorySLAM or 10 to 12 at a mainstage (Bowles). For its smaller cousin see S067.
**Sources:** Dicks; James; Apps; Lazer; Haven; Bowles

## S067 · Anecdote
**Purpose:** Humanize the teller, entertain or make a single point. **Audience:** Social groups, meetings, talk openers. **Teller:** Anyone with a short real incident.
**Choose when:** The incident is amusing or illustrative but has no inner transformation or deep stakes.
**Shape:** Keep it short: under three minutes (Bowles), under one minute for an ordinary struggle at work (Guptara). Because the meaning depends on outside context, add brief commentary, especially when using it to open a talk or nudge a brand message (James). Do not lean on it for lasting impact; anecdotes fade quickly (Dicks). Treat it as raw seed that can be dug into a full story, S066 (Bowles). Two sub-kinds carry rules (Dicks):
- Drinking stories: nobody cares as much as you do; they never impress those you want to impress; you have at most three good ones; they fail in daytime and at work; after 40 they sound sad, unless you are past 70.
- Vacation stories: no one wants a travelogue of food and scenery; tell one only if a real transformation happened that would survive without the exotic backdrop.
**Sources:** Dicks; James; Guptara; Bowles

## S068 · Big versus little personal stories
**Purpose:** Choose the scale of a personal story. **Audience:** Varies with scale. **Teller:** The person who lived it.
**Choose when:** You are deciding between a rare high-stakes event and an everyday incident.
**Shape:** Big stories (crashes, arrests, births, weddings) are harder to connect with; shrink them to one small universal feeling inside the spectacle (Dicks), and tell them with careful timing, framing and safe listeners (Maguire). Little stories (a remark at dinner, holding a sleepy child) need no drama and connect deeply because the emotion is recognized (Dicks); they suit icebreakers and quick workplace or family points (Maguire). For work, check intimacy with S075. Build either with F061.
**Sources:** Dicks; Maguire

## S069 · Achievement and adventure stories
**Purpose:** Tell a success or physical feat without sounding like bragging. **Audience:** Interviewers, application readers, listeners. **Teller:** The achiever.
**Choose when:** You need a victory story, for example for an interview or application, or you climbed or crossed something.
**Shape:** Undercut the triumph: minimize the feat and cast yourself as an underdog taking small steps. For an adventure, reframe the story around what changed inside you instead of the external feat. Use F061 to find the moment of change.
**Sources:** Dicks

## S070 · Positive versus negative outcome stories
**Purpose:** Teach what to do or what to avoid. **Audience:** Talk audiences. **Teller:** A speaker.
**Choose when:** You are choosing the outcome of the story's central struggle.
**Shape:** Positive: the protagonist overcomes the conflict and changes for the better; it teaches what to do, inspires without preaching, and leaves listeners high. Negative: the protagonist fails and ends worse off; it teaches what to avoid but leaves listeners low, so prefer the positive version whenever you can (Karia). Map either on F021.
**Sources:** Karia

## S071 · Life-writing forms by scope
**Purpose:** Pick the right form for writing about a life. **Audience:** Readers of a life account. **Teller:** An author writing about their own or another life.
**Choose when:** You are planning a written life story.
**Shape:** Reminiscence is a raw remembered fragment, such as walking to a one-room school; use it as starting material. Autobiography or biography covers a life chronologically from birth to now; it is vast and tends to become a dry summary in beginner hands. Memoir picks specific themes, incidents or relationships that shaped who the author became; beginners do best here (Apps). Build each memoir chapter as an S066 story.
**Sources:** Apps

## S072 · Traditional tales and myths
**Purpose:** Explain beliefs or carry values. **Audience:** Listeners in a culture or a brand community. **Teller:** A teller retelling an anonymous or archetypal tale.
**Choose when:** You want a public-domain tale with flexible wording and a strong plot.
**Shape:** Keep plots strong and the cast lean, and reword freely. Haven's kinds: anecdote (brief human-interest incident), epic (long heroic poem), fable (universal truth, often talking animals or objects), fairy tale (supernatural events, whimsical or moral), folk tale (anonymous tale of common people), legend (past story taken as historical, unverified), myth (unknown-origin tale explaining beliefs, rites or nature). Quesenberry uses myth for a tale explaining a cultural practice, belief or origin. For a hero structure see F005.
**Sources:** Quesenberry; Haven

## S073 · Nonfiction incident or booktalk
**Purpose:** Make nonfiction vivid and prompt more reading. **Audience:** Students, library or classroom listeners. **Teller:** A teacher, librarian or speaker.
**Choose when:** You want to introduce a history, science or art subject, or a book.
**Shape:** Pick one real episode from a historical, scientific or artistic figure, or one complete scene lifted from a longer book, and tell it as a story so it stands alone and leaves people wanting the rest.
**Sources:** Haven

## S074 · Two-minute pitch to curators
**Purpose:** Pitch a story to those who select tellers. **Audience:** Curators and producers. **Teller:** The prospective storyteller.
**Choose when:** A show asks what your story is.
**Shape:** Summarize in about two minutes, spoilers included: state the stakes, the arc and the ending. No cliffhangers.
**Sources:** Bowles

## S075 · Intimacy levels
**Purpose:** Decide how private a personal story is and where it can be told. **Audience:** Work audiences, friends, close circle. **Teller:** Anyone.
**Choose when:** You are screening a personal story for work.
**Shape:** Sort into three tiers. Front porch: safe stories you can tell anyone, anytime. Kitchen: personal stories for an inner circle of trusted friends. Bedroom: highly intimate stories that make workplace listeners uncomfortable and should stay private. At work tell front-porch stories only.
**Sources:** Callahan

## S076 · Funny release story
**Purpose:** Release tension through laughter. **Audience:** Any group. **Teller:** The person who lived it.
**Choose when:** The room is tense or you want lightness. This is Maguire's Ha-Ha type; the four reaction types are S076 to S079.
**Shape:** Aim the whole story at the laugh. See F207 for placing humor.
**Sources:** Maguire

## S077 · Insight or twist story
**Purpose:** Surprise and delight the mind. **Audience:** Any group. **Teller:** The person who lived it.
**Choose when:** You want an aha reaction. This is Maguire's AHA type.
**Shape:** Aim the story at an insight or a twist; see F063 for engineered surprise and F044 for a four-part twist.
**Sources:** Maguire

## S078 · Tender empathy story
**Purpose:** Evoke empathy and resonance. **Audience:** Trusted listeners. **Teller:** The person who lived it.
**Choose when:** You want deep feeling. This is Maguire's AHHH type.
**Shape:** Aim the story at tenderness; take care with timing and safe listeners (see S068).
**Sources:** Maguire

## S079 · Reverent or transcendent story
**Purpose:** Move the spirit. **Audience:** Listeners open to reverence. **Teller:** The person who lived it.
**Choose when:** You want a sense of awe or the sacred. This is Maguire's AMEN type.
**Shape:** Aim the story at reverence or transcendence rather than laughter or surprise.
**Sources:** Maguire

## S080 · Teaching and know-how stories
**Purpose:** Pass on a skill or tacit know-how so people can reason when rules run out. **Audience:** Learners, teams across silos. **Teller:** An experienced practitioner.
**Choose when:** The skill is hard to codify, or practice needs to spread across departments.
**Shape:** Embed the skill in a story of how and why, with believable complexity, people and outcomes, so learners can reason on their own once the rules run out (Simmons). Use it to move practical know-how across silos and to capture informal, uncodified expertise (Salmon). Salmon also uses these stories to show where execution lags, the know-how gap.
**Sources:** Simmons; Salmon
