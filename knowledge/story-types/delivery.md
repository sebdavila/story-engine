# Story types — delivery (S092–S110)

## S092 · Literary tale told verbatim
**Purpose:** Deliver an authored story or narrative poem whose exact wording is part of the art. **Audience:** Listeners at a reading, recital or school event. **Teller:** A performer who reads or memorizes the text.
**Choose when:** The piece is a literary tale, a story-length poem, a ballad or a book excerpt, and paraphrasing would damage it.
**Shape:** No F needed; the author's structure stays intact. Decide up front between reading aloud and memorizing word for word, then deliver the full emotional inflection rather than a flat recitation. For an excerpt, pick one complete scene so it stands alone.
**Sources:** Haven

## S093 · Jump tale
**Purpose:** Startle the audience for fun. **Audience:** Groups who enjoy suspense, often children. **Teller:** A live teller who controls volume and pauses.
**Choose when:** You want a ghost or suspense story with a physical payoff.
**Shape:** Follow F086 (expectation, tension, revelation): keep the voice hushed and slow, let tension climb through whispers, then drop a sudden loud shout on the final beat.
**Sources:** Haven

## S094 · Participation story
**Purpose:** Make listeners co-performers. **Audience:** Seated groups, or volunteers brought on stage. **Teller:** A facilitator comfortable cueing a crowd.
**Choose when:** You want energy, the group is willing to join in, and the plot can be simple.
**Shape:** Two options. (1) Seated: build the tale around a repeated refrain and teach the audience a sound or gesture to answer it each time it returns. (2) Cast of thousands: use a plot with many small roles, call volunteers up, and let them improvise the parts while you narrate. Keep the plot simple enough to survive the interruptions. For degrees of involvement see F184.
**Sources:** Haven

## S095 · Honoring stories: toast and eulogy
**Purpose:** Celebrate or remember another person. **Audience:** Guests at a celebration, a ribbon-cutting or a memorial. **Teller:** A friend, colleague or relative; the honoree, not the teller, is the hero.
**Choose when:** You must speak about someone else in the room or for someone who is gone.
**Shape:**
- Toast: one to three minutes. Pick one scene that shows an affectionate trait of the person or team, tell it vividly, and finish on a clean exit line such as "Cheers!". No personal transformation arc is needed. See F223 for the one-trait, one-story build.
- Eulogy: anchor the grief in specific, idiosyncratic details that bring the person back to life in the room. Optionally organize everything around a single theme word, for example "grateful". (Bowles)
**Sources:** Bowles

## S096 · Everyday transition tales for children
**Purpose:** Move a resistant child through a chore or change of activity without nagging. **Audience:** A young child, one on one. **Teller:** A parent or carer improvising on the spot.
**Choose when:** The child resists walking, drinking, brushing hair, stopping play, or is overwhelmed in a noisy shop.
**Shape:** No formal F; improvise a tiny quest in a few lines. For physical transitions, invite the child onto a magic carpet or into a mini-mission, such as helping a lost wolf cub get home or collecting special dinosaur water. For errands, recast the shop as a treasure cave, name the items on the list as jewels to collect while leaving the rest for other adventurers, and put the child in charge of the list.
**Sources:** Miller

## S097 · Commencement address
**Purpose:** Send graduates off with one original idea. **Audience:** The graduates only, never the parents. **Teller:** An invited speaker.
**Choose when:** You are asked to give a graduation speech.
**Shape:** Build around one specific, granular takeaway and a story that earns it (see F061 for the moment of change). Leave out self-praise, weather remarks, dictionary quotes and predictions about the future. Speak to the graduates directly, not about them.
**Sources:** Dicks

## S098 · Modular stump-speech obstacle stories
**Purpose:** Fill a long speaking slot with reusable career stories. **Audience:** Conference or campaign audiences. **Teller:** A speaker with several career turning points.
**Choose when:** You need a talk of half an hour or more and want pieces you can reorder or swap.
**Shape:** Prepare several five-minute modules, each with four beats: the goal you wanted, the goal denied, the obstacle you overcame, and the afterglow. Use F006 to map goal, obstacle and outcome in each module, then string the modules together for the engagement.
**Sources:** James

## S099 · Bad-news presentation
**Purpose:** Present unfavourable findings and move the room toward solutions. **Audience:** Senior stakeholders reading red indicators or audit results. **Teller:** A presenter acting as a neutral facilitator.
**Choose when:** The findings are negative and there is a risk of finger-pointing.
**Shape:** Tell it as a high-tension story but keep the tone neutral so blame does not become the topic, then pivot the discussion to what happens next. For the sequence of situation, complication and resolution use F064; for owning failure openly see F329.
**Sources:** Parikh

## S100 · Entertainment and Escapism
**Purpose:** Give pleasure, wonder or a short mental break. **Audience:** Consumers, followers, players. **Teller:** A brand or creator.
**Choose when:** The main goal is immersion or delight, not information or a sale.
**Shape:** Make funny, dramatic, surprising or pop-culture content, or a game, whose value is the experience itself. Vaynerchuk applies this to social posts and games. Use F086 for surprise and F207 for where to place humor. Pair with the other content types in S101, S103 and S104.
**Sources:** Paulsen; Vaynerchuk

## S101 · Engineered Shareable Short-Form
**Purpose:** Get a short piece passed on, remixed and answered. **Audience:** Social media communities. **Teller:** A brand or creator.
**Choose when:** You want reach and community response and can design for it instead of hoping.
**Shape:** Build a short hook on purpose so it invites sharing, remixing and replies. Creek separates accidental captures of a freak or funny moment from videos engineered on his five planks to trigger sharing; do not count on luck. Test the hook against F370 (relatability, novelty, tension, ease).
**Sources:** Paulsen; Creek

## S102 · Urban Legend
**Purpose:** Study or recognize folklore that spreads without an author. **Audience:** Communities passing it person to person. **Teller:** Anonymous, many tellers.
**Choose when:** You are analysing how a participatory tale spreads, or warning how unchecked belief in a story can lead to real-world harm.
**Shape:** Treat it as a case to examine rather than a template to produce: a tale moves virally through a community, gains belief and finally spills into actions.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## S103 · Human Connection Content
**Purpose:** Satisfy people's wish to connect with other people. **Audience:** Followers of a brand or person. **Teller:** A real person speaking conversationally.
**Choose when:** You want rapport, not promotion.
**Shape:** Post conversational updates, replies and relatable everyday moments. No formal arc is required. Balance with S104 and S100.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk

## S104 · Utility and Editorial Content
**Purpose:** Help customers solve a problem and own the audience relationship. **Audience:** Customers and prospects. **Teller:** A brand.
**Choose when:** People have practical questions, or you want a long-term publishing presence.
**Shape:** Two scales. Short: how-to videos, infographics, reviews and practical advice. Long: editorial pieces such as videos, reviews, publications and guides, produced openly by the brand as a media company and run alongside daily micro-content. For data-driven versions see F160.
**Sources:** Vaynerchuk

## S105 · Observational stand-up
**Purpose:** Spark instant recognition and create inside jokes. **Audience:** A room sharing the same annoyances or workplace quirks. **Teller:** A speaker offering personal feelings.
**Choose when:** You want to warm a room with a shared irritation.
**Shape:** Share a personal feeling about a common annoyance and give it a fresh angle so listeners think "me too". See F207 for placement.
**Sources:** Cody

## S106 · Anecdotal stand-up
**Purpose:** Humanize a leader and win emotional investment in a pitch. **Audience:** Investors, clients, staff. **Teller:** The leader, as the underdog.
**Choose when:** You need an opening or middle moment that makes you likable.
**Shape:** Tell a true underdog, fish-out-of-water or embarrassing story about yourself, with a punch line in every beat rather than only at the end. Link to the pitch with F035 or F235.
**Sources:** Cody

## S107 · Put-down humour
**Purpose:** Puncture hierarchy or sharpen a bold brand voice. **Audience:** A room where the target and everyone else are in on the joke. **Teller:** Someone with enough standing to tease upward.
**Choose when:** The target is willing and the relationship is warm, or the brand voice is deliberately bold on social media.
**Shape:** Tease up toward someone with status, in good nature, never down at the weak. If the target or the room is not in on it, drop the joke.
**Sources:** Cody

## S108 · Short-form improv games
**Purpose:** Train single skills such as listening and building. **Audience:** Teams in a workshop. **Teller:** A facilitator running exercises.
**Choose when:** The team needs to practise one skill in isolation.
**Shape:** Run quick rule-bound games such as Yes and..., One-Word Story, First Letter Last Letter, Excuses or Cocktail Party, one skill per game. For a full workshop sequence see F220.
**Sources:** Cody

## S109 · Long-form improv scenes
**Purpose:** Co-author messages or pivot live when the script collapses. **Audience:** Teams building a message together. **Teller:** A group improvising.
**Choose when:** The team must create or rescue a message in the moment.
**Shape:** Start from one central idea and let an unscripted scene grow through beginning, complication and resolution. Use the result as draft messaging. See F277 for improv-led co-creation.
**Sources:** Cody

## S110 · Three-thread improv pieces
**Purpose:** Model interconnected, multi-thread storytelling. **Audience:** Teams learning to link several stories. **Teller:** A group improvising.
**Choose when:** You want a company to see how several storylines can work together.
**Shape:** Take a single-word prompt, improvise three acts, and weave three storylines together so they touch. Compare with S109.
**Sources:** Cody
