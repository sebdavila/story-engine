# Story types — brand (S156–S171)

## S156 · Origin and founding story
**Purpose:** Show how a company, founder or career began, so people see its purpose and values, feel the brand is human, and share an insider identity. **Audience:** Investors, customers, prospective hires, new hires, press and influencers, merged teams. **Teller:** Founder, or any employee who tells it in the third person (Hall).
**Choose when:** Onboarding (Edwards; Terenzzo), pitching to press or ambassadors (Rodriguez), filling an About page (Mastropierro), introducing yourself in an interview or networking pitch (Leitman), differentiating from copycats or recruiting backers (Hall), or holding a merged or changing organization together (Forman; Terenzzo).
**Shape:** Use a quest line (Edwards; see F005): an unmet need, early obstacles, temporary failures, a lasting lesson. Center on one accurate founding aha moment plus what the founders gave up, not a decades-long chronology (Terenzzo). Mine the founder for childhood roots, the frustration that made them look for a better way, or a hard early struggle (Hall). Add what the superpower is and whom it was chosen to help (Butler). Tilt the emotional emphasis toward the audience in the room (Hall). Variants: a one-paragraph tribute or multi-stage history (Mastropierro); a short likeable career or client-help version (Leitman); rebirth moments, disasters averted, triumphs and changes of direction drawn from archives (Callahan; Forman).
**Sources:** Edwards; Rodriguez; Quesenberry; Mastropierro; Leitman; Terenzzo; Butler; Hall; Callahan; Forman

## S157 · Mission or vision story
**Purpose:** Make the organization the hero of an ongoing quest to change customers' lives and benefit society. **Audience:** Employees first, then the market. **Teller:** Leader or company voice.
**Choose when:** You need internal purpose and external differentiation, and the company really is on a continuing mission to create or reinvent something (Edwards).
**Shape:** Quest structure (F005 as the frame): the company is the protagonist, the goal is a standing change in customers' lives, and the stakes are society's benefit. Keep it ongoing rather than resolved.
**Sources:** Edwards

## S158 · Overcoming-the-monster organizational story
**Purpose:** Show how the organization faced a daunting threat so others can reuse the lessons. **Audience:** Teams needing resilience and confidence. **Teller:** A leader or the person who lived it.
**Choose when:** The group needs courage after rejection or a hard period, and you can name tactics and growth gained under pressure (Edwards).
**Shape:** Threat, the organization's response under pressure, then the transferable lessons and personal growth (see F060). Avoid boasting and self-pity; the lesson is the payoff.
**Sources:** Edwards

## S159 · Values in action story
**Purpose:** Make a value or purpose concrete by showing a specific tough moment when someone lived it, so it replaces wall posters and policy manuals. **Audience:** Employees, stakeholders and values-aligned customers or clients. **Teller:** Leader for purpose stories (Hall); front-line employee for quality stories (Biesenbach).
**Choose when:** A value is abstract, you need to prove authenticity, align a team on a standard, steer a sensitive course correction, or attract value-aligned clients (Edwards; Hall; Callahan; Sandler; Simmons; Biesenbach).
**Shape:** Pick one real person in a hard situation and one fork in the road (Edwards; Simmons). The value beats a rule, costs money or demands short-term sacrifice (Callahan; Edwards). Hall's purpose variant has the leader share a personal lesson that exposes vulnerability, often drawn from outside work, which parallels precisely what the team must accept. Callahan's engagement variants show who benefits (purpose), a small daily win (progress) or leader reliability over self-interest (trust). Salmon frames these as value-transmission and identity stories of who we are. A front-line employee's pride can rally staff or reassure buyers (Biesenbach).
**Sources:** Edwards; Hall; Callahan; Salmon; Sandler; Simmons; Biesenbach

## S160 · Employee hero stories of going beyond
**Purpose:** Model company values and build pride through employees who go far beyond the job for a customer. **Audience:** Employees, including acquired staff. **Teller:** Peers and managers, circulated internally.
**Choose when:** You want a service culture others copy, need to unify a dispersed workforce, or must integrate newly acquired employees (Forman; Maxwell).
**Shape:** Frontline person, unexpected customer problem, extraordinary discretionary effort, result that shows the value (Forman; Maxwell). Let stories start at the grassroots and circulate regularly (Maxwell). Leaders modeling integrity can be told the same way (Forman).
**Sources:** Forman; Maxwell

## S161 · Mentor story
**Purpose:** Convey the philosophy, ethics or mission behind your work. **Audience:** Prospects and clients who need to understand your outlook. **Teller:** You or your company.
**Choose when:** The people who shaped your approach matter more than your product.
**Shape:** Star a mentor, historical figure or inspiring model who never used your product; show what they did and the principle it carries, then link the principle to your work (Hauge).
**Sources:** Hauge

## S162 · Brand story
**Purpose:** Make identity and promise concrete so loyalty rests on more than features. **Audience:** Customers, employees, the public. **Teller:** The organization, through real people.
**Choose when:** You need to differentiate and build trust beyond product specs (Paulsen; Forman).
**Shape:** Anchor the abstract promise in real actions with human protagonists, optionally framed by a tagline (Forman). Maxwell offers two modes. Logo brands stress security and consistency, with an authoritative spokesperson as seal of approval. Lifestyle brands stress community that employees and customers live and co-author. Paulsen ties it to emotional expression of identity, purpose and values.
**Sources:** Paulsen; Forman; Maxwell

## S163 · Product and aspirational identity story
**Purpose:** Raise perceived value by telling what goes into a product, what it achieves, or who its user wants to become. **Audience:** Buyers, including different price tiers. **Teller:** Brand.
**Choose when:** You want to support price segmentation or lift how valuable the product feels (Guptara).
**Shape:** Choose one: the making of the product, its outcomes, or the identity of the aspirational user; tell it through a concrete person or process.
**Sources:** Guptara

## S164 · Four essential business stories
**Purpose:** A starter set covering the core business narratives. **Audience:** Customers, staff, investors. **Teller:** Founder, leader or team.
**Choose when:** You are planning a story library and need to know which stories to build first (James, citing Hall).
**Shape:** Four stories: value (proves impact of the product or service), founder (revives the pioneering spirit; see S156), purpose (clarifies priorities; see S159), customer (spotlights one person affected by the work).
**Sources:** James; Hall

## S165 · B2B shared-values story
**Purpose:** Build trust in a professional pitch by revealing shared human values. **Audience:** B2B clients and prospects. **Teller:** The seller or account lead.
**Choose when:** The sale is professional services and trust must go beyond the transaction (Biesenbach).
**Shape:** A short personal story that exposes a value you hold in common with the client, tied to what they care about (for example a sentimental-furniture story for a furniture client).
**Sources:** Biesenbach

## S166 · Brand Narrative Genres
**Purpose:** Set dramatic expectations by borrowing a film genre. **Audience:** Brand strategists and creative teams, then the market. **Teller:** Brand team.
**Choose when:** You must align brand worldview and emotional stakes to a familiar dramatic expectation (Vincent, adapting McKee).
**Shape:** Pick a genre blueprint paired with a brand archetype: Action-Thriller, American Cultural Epic, Buddy Movie, Dark Romance and Family Movie (action, national myth, pairings, love, home), Education Plot, High Adventure and Maturation Plot (growth and quest), Metropolitan Romantic Comedy and Modern Epic (city and grand scale), Musical, Punitive Plot, Sports Challenge, Western, plus Film Noir, Heist and Resurrection plots. Then build the brand story to deliver that genre's promises.
**Sources:** Vincent

## S167 · Internal Versus External Storytelling
**Purpose:** Sort stories by who they are for. **Audience:** Market (exotelling) or staff and leaders (endotelling). **Teller:** Marketing and PR, or leadership and trainers.
**Choose when:** Planning where a story will live and what job it does (Terenzzo).
**Shape:** Exotelling covers marketing, trade, PR, ads and social, to win attention and price elasticity. Endotelling covers leadership, institutional memory, training and presentations, to align culture. Assign each story to one side before you shape it.
**Sources:** Terenzzo

## S168 · Integrated Marketing Story
**Purpose:** One story core carried across all channels. **Audience:** Market. **Teller:** Brand.
**Choose when:** You run paid, owned, earned and physical touchpoints that must feel coherent (Paulsen).
**Shape:** Define the single story core first, then adapt it to each touchpoint without changing the core.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## S169 · Awareness stories
**Purpose:** Introduce yourself to strangers without pitching. **Audience:** Cold audience. **Teller:** Brand.
**Choose when:** Top of funnel (Snow; Lazauskas).
**Shape:** Build on shared values, interests and common ground; keep products out.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## S170 · Consideration stories
**Purpose:** Deepen trust in people who already know you. **Audience:** Warm audience. **Teller:** Brand.
**Choose when:** Middle of funnel (Snow; Lazauskas).
**Shape:** Tell about the people around the offering: customers, employees and innovations.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas

## S171 · Acquisition stories
**Purpose:** Drive purchase. **Audience:** Ready buyers. **Teller:** Brand.
**Choose when:** Bottom of funnel (Snow; Lazauskas).
**Shape:** Make the product, service or deal itself the subject of the story.
**Sources:** Snow; Lazauskas
