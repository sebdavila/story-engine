# Story types — persuasion (S141–S155)

## S141 · Nested mini-SCR inside a broader talk
**Purpose:** Bring general findings home to the specific client. **Audience:** A client hearing a benchmark talk. **Teller:** The consultant or presenter.
**Choose when:** A benchmark presentation needs a bridge from external findings to the client's own situation.
**Shape:** Place a compact situation-complication-resolution (F064) inside the final act of the talk, covering the client's situation, shortfall and action plan (McKinsey). Nesting craft: F088.
**Sources:** McKinsey

## S142 · Opportunity flow
**Purpose:** Win support for untapped potential. **Audience:** Management who must back execution. **Teller:** The analyst or proposer.
**Choose when:** You see unexploited market potential and want targeted action.
**Shape:** Deductive order (F082): Situation, Comment, Opportunity, Solution, Call to action (Parikh). Compare the pitch version F268.
**Sources:** Parikh

## S143 · Option-elimination flow
**Purpose:** Prove a recommendation by ruling out alternatives. **Audience:** Decision-makers. **Teller:** The analyst.
**Choose when:** Diagnosing a problem such as declining sales or an aging billing system.
**Shape:** Deductive order (F082): Problem, Options, Eliminate, Eliminate, Recommendation (Parikh). Related: F365, F225.
**Sources:** Parikh

## S144 · Evolution flow
**Purpose:** Show how shifting needs require an evolving product or way of working. **Audience:** Product and process stakeholders. **Teller:** The proposer.
**Choose when:** Customer or market needs have changed and a feature or process must follow.
**Shape:** Deductive order (F082): Situation, Impact, Solution, Implementation, Recommendation (Parikh). Compare F239.
**Sources:** Parikh

## S145 · Human struggle story inside a business talk
**Purpose:** Build empathy where facts leave an audience cold or guarded. **Audience:** Business audiences. **Teller:** The presenter, or an end user.
**Choose when:** Dry financial facts are not moving people.
**Shape:** A short first-person or character-based account of a project crisis, failure, nervous first time or a user's hardship, placed inside the presentation, with the recovery principle it illustrates (Parikh). One example: a son carrying his father's wheelchair down six flights twice a week. Empathy: F048; opening a vulnerability loop: F270.
**Sources:** Parikh

## S146 · Micro-insight narratives
**Purpose:** Open empathetic conversations on inclusion. **Audience:** Groups in diversity discussions. **Teller:** Someone speaking from their own experience.
**Choose when:** Raising exclusion or privilege without claiming to know others' experience.
**Shape:** A brief personal moment of exclusion or of noticing your privilege, told with humility rather than with an I-know stance (James). Honest disclosure: F329.
**Sources:** James

## S147 · Advocacy and cause story
**Purpose:** Make an abstract policy, health or social issue concrete so people act. **Audience:** Voters, patients, donors, policymakers. **Teller:** Advocate, clinician, campaigner.
**Choose when:** Shifting policy, challenging a harmful narrative, easing fear in healthcare, or fighting donor fatigue.
**Shape:** F169 advocacy pattern: one or two minutes of personal story, one or two figures, a direct ask (Bowles). Healthcare: patient narratives and clear framing to reduce fear and improve compliance (Paulsen). Politics: human stakes over statistics under strict ethics (Paulsen); check with F363. Third sector: hopeful protagonist, concrete villain, and an easy magical-helper action such as a petition or local action (Terenzzo). Ask design: F168.
**Sources:** Bowles; Paulsen; Terenzzo

## S148 · Fables and parables
**Purpose:** Teach a point indirectly with a tale and moral. **Audience:** Listeners who may resist direct prescription. **Teller:** Speaker, used sparingly.
**Choose when:** You want to soften a prescription or sharpen strategic differentiation (Forman), with a fresh, unfamiliar tale (Callahan).
**Shape:** Embed a brief fable, literary or historical analogy, or ministory with an oblique moral inside a larger speech (Forman). Introduce an unverified tale as probably apocryphal so the lesson survives without hurting your credibility (Callahan). Dicks warns such third-person tales lack the vulnerability of a personal story, so prefer S111 for intimacy.
**Sources:** Callahan; Forman; Dicks

## S149 · Humor and satire story
**Purpose:** Bring organizational tensions into the open for discussion. **Audience:** Staff. **Teller:** Satirist or leader.
**Choose when:** A tension cannot be said directly.
**Shape:** Wit or parody that surfaces the tension (Salmon). Leader-targeted satire: F333; humor placement: F207.
**Sources:** Salmon

## S150 · Indirect healing story
**Purpose:** Address a fear or habit without naming the listener. **Audience:** Children with fears or challenging behavior. **Teller:** Parent or storyteller.
**Choose when:** Preaching would fail, for a fear such as the dark or a recurring misbehavior.
**Shape:** Imagine a naughty, angry or cheeky imp on the child's shoulder to blow away, separating person from action, then tell a subtle story about that habit with no sermon (Miller).
**Sources:** Miller

## S151 · Elemental metaphors for teenagers
**Purpose:** Reach adolescents who resist lectures. **Audience:** Teenagers (about 14+) in emotional turmoil. **Teller:** Adult storyteller.
**Choose when:** Direct advice or nursery-style fables will be rejected.
**Shape:** Tell stories of volcanoes, hurricanes or overthrown leaders, and pair them with quizzes, riddles and outdoor quests (Miller). Metaphor types: F186.
**Sources:** Miller

## S152 · Sense-making master narrative
**Purpose:** Interpret everything through one big-picture story. **Audience:** A person, organization or movement. **Teller:** Self, leaders or ideologues.
**Choose when:** Diagnosing why people read events the way they do, or rewriting your own script.
**Shape:** Guptara: the lens that orders events and guides behavior; the personal script, your internal story of why you succeed or fail, shapes attitude and opportunity spotting, and you can rewrite it. Gottschall: an ideological master narrative expands to explain everything and treats contrary facts as proof. Diagnose with F283.
**Sources:** Guptara; Gottschall

## S153 · Group founding myths
**Purpose:** Bind a group by celebrating it. **Audience:** Members of a tribe, nation or organization. **Teller:** Leaders, institutions.
**Choose when:** Analyzing or deciding whether to use unifying origin tales. They unify but distort.
**Shape:** Storr: origin tales encoding status rules and casting outsiders as flat or disgusting silhouettes, binding tribes and mobilizing conflict. Gottschall: a noble lie, a one-sided celebratory founding myth leaving out wrongs and victims. Compare S154; ethics: F363.
**Sources:** Storr; Gottschall

## S154 · Inverted counter-myth
**Purpose:** Replace a celebratory myth with its opposite. **Audience:** Critics of the founding myth. **Teller:** Counter-historian.
**Choose when:** Recognizing a counter-history that is just as one-sided.
**Shape:** Flip the myth's heroes into caricatured villains (Gottschall's ignoble truth). It remains an edited distortion; contrast S153.
**Sources:** Gottschall

## S155 · Conspiracy story
**Purpose:** Hook listeners with thrill and ego reward, beating dry debunking. **Audience:** Listeners drawn to hidden-evil narratives. **Teller:** Narrator.
**Choose when:** Understanding why such stories outcompete facts.
**Shape:** A present-tense moral thriller in which the listener is the hero answering a call to adventure to expose hidden evil (Gottschall). Check with F363, and see F282 and F283.
**Sources:** Gottschall
