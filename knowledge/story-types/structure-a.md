# Story types — structure (S001–S020)

## S001 · Central dramatic question variants
**Purpose:** Pick the question that keeps the audience waiting: what will they most want answered? **Audience:** anyone following a plot-driven piece. **Teller:** author or presenter choosing the story's engine.
**Choose when:** you need one clear driver. External: the question is about getting a concrete thing, person or outcome (a verdict, a diagnosis). Internal: the question is about inner peace or transformation. Most plots pair an external and an internal question. Shifting: the question pivots mid-story, for disorienting or fractured-identity tales. Retrospective: the real question stays hidden behind surface ones and is recognized only in hindsight.
**Shape:** build the story on F007 (dramatic question architecture). Default to external plus internal; use shifting or retrospective only when disorientation or a late recognition is the point.
**Sources:** Rubin

## S002 · Ending types
**Purpose:** Decide how the hero's fortune lands before drafting toward it. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** you must match ending to message. Happy: the hero wins the desired object (Storr: the flaw heals and control returns). Sad or tragic: the hero fails (Storr: the flaw deepens into humiliation, exile or death). Mixed or bittersweet: a win at a steep price, or a failure that brings insight (Storr: control gained in one realm, sacrificed in another). Subverted: justice the audience expects is denied to make a political point.
**Shape:** pair with F042 (ending architectures). Rubin sorts by outcome of the desire; Storr sorts by what happens to the flaw; set the final beat to show which.
**Sources:** Rubin; Storr

## S003 · Comedy (fortune turns up)
**Purpose:** Send the protagonist from worse to better, ending in victory or reconciliation. **Audience:** general; fits happy messaging. **Teller:** brand, author, speaker.
**Choose when:** the message is hope, resolution or reunion. Aristotle: the cast is lesser and laughable through a shortcoming, without grave pain, and enemies reconcile with no one dying.
**Shape:** per Barker and Brown, a darkest moment (all is lost) hits just before the climax, then victory. The Act 3 climax is where fortune flips up (Quesenberry). See F045 for the fortune line.
**Sources:** Quesenberry; Aristotle; Barker; Brown

## S004 · Tragedy (fortune turns down)
**Purpose:** Turn the protagonist's fortune for the worse, often because a false belief is kept. **Audience:** audiences ready for a warning. **Teller:** author; brands only sparingly.
**Choose when:** the message is cautionary or a cause appeal (Quesenberry). Aristotle wants an elevated cast, one plot line, and pity and fear leading to catharsis.
**Shape:** instead of a darkest moment, Barker and Brown put a brief false victory before the fall; the protagonist refuses to shed the misbelief. Act 3 climax turns fortune down.
**Sources:** Quesenberry; Aristotle; Barker; Brown

## S005 · Tragicomedy
**Purpose:** Give a mixed outcome that wins some things and loses others. **Audience:** audiences who distrust clean endings. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** neither a pure win nor a pure fall is true to the material.
**Shape:** a hybrid; it may skip both the all-is-lost beat of S003 and the false victory of S004 (Barker; Brown). Compare S002 mixed endings.
**Sources:** Barker; Brown

## S006 · Courage versus refusal arc
**Purpose:** Stage one threshold moment with two possible outcomes. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** narrator of a personal or character story.
**Choose when:** the story hinges on a single act of nerve. Courage: the protagonist faces the antagonist or threshold despite fear and becomes their best self (comedy or drama). Refusal: they lack the nerve, flee, or will not change, and lose everything (tragedy).
**Shape:** both versions meet at the threshold of F005 (hero's journey); the branch is whether the hero crosses it. Pick the branch to match S003 or S004.
**Sources:** Buster

## S007 · Dilemma types
**Purpose:** Build the hardest choice the hero faces. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** the climax needs a decision with real cost. Two goods: only one can be kept. High price: the desire is reachable at severe moral or personal cost. Lesser of two evils: choose which pain to take.
**Shape:** place the dilemma at the climactic choice; the type decides what the hero gives up. Link to F007.
**Sources:** Rubin

## S008 · Antagonist types
**Purpose:** Decide what opposes the hero. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** the opposition is unclear. Options: a human (well-meaning or villainous), a non-human or supernatural being, a natural force, an inner alter ego, or aggregate situational forces.
**Shape:** name the opposing force in one line, then make every obstacle in the middle come from it. For layered opposition see F012.
**Sources:** Rubin

## S009 · Idea-driven versus feeling-driven stories
**Purpose:** Choose between testing a thesis and evoking a mood. **Audience:** thesis stories suit audiences to be persuaded; feeling stories suit those to be moved. **Teller:** author or presenter.
**Choose when:** idea-driven if you need to argue a claim, using tight cause and effect to test it. Feeling-driven if emotion matters most: keep a loose dramatic question and loosen strict logic for a dreamlike effect.
**Shape:** idea-driven: link beats with F004 (therefore/but). Feeling-driven: keep a thin F007 thread and let images and associations carry the middle.
**Sources:** Rubin

## S010 · Commercial stories
**Purpose:** Reach a wide audience with quick, readable cause and effect and a plain, definite answer to the dramatic question. **Audience:** broad. **Teller:** brand, studio, mainstream author.
**Choose when:** accessibility and emotional payoff matter more than ambiguity.
**Shape:** tight chain of F004 links; resolve the F007 question explicitly. Contrast S011.
**Sources:** Rubin; Storr

## S011 · Literary stories
**Purpose:** Let readers decode and project their own meaning through slow, ambiguous causality and subtle or open change. **Audience:** patient, expert readers. **Teller:** literary author.
**Choose when:** nuance outranks clarity.
**Shape:** loosen causal links, leave change implied, and leave the final answer open. Contrast S010.
**Sources:** Rubin; Storr

## S012 · Seven basic plots
**Purpose:** Borrow a ready-made arc to outline a blog, ad, talk, design or data story. **Audience:** customers, clients, stakeholders. **Teller:** brand, seller, designer, analyst.
**Choose when:** you have a topic and need an arc. Butler's five for brands: overcoming the problem (pain, root cause, relief via your solution), underdog (disadvantaged contender beats a giant), quest (worthy goal pursued), life's journey (vignettes without a destination), solving the mystery (answer withheld, revealed stepwise). Sandler maps Booker's plots to design: monster (an existential threat), rags to riches (growth after decline), quest (field research), voyage and return (bringing back innovation), comedy (delight), rebirth (renewal). Feigenbaum uses all seven, including tragedy, for organizational and data narratives. Livesay uses single genres in sales: rags to riches to inspire, quest to show persistence without bragging, rebirth for reinvention, leave home and have an adventure to make customers the heroes.
**Shape:** pick one plot, then outline with F002 or F021. Failure conditions (Butler): problem fails if prospects feel no pain; underdog rings false for dominant firms; quest flops if the goal lacks value; life's journey needs a brand hook; mystery needs real insight, not clickbait. Example: an outdoor-gear maker ranked third picks underdog and states the rank openly.
**Sources:** Butler; Sandler; Feigenbaum; Livesay

## S013 · Plot and transformation template catalogues
**Purpose:** Check raw material against recurring plots and arcs. **Audience:** the teller. **Teller:** personal storyteller or author mid-draft.
**Choose when:** a draft exists and needs a waymarker, not a starting recipe. Maguire lists 14 negative-to-positive transformation arcs and 19 traditional plot features. Storr summarises the 17-part monomyth, Booker's seven plots, and the six emotional arcs.
**Shape:** draft first, then hold the draft against a catalogue entry and adjust beats; use F005 and F045 for the details instead of repeating them here.
**Sources:** Maguire; Storr

## S014 · Hero's journey
**Purpose:** Follow an ordinary person through conflict to transformation. **Audience:** customers, employees, the public. **Teller:** brand, advocate, data storyteller.
**Choose when:** you have a testimonial, employee advocacy case, or statistics that need a human face. Rodriguez: testimonials and advocacy. Feigenbaum: three acts (departure, initiation, return) weaving population statistics into one person's recovery or behavior change.
**Shape:** map the person's change onto F005; put the number in the initiation act and the new state in the return.
**Sources:** Rodriguez; Feigenbaum

## S015 · Fortune-curve story shapes
**Purpose:** Plot fortune over time to pick an emotional rhythm. **Audience:** stakeholders, customers. **Teller:** founder, designer, analyst.
**Choose when:** outlining a founder or brand story, aligning a deck, or diagnosing flat pacing. Man in the hole: normal, crisis, climb out. Boy meets girl: gain, sudden loss, rebound above the start. Cinderella: misery, helped upward, sharp midnight setback, lasting triumph. Bad to worse: steady decline, rarely suited to business (Lazer rejects it). Lupton models product and service journeys and breakdowns on these; Feigenbaum cites six data-derived arcs to choreograph highs and lows.
**Shape:** draw the curve first, then follow F045 to place beats on it.
**Sources:** Lupton; Lazer; Feigenbaum

## S016 · The mountain
**Purpose:** Raise tension peak after peak to a climax, then descend (not always happily). **Audience:** those you must hold engaged. **Teller:** brand storyteller.
**Choose when:** engagement must stay high through a long piece.
**Shape:** stack rising crises as in F043; the climax is the highest peak, followed by a short descent.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## S017 · Nested loops
**Purpose:** Wrap a technical core in human mini-stories to widen its audience. **Audience:** non-specialists plus specialists. **Teller:** brand, content team.
**Choose when:** a white paper or case study alone is too dry.
**Shape:** outer story about a person, inner technical content, outer story closes the loop; see F088 for a story inside a story.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## S018 · In medias res
**Purpose:** Hook at once by starting in the climax, then loop back for context. **Audience:** fast-scrolling viewers. **Teller:** social or video producer.
**Choose when:** the first seconds decide whether anyone stays.
**Shape:** open on the peak moment, cut back to the beginning, return to it with new meaning. See F049.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## S019 · Many stories converging on one
**Purpose:** Lead several standalone stories into one shared message. **Audience:** internal teams or market. **Teller:** leader, brand team.
**Choose when:** converging ideas: departments each tell their view and all land on one takeaway for cross-functional alignment. Petals: diverse proof points all support one brand narrative.
**Shape:** write each strand as its own short story, then end each on the same central line; see F029.
**Sources:** Rodriguez

## S020 · False start
**Purpose:** Break assumptions by opening predictably, then restarting from a new angle. **Audience:** jaded audiences. **Teller:** brand storyteller.
**Choose when:** the audience thinks it already knows your message.
**Shape:** run a familiar opening for a few lines, interrupt it abruptly, restart with the fresh angle.
**Sources:** Rodriguez
