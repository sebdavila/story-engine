# Story types — structure (S021–S040)

## S021 · Epic
**Purpose:** Narrate noble characters at length rather than act them out. **Audience:** listeners or readers. **Teller:** narrator.
**Choose when:** the story spans a long time or several events happening at once. Aristotle: the epic is narrated, not acted, is not bound to a time limit, and can show simultaneous events.
**Shape:** narrate rather than act out; the narrator moves freely across time and simultaneous events. See F002.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## S022 · Simple versus complex plots
**Purpose:** Choose how fortune changes. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** deciding the engine of the turn. Simple: fortune shifts through one continuous action, with no reversal or recognition. Complex: fortune shifts through reversal, recognition, or both.
**Shape:** for complex plots, engineer the turn with F003; for simple plots keep a single unbroken action.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## S023 · Kinds of tragedy
**Purpose:** Pick a tragic subtype. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** writing a tragedy. Complex: driven by reversal and recognition. Suffering: built on painful, destructive action (Iliad, Ajax). Character-based: built on moral character (Odyssey, Peleus). Simple (Prometheus).
**Shape:** choose the subtype, then build the middle around its driver; see F003 for the complex kind. Compare S004.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## S024 · Double plot with poetic justice
**Purpose:** Reward the good and punish the wicked in two threads. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** the piece suits comedy; Aristotle ranks it second-best for tragedy and a better fit for comedy.
**Shape:** run two threads, one for each side, and resolve each with its deserved outcome. See F055 for joining threads.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## S025 · Tragic arc options
**Purpose:** Decide who falls and why. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** casting a tragedy. A good person falling is shocking and distasteful. A wicked person rising is untragic and offensive. A wicked person falling lacks pity and fear. Best: a decent, in-between person who falls through an error (hamartia).
**Shape:** make the protagonist decent but flawed, and let one mistake cause the fall. Compare S004.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## S026 · Kinds of recognition
**Purpose:** Pick how a character discovers a truth. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** you need a discovery scene. Ranked low to high: signs or tokens (least artistic), the writer's invention (inartistic), memory, reasoning or inference (second best), a composite of the audience's false reasoning, and natural plot events (best).
**Shape:** let the discovery grow out of the plot itself; avoid a token or an authorial announcement. Build with F003.
**Sources:** Aristotle

## S027 · Coming-of-Age Story
**Purpose:** Hold loose episodes together by growth rather than a single outer quest. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** the through-line is maturation, self-knowledge or independence.
**Shape:** unify the episodes by the goal of maturation, self-knowledge or independence rather than one outer quest. See F021 for the change arc.
**Sources:** Barker; Brown

## S028 · Parallel Timelines
**Purpose:** Run two or more periods side by side. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** each period has its own tale. The past strand is a complete story with its own inciting incident, arc and climax, not backstory or flashback.
**Shape:** outline each strand with its own beats and interleave them; give each a climax.
**Sources:** Barker; Brown

## S029 · Game and emergent narrative
**Purpose:** Let the story arise from a player's choices inside rules. **Audience:** users or players. **Teller:** designer or brand.
**Choose when:** users should solve problems in first person. Rodriguez: brand gaming, internal customer-centric mini-hacks, loyalty rewards, gamified advocacy. Paulsen: character change spread over action, adventure, strategy and process-oriented formats.
**Shape:** define the rules, the choices and the character change they produce; see F030 for audience agency.
**Sources:** Paulsen; Rodriguez

## S030 · Place-Based Immersive Experience
**Purpose:** Let the audience move through a staged place and find the story in sequence. **Audience:** visitors. **Teller:** designer or host.
**Choose when:** the audience moves through a physical place. Guided tours: a host or audio guide turns movement into discovery. Themed entertainment: guests are wrapped in a multi-sensory world from entrance to exit.
**Shape:** let the movement through space be the sequence of discovery, from entrance to exit; see F031 for design dimensions.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## S031 · Wonder Versus Cautionary Speculation
**Purpose:** Explore a possible future with awe or with a warning. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** speculative author or designer.
**Choose when:** Verne-style wonder explores for awe; Wells-style tales warn about a future.
**Shape:** choose wonder or warning; see F108 for futures.
**Sources:** Paulsen

## S032 · Thesis-Based Story
**Purpose:** Begin a story from a bold claim about yourself and prove it. **Audience:** live listeners. **Teller:** personal storyteller.
**Choose when:** you do not know how to start, or have a quirk, flaw or obsession. 
**Shape:** 1. State the claim. 2. Give one or two quick illustrations. 3. Tell one detailed event with beginning, middle, end and climax that proves it. Never a list of many short examples. Example: a baker declares she cannot resist a sale, mentions two small buys, then tells the full day she drove six hours for discounted ovens.
**Sources:** Leitman

## S033 · Layered Small-Big Story
**Purpose:** Carry heavy emotion through a light story. **Audience:** live listeners. **Teller:** personal storyteller.
**Choose when:** a big life turn would turn into therapy if told directly.
**Shape:** tell a light, entertaining story about a minor event; at the end let it reveal the major change, without preaching. Example: a funny tale about a church-bake sale ends with the narrator realizing he will leave town.
**Sources:** Leitman

## S034 · Full-Circle Story
**Purpose:** Show change by ending where you began. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** personal storyteller.
**Choose when:** the setting, relationship or mindset can recur.
**Shape:** open in a place or state; end in the same one, showing how you differ. The contrast between the two matching points carries the change.
**Sources:** Leitman

## S035 · Witnessed Story
**Purpose:** Make another person's event your story. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** a witness.
**Choose when:** you saw an extreme event or fall from grace happen to someone else.
**Shape:** thread your thoughts, reactions and stakes through the event from start to end.
**Sources:** Leitman

## S036 · Reversal Story
**Purpose:** Provoke an audible reaction with a fresh twist or status flip. **Audience:** live listeners. **Teller:** personal storyteller.
**Choose when:** you have a non-cliché turn that changes the room's breath (a gasp, shout, or laughter turning to applause).
**Shape:** build expectation, then flip it; see F063 and F086.
**Sources:** Leitman

## S037 · Screen story formats
**Purpose:** Fit the arc to the format length. **Audience:** viewers. **Teller:** director or screenwriter.
**Choose when:** feature: a linear journey: the protagonist steps into a strange situation with a goal, meets mounting complications, learns something and ends changed. Short: one act over a day or two, one crisis, one insight. Series: ensemble strands over tight cycles. Non-linear: fractured, reversed or embedded time, favoring a subjective experience over causal plotting.
**Shape:** use F004 for the feature's escalating buts; for non-linear films, prioritise the character's disoriented experience over causal plotting.
**Sources:** Simson

## S038 · External genres by core stake
**Purpose:** Pick an outer genre by the stake in play. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** choosing a genre. Action: life/death. Adventure: near-death/accomplishment. Crime: unsolved/solved. Horror: damnation/salvation. Thriller: horrible death/safety. Romance: alienation/commitment. Buddy: break-up/love. Performance: rock bottom/achievement. Society: tyranny/benevolent structures.
**Shape:** pick the genre by its stake pair. Compare S044 to S055.
**Sources:** Emunds

## S039 · Internal genres by faculty of consciousness
**Purpose:** Pick an inner plot by what part of the self changes. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** self-image: selfishness/altruism, identity, redemption, self-realization. Subconscious: education, skill, understanding, revelation, amnesia. Emotions: emotional intelligence or strength, addiction, attitude, trauma/peace. Intelligence: maturation, psychopathy/empathy. Worldview: objectivity, naivete/experience, disillusionment, sin/grace, enlightenment. Also punishment, testing, self-respect, self-doubt, affection, sentimental, degeneration.
**Shape:** pick the faculty and one plot within it. See F091 for layered change.
**Sources:** Emunds

## S040 · Narrative genres
**Purpose:** Choose among drama, literary fiction, comedy or satire, and tragedy. **Audience:** any. **Teller:** author.
**Choose when:** drama aims at emotional or character truth; literary fiction adds artistic prose; comedy or satire adds a humorous voice or frame; tragedy is drama with an unhappy ending.
**Shape:** compare S038 and S039.
**Sources:** Emunds
